## SLIDE 2: INTRODUCTION & BACKGROUND (1/3)

### Substance Dependence: Indian Context

- India faces a significant substance use crisis; MAGNITUDE study (2019) estimated ~3.1 crore individuals affected by substance use disorders
- Opioid dependence constitutes a major public health burden, particularly in Punjab, Rajasthan, Northeast India, and metropolitan areas
- WHO estimates India accounts for ~25% of global opioid-related deaths in South-East Asia
- Economic burden includes lost productivity, healthcare costs, and family dysfunction
- National Drug Dependence Treatment Centre (NDDTC) reports increasing treatment-seeking among opioid users
- Indian de-addiction centers primarily offer pharmacotherapy (OST, naltrexone) with limited structured psychotherapy
- Psychosocial interventions remain under-utilized despite evidence of superior combined treatment outcomes

## SLIDE 3: INTRODUCTION & BACKGROUND (2/3)

### The Relapse Problem in De-Addiction

- Relapse rates in substance dependence range from 40–60% within the first year post-treatment (NIDA, 2020)
- Indian studies report even higher relapse rates (~70–80%) in opioid dependence (Mattoo et al., 2009)
- Triggers for relapse: craving, negative affect, interpersonal conflict, environmental cues
- Traditional relapse prevention (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985) has moderate efficacy but limited mindfulness integration
- Automatic cognitive-behavioral patterns (e.g., "apparently irrelevant decisions") perpetuate relapse cycles
- Need for interventions addressing both cognitive automaticity and emotional dysregulation
- Brief interventions are essential for Indian rehab settings with limited resources and high patient turnover

## SLIDE 4: INTRODUCTION & BACKGROUND (3/3)

### Mindfulness in Addiction Treatment

- Mindfulness: intentional, non-judgmental, present-moment awareness (Kabat-Zinn, 1990)
- Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP) developed by Bowen, Chawla, & Marlatt (2011)
- Integrates Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) + Cognitive-Behavioral Relapse Prevention (RP)
- Core mechanisms: decentering from craving, disrupting automatic reactivity, increasing distress tolerance
- MBRP targets the "craving → use" automaticity by cultivating awareness of triggers
- Brief adaptations (6–8 sessions) show promise for resource-limited settings
- Growing global evidence supports MBRP; however, Indian validation remains scarce
- Rationale: A brief MBRP model can be feasibly implemented in Indian de-addiction centers

## SLIDE 5: VARIABLE 1 – CRAVING

### Definition
- **Clinical:** An intense, subjective urge or desire to use a substance, often triggered by internal/external cues
- **Theoretical:** A motivational state arising from incentive-sensitization (Robinson & Berridge, 1993) and conditioned reinforcement

### Relevance in Substance Dependence
- Primary predictor of relapse in opioid dependence
- Mediates the relationship between cue exposure and substance use behavior
- Intensity correlates with severity of dependence and treatment dropout

### Neuropsychological/Behavioral Basis
- Mesolimbic dopamine pathway activation (VTA → Nucleus Accumbens)
- Prefrontal cortex hypoactivation during craving episodes
- Conditioned associations between environmental cues and reward anticipation
- Dysregulated stress-reward interaction in chronic opioid users

### MBRP Techniques Targeting Craving
- **Urge Surfing:** Observing craving as a transient wave without acting on it
- **Mindfulness Exposure:** Non-reactive awareness of craving sensations
- **SOBER Breathing Space:** Pause-observe-redirect during craving triggers
- **Cognitive Decentering:** "I am having a craving" vs. "I need the drug"

---


## SLIDE 6: VARIABLE 2 – IMPULSIVITY

### Definition
- **Clinical:** A tendency toward rapid, unplanned actions without adequate consideration of consequences (Moeller et al., 2001)
- **Theoretical:** Multi-dimensional construct comprising motor impulsivity, attentional impulsivity, and non-planning impulsivity (Patton et al., 1995)

### Relevance in Substance Dependence
- Higher trait impulsivity predicts initiation, escalation, and relapse in substance use
- Opioid-dependent patients show elevated impulsivity across all BIS-11 domains
- Impulsivity mediates the relationship between craving and actual use behavior
- Associated with treatment non-adherence and premature dropout

### Neuropsychological/Behavioral Basis
- Prefrontal cortex (PFC) dysfunction → impaired executive control
- Reduced inhibitory control (Go/No-Go, Stop-Signal paradigms)
- Impaired delay discounting → preference for immediate reward
- Dorsolateral PFC hypoactivation linked to poor decision-making in substance users

### MBRP Techniques Targeting Impulsivity
- **Response Inhibition Training:** Mindful pause before automatic behavioral responses
- **Awareness Training:** Noticing impulse-action sequences without engagement
- **Mindful Decision-Making:** Creating space between stimulus and response
- **Body Scan Awareness:** Recognizing somatic precursors to impulsive action
- **"STOP" Technique:** Stop-Take a breath-Observe-Proceed mindfully

---

## SLIDE 7: VARIABLE 3 – MINDFULNESS

### Definition
- **Clinical:** The capacity to attend to present-moment experience with openness, curiosity, and non-judgment
- **Theoretical:** A multi-faceted construct comprising: Observing, Describing, Acting with Awareness, Non-Judging, Non-Reactivity (Baer et al., 2006)

### Relevance in Substance Dependence
- Substance-dependent individuals show significantly lower dispositional mindfulness
- Low mindfulness associated with higher craving, impulsivity, and emotional dysregulation
- Mindfulness acts as a protective factor against relapse triggers
- Improvements in mindfulness mediate treatment outcomes in MBRP studies

### Neuropsychological/Behavioral Basis
- Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC) activation → enhanced self-regulation
- Insula activation → improved interoceptive awareness
- PFC-amygdala connectivity → better emotional regulation
- Neuroplasticity: meditation practice restructures attention and reward networks
- Default Mode Network (DMN) regulation → reduced rumination

### MBRP Techniques for Enhancing Mindfulness
- **Sitting Meditation:** Focused attention on breath, body sensations, thoughts
- **Body Scan:** Systematic non-judgmental awareness of bodily states
- **Mindful Movement:** Gentle yoga/walking with present-moment focus
- **Non-Judgmental Awareness Practice:** Labeling experiences without evaluation
- **Loving-Kindness Meditation:** Compassion cultivation reducing self-criticism

## SLIDE 8: REVIEW OF LITERATURE (1/7) – MBRP Foundational Studies

- Bowen, S., Chawla, N., & Marlatt, G. A. (2011) developed the original MBRP protocol as an 8-session aftercare program integrating mindfulness practices with cognitive-behavioral relapse prevention strategies
- Bowen, S., Witkiewitz, K., Clifasefi, S. L., et al. (2014) conducted an RCT (N=286) comparing MBRP vs. RP vs. TAU; MBRP showed significantly fewer days of substance use and heavy drinking at 12-month follow-up compared to RP and TAU
- Witkiewitz, K., & Bowen, S. (2010) demonstrated that MBRP reduced the relationship between depressive symptoms and craving, suggesting mindfulness mediates affect-craving pathways
- Bowen, S., & Marlatt, G. A. (2009) found that brief mindfulness meditation reduced craving among incarcerated substance users compared to controls
- Zgierska, A., Rabago, D., Chawla, N., et al. (2009) provided a systematic review showing mindfulness meditation as a promising adjunct in substance use disorder treatment

---

## SLIDE 9: REVIEW OF LITERATURE (2/7) – Craving Studies

- Witkiewitz, K., Bowen, S., Douglas, H., & Hsu, S. H. (2013) found MBRP participants showed significant reductions in craving intensity compared to TAU over 4-month follow-up
- Garland, E. L., Froeliger, B., & Howard, M. O. (2014) demonstrated that Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (MORE) reduced opioid craving through enhanced positive reappraisal and savoring mechanisms
- Garland, E. L., Manusov, E. G., Froeliger, B., et al. (2014) reported that mindfulness training was associated with reduced craving and decreased neural reactivity to drug cues in chronic pain patients on opioid therapy
- Brewer, J. A., Mallik, S., Babuscio, T. A., et al. (2011) showed mindfulness training (vs. CBT) resulted in greater reductions in craving in cigarette smokers
- Hsu, S. H., Collins, S. E., & Marlatt, G. A. (2013) found that urge surfing effectively reduced craving intensity and frequency in alcohol-dependent outpatients

---

## SLIDE 10: REVIEW OF LITERATURE (3/7) – Impulsivity Studies

- Garland, E. L., Roberts-Lewis, A., Tronnier, C. D., et al. (2016) showed mindfulness-based intervention reduced impulsivity scores (BIS-11) significantly in substance-dependent adults
- Murphy, C., & MacKillop, J. (2012) found that trait mindfulness was inversely associated with impulsive decision-making (delay discounting) in alcohol users
- Korponay, C., Dentico, D., Kral, T., et al. (2019) demonstrated that long-term meditators exhibited lower impulsivity and greater prefrontal cortical thickness compared to matched controls
- Alfonso, J. P., Caracuel, A., Delgado-Pastor, L. C., & Verdejo-García, A. (2011) showed combined mindfulness-inhibitory control training reduced impulsive responding in substance users
- Peters, J. R., Erisman, S. M., Upton, B. T., et al. (2011) found brief mindfulness induction reduced impulsive responses on behavioral tasks among high-impulsivity individuals

---


## SLIDE 11: REVIEW OF LITERATURE (4/7) – Mindfulness & Substance Use

- Karyadi, K. A., VanderVeen, J. D., & Cyders, M. A. (2014) conducted a meta-analysis showing significant negative association between mindfulness and substance use (r = −0.21), craving, and related problems
- Li, W., Howard, M. O., Garland, E. L., et al. (2017) meta-analysis of 42 RCTs demonstrated mindfulness-based interventions significantly reduced substance misuse (d = 0.33), craving (d = 0.68), and stress (d = 0.44)
- Chiesa, A., & Serretti, A. (2014) reviewed 24 studies and concluded mindfulness-based interventions show moderate efficacy for reducing substance use and craving across various substances
- Bowen, S., Witkiewitz, K., Dillworth, T. M., & Marlatt, G. A. (2007) found MBRP increased acceptance and acting with awareness (FFMQ facets) among post-treatment substance users
- Grant, S., Colaiaco, B., Motala, A., et al. (2017) Cochrane-level review found moderate-quality evidence supporting mindfulness-based interventions for substance use disorders

---

## SLIDE 12: REVIEW OF LITERATURE (5/7) – Brief Interventions

- Glasner-Edwards, S., Mooney, L. J., Ang, A., et al. (2017) demonstrated that abbreviated (6-session) mindfulness-based intervention was feasible and effective in reducing stimulant use in outpatient settings
- Davis, J. M., Goldberg, S. B., Anderson, M. C., et al. (2014) showed a brief (4-week) mindfulness training reduced cigarette consumption and craving among smokers
- Shorey, R. C., Elmquist, J., Gawrysiak, M. J., et al. (2017) found brief (single-session + booster) mindfulness intervention reduced substance craving in residential treatment patients
- Roos, C. R., Bowen, S., & Witkiewitz, K. (2017) demonstrated that even brief mindfulness practice (daily 15-min meditation) mediated MBRP's effects on craving and substance use
- Rationale for brief models: Indian rehabilitation settings typically have 4–8 week admission duration, necessitating condensed evidence-based protocols

## SLIDE 15: RESEARCH GAP

### Identified Gaps in Existing Literature

- **Lack of Indian MBRP studies:** No published RCT has tested MBRP (standard or brief) specifically in Indian opioid-dependent populations
- **Limited combined variable assessment:** Most studies examine craving OR mindfulness in isolation; few simultaneously assess craving, impulsivity, AND mindfulness as treatment outcomes
- **Absence of brief protocol validation:** Indian rehab settings require condensed interventions (6–8 sessions) but no brief MBRP model has been empirically validated in this context
- **Overreliance on pharmacotherapy:** Indian treatment landscape lacks integration of structured, evidence-based psychological interventions alongside OST/pharmacotherapy
- **Population specificity:** Global MBRP studies predominantly focus on alcohol/polysubstance users; opioid-specific MBRP evidence is limited
- **Cultural adaptation need:** Mindfulness practices require culturally congruent adaptation for Indian populations (familiar meditation traditions, Hindi/regional language delivery)

### Present Study Addresses:
- First brief MBRP trial in Indian opioid-dependent sample
- Simultaneous assessment of craving, impulsivity, and mindfulness
- Feasibility within Indian de-addiction center infrastructure

## SLIDE 19: RESEARCH DESIGN

### Pre-test Post-test Control Group Experimental Design

```
R    O₁    X₁    O₂    → Experimental Group (Brief MBRP + TAU)
R    O₁    X₂    O₂    → Control Group (Psychoeducation + TAU)
```

**Where:**
- R = Random assignment to groups
- O₁ = Pre-test assessment (FFMQ, BIS-11, HCQ, ASSIST)
- X₁ = Brief MBRP intervention (6–8 sessions) + Treatment As Usual
- X₂ = Psychoeducation (6–8 sessions) + Treatment As Usual
- O₂ = Post-test assessment (FFMQ, BIS-11, HCQ)

**Design Features:**
- True experimental design with randomization
- Active control group (Psychoeducation) to account for attention/contact effects
- TAU maintained in both groups to ensure ethical treatment continuity

## SLIDE 21: SAMPLE SIZE ESTIMATION

### Power Analysis & Sample Size Calculation

**Formula Used:** Cohen's Power Analysis for Independent Samples

```
n = [(Zα/2 + Zβ)² × 2σ²] / d²
```

**Parameters:**
- Effect size (d) = 0.50 (medium; based on Li et al., 2017 meta-analysis: d = 0.33–0.68)
- Power (1 − β) = 0.80 → Zβ = 0.84
- Significance level (α) = 0.05 (two-tailed) → Zα/2 = 1.96
- Assumed σ = 1 (standardized)

**Calculation:**
```
n = [(1.96 + 0.84)² × 2 × (1)²] / (0.50)²
n = [(2.80)² × 2] / 0.25
n = [7.84 × 2] / 0.25
n = 15.68 / 0.25
n = 62.72 ≈ 63 total (≈ 32 per group)
```

**Alternative (G*Power):**
- Using G*Power 3.1 for independent t-test (two-tailed): d=0.50, α=0.05, power=0.80 → n=64 per group (for t-test) OR n=34 per group (for ANCOVA with 1 covariate)

**Final Decision:**
- **N = 60 (30 per group)** — justified by ANCOVA as primary analysis (reduces required n) and consistent with similar intervention studies (Bowen et al., 2009; Glasner-Edwards et al., 2017)
- Recruit 70 total (35/group) to account for attrition (~15%)

## SLIDE 25: TOOLS – MINDFULNESS & IMPULSIVITY

### 1. Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ)
- **Authors:** Baer, R. A., Smith, G. T., Hopkins, J., Krietemeyer, J., & Toney, L. (2006)
- **Description:** 39-item self-report measure assessing 5 facets of mindfulness: Observing, Describing, Acting with Awareness, Non-Judging, Non-Reactivity
- **Scoring:** 5-point Likert scale (1 = never true to 5 = always true); higher scores = greater mindfulness
- **Reliability:** Internal consistency α = 0.75–0.91 across facets; test-retest reliability adequate
- **Validity:** Convergent validity with other mindfulness measures; discriminant validity established; sensitive to mindfulness intervention effects
- **Indian Usability:** Hindi adaptation available; used in Indian mindfulness studies (Sugiura et al., 2012; adaptations in clinical samples)

### 2. Barratt Impulsiveness Scale (BIS-11)
- **Authors:** Patton, J. H., Stanford, M. S., & Barratt, E. S. (1995)
- **Description:** 30-item self-report scale measuring 3 factors: Attentional Impulsivity, Motor Impulsivity, Non-Planning Impulsivity
- **Scoring:** 4-point scale (1 = rarely/never to 4 = almost always); higher scores = greater impulsivity
- **Reliability:** Internal consistency α = 0.79–0.83; test-retest r = 0.83
- **Validity:** Well-validated in substance use populations; discriminates between substance users and healthy controls
- **Indian Usability:** Used extensively in Indian psychiatric research; Hindi version validated (Mathew et al., 2014)

## SLIDE 26: TOOLS – CRAVING ASSESSMENT

### 3. Craving Scale: Comparison & Selection

| Scale | Description | Suitability for Opioids |
|-------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Heroin Craving Questionnaire (HCQ)** | 45-item (brief: 14-item) multidimensional craving measure specific to heroin/opioids | ⭐ HIGHLY SUITABLE |
| Obsessive Compulsive Drug Use Scale (OCDUS) | 12-item scale measuring obsessive thoughts and compulsive urges related to drug use | Moderate (generic) |
| Visual Analog Scale (VAS) | Single-item 0–100mm line rating current craving intensity | Limited (unidimensional) |

### ⭐ RECOMMENDED: Heroin Craving Questionnaire (HCQ-Brief, 14 items)
- **Authors:** Tiffany, S. T., Fields, L., Singleton, E., Haertzen, C., & Henningfield, J. E. (2000)
- **Description:** Opioid-specific craving measure; assesses desire to use, intention to use, anticipation of positive outcome, relief from withdrawal, and lack of control
- **Scoring:** 7-point Likert scale; higher scores = greater craving
- **Reliability:** α = 0.87–0.93; subscale reliabilities 0.77–0.90
- **Validity:** Convergent validity with VAS craving, drug use behavior; sensitive to intervention effects
- **Indian Usability:** Applicable to Indian heroin/opioid users; translation feasible; used in opioid craving research internationally
- **Justification:** Substance-specific measurement is preferred over generic scales for opioid-dependent populations (EMCDDA guidelines)

## SLIDE 27: TOOLS – SEVERITY ASSESSMENT (Pre-Test Only)

### 4. Severity of Substance Use: Comparison & Selection

| Scale | Description | Suitability |
|-------|-------------|-------------|
| **ASSIST (WHO)** | 8-item screening for risk level across 10 substance categories | ⭐ BEST CHOICE |
| DAST-20 | 20-item drug abuse screening (yes/no format) | Good but less comprehensive |
| AUDIT | 10-item alcohol-specific screening | Only if alcohol subgroup |

### ⭐ RECOMMENDED: Alcohol, Smoking and Substance Involvement Screening Test (ASSIST) – WHO
- **Authors:** WHO ASSIST Working Group (2002); Humeniuk, R., Ali, R., Babor, T. F., et al.
- **Description:** 8-item clinician/self-administered screening tool assessing risk level for multiple substances (tobacco, alcohol, cannabis, opioids, stimulants, sedatives, hallucinogens, inhalants, others)
- **Scoring:** Substance-specific risk scores → Low (0–3), Moderate (4–26), High (27+) for drugs
- **Reliability:** Test-retest r = 0.58–0.90 (across substances); internal consistency α = 0.77–0.94
- **Validity:** Concurrent validity with ASI, DAST, AUDIT; sensitivity 0.80, specificity 0.71 for substance dependence
- **Indian Usability:** WHO-validated; Hindi version available; extensively used in Indian NDDTC studies; culturally appropriate; brief administration (5–10 minutes)
- **Purpose in Present Study:** Used at PRE-TEST ONLY to establish baseline severity and ensure group equivalence; NOT used as an outcome measure
- **Justification:** Multi-substance coverage captures polysubstance patterns common in Indian opioid users; WHO endorsement ensures credibility; free to use

## SLIDE 31: DATA ANALYSIS

### Statistical Analysis Plan

- **Descriptive Statistics:** Mean, SD, frequency, percentages for sociodemographic and clinical variables
- **Normality Testing:** Shapiro-Wilk test to determine distribution of outcome variables
- **Within-Group Comparison:**
  - Paired samples t-test (pre-test vs. post-test within each group)
  - For non-normal data: Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test
- **Between-Group Comparison:**
  - Independent samples t-test (post-test comparison between groups)
  - For non-normal data: Mann-Whitney U Test
- **Primary Analysis:**
  - Analysis of Covariance (ANCOVA) with post-test scores as DV, group as IV, and pre-test scores as covariate
  - Controls for baseline differences and increases statistical power
- **Effect Size:** Cohen's d for between-group differences
- **Significance Level:** α = 0.05 (two-tailed)
- **Software:** SPSS Version 26.0 / JASP (open source)
- **Intent-to-Treat (ITT):** Last observation carried forward (LOCF) for dropouts

## SLIDE 33: EXPECTED RESULTS

### Anticipated Outcomes

- **Craving (HCQ):**
  - ↓ Significant reduction in craving scores in the Experimental group (Brief MBRP + TAU) compared to Control group (Psychoeducation + TAU)
  - Mechanism: Urge surfing and mindfulness exposure reduce automatic craving reactivity

- **Impulsivity (BIS-11):**
  - ↓ Significant reduction in impulsivity scores (particularly Motor and Attentional subscales) in the Experimental group
  - Mechanism: Mindfulness practice enhances prefrontal inhibitory control and response awareness

- **Mindfulness (FFMQ):**
  - ↑ Significant increase in mindfulness scores (particularly Acting with Awareness and Non-Reactivity facets) in the Experimental group
  - Mechanism: Structured meditation practice cultivates dispositional mindfulness

- **Overall Pattern:** Brief MBRP + TAU expected to demonstrate superior outcomes across all three DVs compared to Psychoeducation + TAU

## SLIDE 34: CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS

### Implications for Practice

- Validates a **brief, structured MBRP model** feasible for Indian de-addiction settings with time-limited admissions
- Provides **evidence-based psychological intervention** to complement existing pharmacotherapy (OST, naltrexone)
- Demonstrates that **mindfulness-based approaches** are culturally compatible with Indian populations (resonance with meditative traditions)
- Addresses **multiple relapse risk factors simultaneously** (craving + impulsivity + mindfulness) through a single integrated protocol
- Supports **task-shifting potential:** Brief MBRP can be delivered by MPhil-trained Clinical Psychologists in resource-limited settings
- Informs **treatment policy** at national level (NIMHANS, NDDTC, State Mental Health Authorities) for integrating MBRP into standard de-addiction care
- Contributes to **RCI-recognized intervention repertoire** for clinical psychology training and practice in India

---

## SLIDE 35: LIMITATIONS

### Anticipated Limitations

- **Sample specificity:** Male opioid-dependent patients from single center may limit generalizability to females, other substances, or diverse settings
- **Short-term assessment:** Post-test immediately after intervention; no long-term follow-up to assess maintenance of gains
- **Self-report measures:** HCQ, BIS-11, FFMQ are self-report; susceptible to social desirability bias and limited insight
- **No biological markers:** Craving measured subjectively; no physiological/neuroimaging corroboration
- **Therapist effects:** Single therapist delivery may introduce therapist-specific confounds
- **Attention control:** Psychoeducation controls for contact but not for specific mindfulness mechanisms (active placebo limitation)
- **Attrition:** Substance-dependent population may have higher dropout rates despite planning

---

## SLIDE 36: FUTURE DIRECTIONS

### Recommendations for Future Research

- **Follow-up studies:** 3-month and 6-month follow-up assessments to evaluate sustained effects of Brief MBRP
- **Multi-site RCTs:** Replicate across multiple Indian de-addiction centers for enhanced generalizability
- **Female participants:** Include female substance-dependent patients to examine gender-specific effects
- **Neuroimaging integration:** fMRI/EEG studies to examine neural mechanisms of MBRP effects on craving circuits
- **Dose-response analysis:** Compare 6-session vs. 8-session vs. full 8-week MBRP to identify optimal dosage
- **Mediator analysis:** Examine whether mindfulness improvement mediates craving and impulsivity reduction
- **Technology-assisted delivery:** Explore app-based/digital MBRP for post-discharge maintenance and rural access
- **Cultural adaptation research:** Formal cultural adaptation and validation of MBRP for Indian linguistic and cultural contexts
- **Comparative effectiveness:** MBRP vs. other evidence-based interventions (CBT, ACT, Contingency Management) in Indian samples
