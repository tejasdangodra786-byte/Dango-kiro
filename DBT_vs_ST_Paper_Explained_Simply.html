1Research basics you need first

Read this once. After this, the rest of the paper will make sense.

1.1 What is a research paper?

A research paper is a report written by scientists after they have done a study. It tells other people what question they asked, how they tried to answer it, what they found, and what they think it means. Almost every paper in psychology has the same parts:

PartWhat it answers
AbstractThe whole paper in one paragraph.
IntroductionWhy does this question matter? What do we already know? What is missing?
MethodsExactly how was the study done? Who took part? What was measured? How was the data analysed?
ResultsWhat were the numbers? Only facts, no opinions.
DiscussionWhat do the results mean? How do they fit with earlier studies? What are the weak points?
ConclusionThe final take-home message.

1.2 What is a "trial"? What is an RCT?

A clinical trial is a study where researchers give people a treatment and then check whether they got better.

An RCT (randomized controlled trial) is the strongest kind of trial. "Randomized" means a computer decides, by chance, which treatment each person gets. It is like tossing a coin for each patient.

WHY TOSS A COIN? If the doctor chose who gets which therapy, they might give one therapy to the less sick patients without even noticing. Then that therapy would look better only because its patients were healthier to begin with. When chance decides, both groups end up similar in age, illness, background and everything else, on average. So if one group improves more at the end, we can believe it is because of the therapy.
EXAMPLE Imagine you want to know which of two coaching centres is better. If the bright students all choose Centre A, Centre A will get better marks even if its teaching is no better. If a lottery decided which centre each student joins, the two centres would get similar students, and the marks would tell you something real about the teaching.

1.3 Groups, arms and comparison

Each treatment in a trial is called an arm or a group. In this paper there are two arms: the DBT arm and the Schema Therapy (ST) arm. Many trials also have a group that gets no treatment or "usual care". This trial did not have one. It compared two real treatments against each other. This is called a head-to-head or comparative trial.

1.4 Sample and population

The population is everyone the result is meant to apply to (for example, all adults with BPD). The sample is the smaller group who actually took part (here, 164 patients in Lübeck, Germany). We study the sample and hope it tells us about the population.

1.5 Variables and outcomes

An outcome is the thing you measure to see if treatment worked, such as BPD symptoms or depression.

  • The primary outcome is the single most important outcome, chosen before the study starts. The main conclusion of the trial depends on it.
  • Secondary outcomes are extra outcomes. They are useful, but less trustworthy, because when you test many things, some will look "significant" just by luck.

1.6 Hypothesis and the "null hypothesis"

A hypothesis is the researcher's prediction. Here: "DBT and ST will give different results."

The null hypothesis is the boring opposite: "There is no difference." Statistics tests try to see whether the data are strong enough to reject the boring explanation.

A two-sided hypothesis means "there is a difference, but we do not say which way". A one-sided hypothesis would be "DBT will be better". Two-sided is stricter and more honest when you do not know which way it will go.

1.7 Mean and SD

The mean (M) is the average. The SD (standard deviation) tells you how spread out the scores are. Age 33.7 (SD 10.6) means the average age was about 34, and most people were roughly between 23 and 44.

1.8 The p-value

The p-value answers one question: "If there were truly no difference, how often would we see a difference this big just by chance?"

  • p = 0.01 means about 1 time in 100. That is rare, so we think the difference is probably real.
  • p = 0.094 means about 9 times in 100. That is not rare enough.

Scientists usually use a cut-off of 0.05 (5 in 100). Below 0.05 is called statistically significant. Above 0.05 is not significant.

BE CAREFUL "Not significant" does not mean "the same" or "no difference". It only means "we cannot be sure the difference is real". A small study can easily miss a real difference.

1.9 The confidence interval (CI)

A study gives a "best guess" number, for example "DBT was 3.32 points better". But we know this guess is not exact. The 95% confidence interval is the range where the true value probably lies.

EXAMPLE A weather forecast says "tomorrow will be 30°C, give or take 3 degrees". The best guess is 30, and the range is 27 to 33. A confidence interval works the same way.

How to read it: if the range includes zero (no difference), the result is not significant. If the whole range is on one side of zero, it is significant. A wide range means we are unsure. A narrow range means we are fairly sure.

1.10 Effect size (Cohen's d and r)

A p-value tells you whether a difference is likely to be real. It does not tell you how big it is. The effect size tells you the size.

Cohen's d measures a difference in "standard deviation units". Rough guide:

dMeaning
around 0.2small
around 0.5medium
0.8 or morelarge
above 1.5very large (this is unusual in psychotherapy)

This paper uses d in two very different ways. Please do not mix them up:

  • Within-group d = how much one group changed from start to end. "Did DBT patients get better?"
  • Between-group d = how different the two groups are from each other. "Was DBT better than ST?"

r is another effect size, used in the statistics tables. Rough guide: 0.1 small, 0.3 medium, 0.5 large.

1.11 t and df

You will see things like t(131) = 1.69. The t is a test number: the bigger it is (ignoring the minus sign), the stronger the evidence for a difference. The df ("degrees of freedom", here 131) is related to how much data went into the test. You do not need to calculate these. Just know that the p-value comes from them.

1.12 Intention-to-treat (ITT)

Intention-to-treat means: analyse every patient in the group they were put in, even if they stopped coming. The opposite is a completer analysis, which only looks at people who finished.

WHY ITT? People who finish therapy are usually the ones it suited. If you only count them, a therapy can look better than it is. ITT gives a more honest, real-life picture.

1.13 Dropout, missing data and follow-up

Dropout (or attrition) is when a patient stops treatment early. Missing data is when a planned measurement was not collected, for example because the patient did not come to the interview. Follow-up is checking on patients some time after treatment has ended, to see if the improvement lasts.

1.14 Blinding

Blinding means someone does not know which treatment a patient got. In psychotherapy, patients and therapists always know. But the people who score the interviews (the raters) can be kept blind. That stops them, even without meaning to, from scoring one group more kindly.

1.15 Reliability and validity

  • Reliability = consistency. If two raters score the same patient, do they get the same score? It is often measured with the ICC (intraclass correlation). ICC goes from 0 to 1. Above 0.9 is excellent.
  • Internal validity = can we trust the result inside this study? (Was it done fairly?)
  • External validity or generalizability = does the result apply to other people and places, for example patients in India?

1.16 Statistical significance vs clinical significance

Statistical significance asks: "Is this difference likely to be real, or just chance?"
Clinical significance asks: "Is this difference big enough to matter in a patient's life?"
A tiny difference can be statistically significant in a huge study but useless for patients. And a meaningful difference can be "not significant" in a small study.

4Background: BPD and the two therapies

The paper assumes you already know this. These details come from standard textbooks, not from the paper itself.

4.1 What is Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)?

BPD is a long-lasting pattern where a person's emotions, relationships and sense of self are very unstable. The main problem is emotion dysregulation: feelings come very fast, become very strong, and take a long time to calm down.

The DSM lists 9 features. A person needs at least 5 for a diagnosis:

  1. Desperate efforts to avoid being abandoned
  2. Intense, unstable relationships (swinging between "you are perfect" and "I hate you")
  3. Unstable sense of who they are
  4. Impulsive, risky behaviour (spending, substances, binge eating, unsafe sex)
  5. Repeated self-harm or suicidal behaviour
  6. Quick, intense mood changes
  7. Long-lasting feeling of emptiness
  8. Intense, hard-to-control anger
  9. Under stress, brief paranoid thoughts or feeling unreal (dissociation)
EXAMPLEA friend does not reply to a message for a few hours. A person with BPD may feel sure they are being abandoned, feel huge panic and anger, send many angry messages or self-harm to calm down, feel relief for a short while, and then feel deep shame. Then the cycle repeats.

BPD is also very costly for society, mainly because patients are often admitted to hospital. The authors cite several cost studies on this.

4.2 What is DBT?

Dialectical Behavior Therapy was developed by Marsha Linehan. It says that BPD comes from a mix of a person who is emotionally very sensitive and an environment that ignores or punishes their feelings (the "biosocial theory").

DBT teaches practical skills in four areas:

  • Mindfulness: noticing what is happening right now, without judging it
  • Distress tolerance: getting through a crisis without making it worse
  • Emotion regulation: understanding and calming strong feelings
  • Interpersonal effectiveness: asking for what you need and saying no, while keeping relationships

"Dialectical" means holding two opposite things together: the therapist fully accepts the patient as they are, and at the same time pushes for change.

4.3 What is Schema Therapy (ST)?

Schema Therapy was developed by Jeffrey Young. It says that when a child's basic emotional needs (safety, love, freedom to express feelings) are not met, the child develops schemas: deep, painful beliefs such as "people always leave me" or "I am bad".

In BPD, the person switches quickly between modes, which are emotional states. Examples: the abandoned child (scared and alone), the angry child, the punishing parent (harsh self-criticism), the detached protector (shutting off feelings), and the healthy adult (the part therapy tries to build).

ST uses:

  • Limited reparenting: the therapist offers some of the warmth and care the patient missed, within professional limits
  • Imagery rescripting: the patient imagines a painful childhood memory, and the therapist helps them change what happens in it
  • Chair work: the patient speaks to different modes placed on different chairs

7Methods: how the study was done

This is the most important part for judging whether we can trust the results. Each step is explained, along with why it was done.

7.1 Study design

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSA single-centre randomized clinical trial at a tertiary outpatient centre of a university clinic in Lübeck, Germany, which mainly treats severely affected patients who cannot be treated elsewhere. It followed CONSORT guidelines and recommendations for trials of psychological treatments. The full plan was published earlier as a study protocol.
IN SIMPLE WORDS
  • Single-centre: everything happened in one clinic. This is easier to control, but the results may not apply everywhere.
  • CONSORT: an international checklist that tells researchers exactly what to report in a trial, so readers can check its quality. It includes the patient flow diagram.
  • Study protocol: the detailed plan, published in 2018 before the results. This lets readers check whether the researchers stuck to their plan.

7.2 How many patients? (Sample size and power)

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSThey planned 160 patients. A power analysis (80% power, medium effect size, α = 0.05) showed 128 were needed. They added 32 more (20%) to allow for dropouts.
IN SIMPLE WORDS

Before starting, researchers work out how many patients they need. This is called a power analysis.

  • Power 80%: if a real difference of the expected size exists, the study has an 80% chance of finding it (and a 20% chance of missing it).
  • Medium effect size: they planned to detect a medium-sized difference (d around 0.5).
  • α (alpha) = 0.05: the cut-off for "significant" (the 5-in-100 rule from Part 1).
  • 128 + 32 = 160: 128 for the maths, plus 20% extra because some people always drop out.
BE CAREFULThe study was built to find a medium difference. The difference they actually found was small (d = 0.24). To detect a small difference reliably, you need many more patients (several hundred). So the study may simply have been too small to see it. This is one of the most important points about this paper.

7.12The statistics, explained step by step

This is the hardest part of the paper. Each sentence is turned into plain language. You do not need to do the maths. You just need to understand what each step is for.

Step 1: they had to change their plan

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSThe original plan was to compare the slopes (the speed of improvement) from baseline to the 1-year follow-up. Because of unexpected differences between the groups at baseline, especially in self-rated BPD symptoms and depression, they changed the plan and controlled for baseline scores. Controlling for baseline is also recommended in general. So the main test became the difference between groups at the 1-year follow-up.
IN SIMPLE WORDS

Original plan: "Which group's line goes down faster?"

New plan: "At the final check, after making allowance for where each patient started, which group is lower?"

They changed because the DBT group started sicker. If you just compare final scores, a group that started sicker is at an unfair disadvantage. Adjusting for the starting score levels the field.

EXAMPLETwo runners finish a race at the same time, but one started 20 metres behind. Who is faster? You must allow for the starting position. Adjusting for baseline does the same for patients.
BE CAREFULChanging the analysis plan after seeing data is something readers should always notice. Here the authors explained it openly, gave a good reason, and also ran the original plan as a check (it gave the same answer). That is the right way to handle it.

Step 2: who was included in the analysis (ITT)

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSThe analysis followed intention-to-treat and used all available data. A patient was included if they were randomized and attended at least one individual session (where they learned their group). Missing values were not filled in, because GLMMs give valid estimates under the same assumptions as multiple imputation. They used SPSS version 28.
IN SIMPLE WORDS
  • Everyone who came to at least one session was counted, whether they finished or not. That is 164 people. (The 3 who never came at all were left out. Strictly, this is called a "modified" ITT.)
  • Missing values not imputed: some researchers "fill in" missing scores with estimates (called imputation). These authors did not. They used a method (GLMM) that can work with incomplete data directly.
  • SPSS: the statistics software they used.
BE CAREFULThis only works if the missing data are "missing at random", meaning the people who did not come to interviews are similar to those who did, once you know their earlier scores. If the people who stayed away were those doing badly (or doing very well), the results could be biased. Nobody can fully check this.

Step 3: the main method, GLMM with gamma regression

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSBecause the scores had skewed distributions, continuous outcomes were analysed with GLMM gamma regression. As gamma regression cannot handle zeros, 0.01 was added to all scores where zeros existed.
IN SIMPLE WORDS

What is a "distribution"? If you list everyone's score and draw a graph of how many people got each score, that shape is the distribution.

Normal distribution: a symmetrical bell shape, with most people in the middle. Many basic statistics tests assume this shape.

Skewed distribution: lopsided. For example, many people have low scores and a few have very high scores, so the graph has a long tail on one side. Symptom scores are often like this, especially after treatment, when many people are near zero.

GLMM (Generalized Linear Mixed Model):

  • Model: a mathematical description of how scores change.
  • Linear: built from simple additions of effects (time, treatment and so on).
  • Generalized: it can handle data that is not bell-shaped, such as skewed scores.
  • Mixed: it handles repeated measurements from the same person, and patients who missed some time points.

Gamma regression is the version of GLMM made for skewed scores that are always above zero. Because it cannot accept a score of exactly 0, they added a tiny 0.01 to every score. This does not change the results in any meaningful way.

WHY NOT A SIMPLE t-TEST?A simple test would need complete data, a bell-shaped distribution, and only one measurement per person. This study had six measurements per person, lots of missing data, and skewed scores. GLMM can handle all three.

Step 4: allowing for groups of patients who did therapy together

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSWhere possible, they included a random effect of the treatment cohort, because patients took part in group therapy together.
IN SIMPLE WORDSPatients in the same therapy group influence each other. A very supportive group might help all its members, and a difficult group might hold them back. So the patients are not completely independent. The "random effect of cohort" tells the model which patients were in the same group, so it does not treat them as fully separate people.

Step 5: how they modelled time (piece-wise)

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSTime was modelled to fit the data best (linear, logarithmic or segmented). If change during treatment looked different from change during follow-up, they used piece-wise regression, with the treatment phase as one section and the follow-up as another.
IN SIMPLE WORDSScores do not always go down in a straight line. During therapy, BPDSI scores dropped quickly. After therapy, they stayed flat. A piece-wise model draws two separate line pieces, one for the therapy period and one for the follow-up period, instead of forcing one straight line through everything.
EXAMPLEA person loses weight quickly during a 6-month diet programme, then stays the same weight afterwards. One straight line would describe this badly. Two pieces, "going down" then "flat", describe it well.

Step 6: allowing for medicines

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSAs planned in the protocol, psychotropic medication use was included as a running covariate, coded at each assessment point. Because this can be debated, they also ran the analysis without it.
IN SIMPLE WORDSAbout 3 in 4 patients were on psychiatric medicines, and this could change over time. A covariate is an extra factor the model takes into account. "Running" means it was recorded freshly at every time point. So the model can separate the effect of the therapy from the effect of medicines. They checked that removing this step did not change the answer.

Step 7: allowing for the baseline differences

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSBecause the DBT group had higher scores on almost all measures at baseline, they added more covariates: a centred overall severity score based on principal component analysis of all baseline outcomes, the number of comorbid disorders, and the patient's own baseline score on that outcome. The baseline time point was then removed from the time variable. So the main test of treatment was the difference after the 1-year follow-up.
IN SIMPLE WORDS
  • Principal component analysis (PCA): a method that combines many scores into one summary score. Here it made one "how ill was this person at the start, overall" score from all the starting questionnaires.
  • Centred: the score was shifted so the average person is at zero. This just makes the numbers easier to read.
  • Number of comorbidities: how many other diagnoses the person had.
  • Baseline score as covariate: the model knows each patient's starting score on that particular measure.

Because the starting score is now a covariate, it is no longer one of the time points. That is why the paper says improvement over time was tested "from 0.5 years to the 1-year follow-up".

BE CAREFULThis information was missing for about half the patients (49.4% and 54.9%). So we do not really know how much other treatment patients got after the study. Extra treatment could have helped some patients and blurred the comparison.

13Discussion: what the authors think it means

Paragraph 1: both therapies worked

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSBoth groups showed significant drops in BPD and general symptoms, and improvements in functioning and quality of life. Within-group effect sizes were large for most outcomes, supporting the effectiveness of both ST and DBT in this outpatient setting.
IN SIMPLE WORDSPatients in both therapies got much better on almost everything.
BE CAREFULStrictly, a within-group improvement does not prove the therapy caused it. People can also improve because time passes, or because they joined when they were at their worst and naturally moved back towards average (this is called regression to the mean). Proof would need an untreated comparison group, which this study did not have. But earlier trials have already shown both therapies beat usual care, so it is reasonable to believe much of the improvement came from therapy.

Paragraph 2: no difference, except anger and dropout timing

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSAgainst the hypotheses, there were no significant differences between DBT and ST on the main outcome and nearly all secondary outcomes. The anger subscale favoured DBT, which fits DBT's focus on emotion regulation, but it was just one subscale. Dropout totals did not differ, but DBT patients tended to leave earlier. Overall dropout was low compared with a recent meta-analysis.
IN SIMPLE WORDSThe authors are honest: their predictions did not come true. They do not over-sell the anger finding.

Paragraph 3: why no difference? The authors' explanations

ExplanationIn simple words
1. Shared ingredientsBoth therapies give a clear framework and explanation, an active, supportive and validating therapist, a focus on emotions, and a balance of acceptance and change. For BPD patients, these common elements may be what really helps.
2. Different routes, same destinationThe techniques are very different (skills in DBT, experiential work in ST). They may work through different "pathways of change" but end up in the same place.
3. Hidden subgroupsOn average there is no difference, but one therapy may suit some kinds of patients better (for example, different types of childhood abuse, or different other diagnoses). A meta-analysis by Kaiser and Herzog (2023) found that treatment effects in BPD vary between patients, which supports choosing therapy for each person.
4. Too small a studyWhen both therapies are good, the difference between them is likely to be small, and this sample size may be too small to find it. The study was not a non-inferiority trial, so it cannot prove ST is as good as DBT.
EXAMPLE: "DIFFERENT ROUTES, SAME DESTINATION"Two people want to get fit. One goes to the gym, the other plays football. They do very different things, but both end up fitter. Asking "which is better?" may matter less than "is the person doing something regularly and with good support?"

Paragraph 4: the authors' list of limitations

  • No waitlist control group. The authors felt it would be unethical to make such ill patients wait for 18 months, and that it might put people off joining. Earlier studies already show both therapies beat usual care.
  • Missing data from dropouts may weaken the results.
  • Deviations from standard DBT: some patients had no skills group, and push-outs were decided case by case. A component study by Linehan and colleagues (2015) shows the skills group is an important part of DBT, so DBT without the group may be weaker. The check without these patients gave the same result, though.
  • Adherence checks used a substitute for the "gold-standard" DBT rating, because funding did not cover outside experts.
  • Single site and a focus on very severe patients limits how far the results apply elsewhere (external validity).

Paragraph 5: the authors' list of strengths

  • Interview-based main outcome with excellent rater agreement
  • Many secondary outcomes covering many areas of life
  • Harms recorded, therapy quality checked, a full year of follow-up
  • Followed CONSORT
  • Very few exclusion criteria, so patients with almost any other disorder could join

14My critical appraisal: how much can we trust it?

MY NOTEThis section is my own judgement, not from the paper.

What was done well

  • A genuinely new and useful question
  • Proper randomization by an outsider, with concealment and stratification
  • Blind raters and an interview as the main measure, with near-perfect rater agreement
  • Both groups got the same amount of therapy, from trained and supervised therapists
  • Honest reporting: they explain the change of plan, run many checks, and do not over-claim
  • Real, very ill patients, not a "clean" research sample
  • Ethics approval and written consent from all patients

Weak points

  • About half the main interviews were missing at the final check. This is the biggest problem.
  • Too small to find a small difference. It was planned for a medium effect.
  • Groups were not equal at the start, despite randomization, so the analysis had to be changed.
  • Registered after the study started. Normally a trial is registered first, so nobody can quietly change the main outcome later. The authors say nothing changed, but readers cannot fully check.
  • Possible allegiance. Several authors earn money from schema therapy workshops and supervision, and some from DBT. The team includes experts in both, which helps balance, but it is still worth noting.
  • Only raters were blind. Most secondary outcomes are self-report, and patients knew their therapy.
  • One German centre with mostly German women. Results may differ elsewhere.
  • Small reporting errors: p = 0.94 in the text, d = "−24" in the abstract, and some numbers in Figure 1 that do not add up.

Overall

MY VERDICTA carefully run, honestly reported trial. It gives good reassurance that both DBT and ST can help very ill BPD patients. But it cannot tell us which is better, or whether they are equal.

15The authors' conclusion

WHAT THE PAPER SAYSPRO*BPD was the first large randomized trial to compare DBT and ST for BPD. Both groups improved a lot, showing that even severely ill patients with many other disorders can be treated successfully with either. There were no significant differences overall, except for anger and the timing of dropout. An equivalence trial is needed to test whether the two are equally effective.
IN SIMPLE WORDSBoth work. Neither has been shown to be better. To show they are equal, someone needs to do a different kind of study.

What is a non-inferiority or equivalence trial?

This study asked: "Is there a difference?" It could not find one. But "could not find a difference" is not the same as "proved they are the same".

An equivalence trial asks a different question: "Is the difference smaller than an amount we decided does not matter?" Before starting, researchers pick a margin, for example "a difference of less than 3 BPDSI points does not matter to patients". Then they recruit enough patients to show the true difference is inside that margin. A non-inferiority trial is similar, but only checks that one therapy is not worse than the other by more than the margin.

16What this means for practice, and for India

For psychologists

  • Offer people with BPD a specialist, structured therapy. DBT and ST both look like reasonable choices.
  • Do not tell patients that one is proven better. This study does not support that.
  • Choose together with the patient, based on their wishes, what training and supervision you have, and whether a group is available.
  • If anger is the main problem, DBT may have a slight edge, but this is tentative.
  • Plan for a long programme, and be extra careful about dropout early in DBT and later in ST.

For Indian settings

MY NOTEThis part is my own thinking. The paper does not discuss India.
  • Time and cost: 3 hours a week for 18 months is very hard for most Indian services and patients, given waiting lists, travel and fees. Shorter or stepped formats need testing.
  • Groups: group sessions let one therapist reach several patients, which suits busy services.
  • Training: few therapists in India are fully trained and supervised in DBT or ST.
  • Family and culture: family is very involved in care in India. Involving family and adapting examples and language may help.
  • Evidence: the sample was 92% German. We need studies in Indian patients before assuming the same results.

19Questions you may be asked (click to open)

What was the research question?

Is DBT or Schema Therapy more effective for adults with BPD, measured by BPD severity (BPDSI) one year after therapy ends?

Why was this study needed?

Both therapies work, but they had never been compared directly. Services and patients need to know which to choose.

Why randomize?

So the two groups are similar in everything except the therapy. Then any difference at the end is likely caused by the therapy.

Why stratify by sex?

Only about 1 in 5 patients were men. Randomizing men and women separately stops one group ending up with many more men by chance.

Why were raters blinded?

So they could not, even without meaning to, score one therapy's patients more kindly.

Why measure one year after therapy?

To see whether improvement lasts after weekly therapy stops.

Why use intention-to-treat?

Counting only completers makes a therapy look better than it really is. ITT is more honest.

Why did they change the analysis plan?

The DBT group started sicker on many measures. Comparing final scores after adjusting for starting scores is fairer. This is also recommended in general.

Why gamma regression?

Symptom scores were skewed (lopsided) and always positive. Gamma regression is designed for this kind of data.

What was the main result?

DBT was 3.32 BPDSI points lower at the final check (95% CI −0.58 to 7.22, p = 0.094, d = −0.24). Not significant.

Did patients get better?

Yes, a lot. Within-group effect sizes were 2.45 for DBT and 1.78 for ST, which are very large.

Was anything significant between the therapies?

Only the anger subscale (DBT better) and the timing of dropout (more DBT patients left in months 3 to 6, more ST patients in months 12 to 15).

Does this prove the two therapies are equal?

No. 'Not significant' only means 'not sure'. Proving equality needs an equivalence or non-inferiority trial with a pre-set margin.

What is the biggest weakness?

About half the main interviews were missing at the final check, and the study was too small to detect a small difference.

Why was there no untreated group?

The authors thought it would be unethical to leave such ill patients untreated for so long. The cost is that we cannot fully separate therapy effects from natural improvement.

Can we apply this in India?

Only partly. The patients were mostly German women in a specialist clinic, with 3 hours of therapy a week for 18 months. We need Indian studies and adapted formats.

20One-minute summary

DBT and Schema Therapy are both recommended for BPD, but had never been compared directly. In this German trial, 164 severely ill adults with BPD were randomly given one of the two therapies, with 3 hours of therapy a week for 18 months. The main outcome was BPD severity, measured by a blind interviewer, one year after therapy ended.

Both groups improved a lot. DBT ended about 3 points lower, but this was not significant (p = 0.094) and the effect was small. Nearly every other outcome was the same. Only anger was significantly better with DBT. About 23% dropped out in each group, though DBT patients left earlier and ST patients later. Harm was rare.

The study was carefully done, but half the final interviews were missing and it was too small to find a small difference. So: both therapies are good options, and we still do not know whether one is better or whether they are truly equal.
