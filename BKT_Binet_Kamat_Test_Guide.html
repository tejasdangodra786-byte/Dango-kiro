1 · What is the Binet-Kamat Test?

Full nameBinet-Kamat Test of Intelligence
Common abbreviationBKT
DeveloperV. V. Kamat (Indian adaptation / revision of the Binet-Simon Intelligence Scale)
TypeIndividually administered, age-scale intelligence test yielding a ratio IQ (MA/CA×100)
NatureMixed — contains both verbal and non-verbal / performance items (it is not a purely performance test)
Theoretical basisBinet's concept of intelligence as a global / general ability that increases with mental age; tests are grouped by the age at which most children pass them
PopulationIndian children & young people, age 3 to 22 years (Kannada- and Marathi-speaking standardization)
OutputBasal Age, Mental Age (MA), IQ, and a 6-domain profile
Key Concept BKT is an age-scale: items are sorted into the age level (Year III, IV, V … XVI, and adult levels) at which a majority of standardization children first pass them. A child earns "months of credit" for each item passed, these are summed into a Mental Age, and IQ is the ratio of Mental Age to Chronological Age.

Introduction

The Binet-Kamat Test (BKT) is V. V. Kamat's Indian revision of the Binet-Simon Intelligence Scale — an individually administered age-scale intelligence test for children and young people aged 3 to 22 years. It was standardized on Kannada- and Marathi-speaking children in India, which is why it remains widely used in Indian clinical and educational settings: it is simple, economical and available in Indian languages. Its items are arranged by age level from Year III upward, sampling six cognitive domains — language, memory, reasoning, conceptual thinking, visuo-motor ability and social intelligence — so it yields both a single global score and a qualitative profile of relative strengths and weaknesses.

Administration is adaptive: the examiner begins near the child's expected level, works down until a basal age is reached (the highest age level at which the child passes every item) and up until a ceiling is reached (a level where all items fail). Month-credits are added for every item passed above the basal to build the Mental Age (MA), and the IQ is then computed as MA ÷ CA × 100. This makes BKT a ratio-IQ test — which is both a defining feature and its most-quoted limitation, because the standardization sample (N ≈ 1074) produced a mean IQ near 100 but a standard deviation of 18.7, wider than the 15 used by modern tests, so BKT tends to yield more extreme scores. Behavioural observation during testing is treated as an integral part of interpretation, and the six-domain classification (adapted from Lezak) allows the examiner to look beyond the global IQ.

Historically, the test descends from Binet & Simon (France, 1905 / 1908 / 1911), was revised by Terman as the Stanford-Binet in the USA and by Cyril Burt in Britain; Kamat's revision placed each item at the age position that best matched Binet's original placement using Indian pass-rate data.

Viva Tip — one-line definition "BKT is V. V. Kamat's Indian revision of the Binet-Simon scale — an individually administered age-scale intelligence test for ages 3–22 that yields a ratio IQ (MA/CA×100) and a six-domain profile."

Advantages & Limitations

Advantages
  • Available in Indian languages (Kannada, Marathi) — culturally more appropriate than imported tests.
  • Simple, quick and economical to administer, score and interpret.
  • Wide age band (3–22) in a single instrument.
  • Yields a rich qualitative picture (basal/ceiling spread, domain profile, behaviour).
Limitations
  • Uses ratio IQ — conceptually weak in adolescence/adulthood (MA plateaus while CA rises).
  • SD = 18.7 makes scores non-comparable with SD-15 tests and inflates extreme values.
  • Old norms; limited to Kannada/Marathi standardization.
  • Heavily language-loaded; not ideal as a sole test for language-impaired or hearing-impaired clients.

3 · Ratio IQ vs Deviation IQ

You were right — BKT is a ratio-IQ test. This is one of its defining features and its biggest conceptual limitation, so it is worth being precise.

Ratio IQ (used by BKT) IQ = (Mental Age ÷ Chronological Age) × 100.
Introduced by Stern; assumes mental growth is roughly linear with age. Works reasonably for children but breaks down after ~15–16 years, because mental age stops rising while chronological age keeps increasing — so a normal adult's "ratio IQ" would artificially fall. BKT handles this by capping the age denominator (Terminal Age) for older testees.
Deviation IQ (used by modern tests) IQ is a standard score showing how far a person sits from the mean of their own age group (mean 100, SD 15). Used by Wechsler scales and modern Stanford-Binet. It does not depend on the mental-age concept and stays valid across adulthood.
Scoring Alert Because BKT's SD is 18.7 (not 15), a BKT IQ is not directly comparable to a Wechsler IQ. A BKT IQ of 70 is not the same distance below the mean as a Wechsler IQ of 70. Use the adjustment formula (Section 13) when comparison to SD-15 tests is required.

4 · Purpose & When to Use

Use BKT for:

  • General intellectual assessment of children/adolescents (3–22 yrs) in Indian settings.
  • Estimating Mental Age and IQ for educational placement and certification.
  • Screening / assessment in intellectual disability, learning problems and developmental delay.
  • Baseline cognitive assessment in child psychiatric / clinical populations.
  • Situations needing an Indian-language instrument.

BKT may NOT be sufficient alone when:

  • The client is a normal adult (ratio IQ becomes unreliable → prefer Wechsler/deviation-IQ test).
  • The client has significant language, speech or hearing impairment (test is language-loaded → add a performance test, e.g. Bhatia's Battery or Raven's).
  • A precise, defensible classification for disability certification is needed and the wide SD (18.7) would distort the cut-off.
  • Fine profile analysis (index scores) is required — use a factor-based test.
Clinical Pearl A frequent clinical pairing: Binet-Kamat (verbal + non-verbal, language-based) plus a pure performance test such as Bhatia's Battery — so the two together cover both language-loaded and language-free abilities.

5 · Structure & Age Levels

BKT is organised as an age scale. Each age level (a "Test for Age") holds a fixed set of items — typically 6 main items plus one or more alternatives. The recording sheet lays these out in columns headed by Roman numerals: IIIIV VVIVIIVIII IXXXIIXIV XVI … and adult levels up to XXII.

Key Concept — age spacing Below ~Year V the scale ideally needs finer age fractions (the manual notes that a Year-III item is one passed by ~50% of children between 2y0m and 2y2m, and so on). In practice each completed age level contributes a block of months toward Mental Age.

The item families that recur across ages

Rather than being separate "subtests", the same types of task reappear at increasing difficulty and are cross-referenced on grouped cards:

6 · Materials Required

  • Test kit / item cards — the boxed "Test for Age" cards (Age 3 … 22) and the grouped cross-reference cards (Repeating Digits, Comprehension, Syllables, Enumeration/Description/Interpretation of Pictures, Digits Reversed, Similarities/Differences).
  • Picture cards — Railway Station, Reception (at home), Motor Accident, Domestic Scene.
  • Comparison-of-lines card and aesthetic-comparison card.
  • Form / discrimination cards (Kuhlmann forms), divided (cut-up) card, missing-features pictures, colour cards (Red, Yellow, Blue, Green).
  • Small objects for naming: key, paisa/coin, closed penknife, watch, ordinary lead pencil; counting materials — paper strips / coins (up to 13), coins for "counting four paisa".
  • Construction / form-board puzzle (Healy–Fernald type) for higher ages.
  • Paper & pencil for copying a square, pattern drawing, etc.
  • Recording sheet (with Age-Level grid, Basal Age, Mental Age, Terminal Age, IQ) and a stopwatch.
Important Use only the objects/cards specified for the version you are administering, and use the Kannada or Marathi stimulus set with Indian children — never the English text.

7 · General Administration

Setting & rapport

  • Quiet, well-lit, distraction-free room; child comfortably seated across a small table.
  • Build rapport first; keep the child at ease — with very young children the biggest challenge is securing and holding attention.

Instructions & examiner behaviour

  • Use the manual's exact wording ("formula") for each item; the manual sometimes softens wording to hold attention (e.g. "This is one and this is another").
  • Present each item neutrally; give encouragement, but never cue the correct answer by look, tone or gesture.
  • Allow the specified repetitions and time limits precisely.

Where to start, stop & record

  • Start at an age level a little below the child's expected level.
  • Work downward until a basal is reached (an age level where all items pass).
  • Work upward until a ceiling is reached (an age level where all items fail).
  • Record every item as + (pass) or − (fail) in the age-level grid, plus verbatim responses and behaviour.

Handling questions / errors / interruptions

  • Score the response as given; do not correct the child.
  • Accept baby-talk / abbreviated names where the manual allows (e.g. "tick-tick" for a watch at Year III).
  • If attention breaks, re-secure it and, where permitted, re-present the trial series once.
Remember — 12-step per-item routine Prepare → place material → give the instruction (formula) → demonstrate (if allowed) → practice item (if any) → present test items → time → apply discontinuation → record (+/−) → score → convert to month-credit → interpret. This routine is the same for every item; only the content and pass-criterion change.
Common Error Failing to establish a proper basal before testing upward. If you skip this, the Mental-Age sum is wrong and the whole IQ is invalid.

Higher levels (Year XIX / XXII — adult)

The highest levels add the longest memory spans (e.g. repeating 30 syllables at XIX; 8 digits at XXII) and the most abstract reasoning items, and are used to establish the ceiling / Terminal Age for older testees.

Common Error Reading digit series at the wrong pace. The manual specifies pacing carefully — most series are read one every half-second, but the longer/starred series (5, 6, 7, 8 digits and all reversed series) are read one per second. Wrong pacing invalidates the item.

Alternate (substitute) items — what they are & why they matter

At almost every age level the manual lists one or more alternate items (marked Alt #1, Alt #2, Alt #3 on the record sheet, and A1 / A2 / A3 on the domain sheet). These are reserve tests kept in hand for the same age level.

Key Concept — why alternates exist A main item can sometimes become unusable for a particular child — for example the material is spoiled or missing, the item was accidentally cued/spoiled by the examiner, the child has already been coached on it, or a sensory/motor limitation makes that specific task unfair. An alternate lets the examiner still get a valid measure at that age level without losing the whole level's credit.

How scoring works with alternates

  • The alternate is substituted for the spoiled main item at that age level and scored by its own pass criterion (+/−), exactly like a regular item.
  • The substituted alternate then carries the same month-credit as the main item it replaced (e.g. ~2 months where a 12-month level has 6 items).
  • An alternate is used only to replace one invalid main item — you do not add alternates on top of the six main items to earn bonus credit.
  • If a valid administration of all main items is possible, the alternate is not needed.
  • Using an alternate is the manual's preferred fix — it is cleaner than statistical prorating (see §14) and should be tried first.

9 · Scoring — The Core Chain

This is the most important part. Keep the chain in mind and never collapse two steps into one.

Raw performance (+/− per item)→ Basal Age→ + month-credits for items passed above basal→ Mental Age (MA)→ IQ = MA / CA × 100→ Classification + Domain profile
Scoring Alert — keep these distinct
  • Raw score = pass/fail (+/−) on each item.
  • Basal Age = highest age level where all items pass.
  • Mental Age = Basal Age (in months) + summed month-credits for items passed above basal.
  • IQ = MA ÷ CA × 100 (a ratio, not a mental age).
  • Adjusted IQ = the BKT IQ rescaled from SD 18.7 to SD 15 (see §13).

How each item is scored

  • Each item is scored pass (+) or fail (−) against its stated criterion (e.g. "1 out of 3", "3 of 4", "no errors", within the time limit).
  • There is generally no partial credit per item — the item either meets its criterion or not. (Exceptions are items scored in marks, e.g. fables at XII scored out of 4 with ½-credit each; these marks then determine whether the age-level item counts as passed.)
  • Time-limited items failed on time are scored as fails.
  • Items below the basal are assumed passed; items at/above the ceiling are assumed failed.

Month-credit per item

Key Concept Each age level spans a number of months (e.g. one year = 12 months) and holds a fixed number of items, so each item passed above the basal is worth = (months in that age level) ÷ (number of items at that level). Because most BKT age levels carry 6 items across 12 months, each item passed is typically worth 2 months of Mental Age. (Use the exact credit printed for each level in the version you administer.)

10 · Chronological Age (CA)

CA is Date of Testing − Date of Birth, expressed in years and months (days are usually rounded). Subtract day-from-day, month-from-month, year-from-year, borrowing where needed.

Worked example
Date of Testing : 2026 - 08 - 21 (Y-M-D) Date of Birth : 2016 - 11 - 28
Days : 21 − 28 → borrow 1 month (30 d): 51 − 28 = 23 days Months: (8 − 1 borrowed) = 7 ; 7 − 11 → borrow 1 year (12 m): 19 − 11 = 8 months Years : (2026 − 1 borrowed) = 2025 ; 2025 − 2016 = 9 years
CA = 9 years 8 months (23 days) → for BKT use 9y 8m = 116 months
Remember Convert CA to months for the IQ formula: months = (years × 12) + months. 9y 8m = 116 months. Rounding convention for days: ≥16 days often rounds up a month — follow your centre's convention consistently.

11 · Basal Age & Mental Age (MA)

Step 1 — Basal Age (BA)

Find the highest age level at which the testee passes every item. Everything below is credited automatically. Express BA in months (e.g. Year VI basal = 72 months).

Step 2 — Add credits above the basal

For every item passed at levels above the basal, add its month-credit (typically 2 months per item when a 12-month level has 6 items).

Worked example — building MA
Basal Age : Year VI, all 6 items passed → 72 months Year VII : passed 4 of 6 items → 4 × 2 = 8 months Year VIII: passed 3 of 6 items → 3 × 2 = 6 months Year IX : passed 1 of 6 items → 1 × 2 = 2 months Year X : passed 0 items → ceiling reached
MA = 72 + 8 + 6 + 2 = 88 months = 7 years 4 months
Common Error Forgetting to credit the basal months, or double-counting items below the basal. Below-basal items are already inside the basal figure — only add credits for items passed above the basal.

12 · IQ Calculation (Ratio IQ)

IQ Calculation — the formula
IQ = ( Mental Age ÷ Chronological Age ) × 100
Both MA and CA in the same units (months). For older testees who have reached the top of the scale, CA is replaced by the Terminal Age (TA): IQ = (MA ÷ TA) × 100 (see §14 and the recording sheet).
Worked example (continuing §11)
MA = 88 months→ CA = 116 months→ IQ = 88/116 × 100→ IQ ≈ 75.9 ≈ 76
IQ = (88 ÷ 116) × 100 = 0.7586 × 100 = 75.9 → 76 (BKT ratio IQ)
Scoring Alert Do not stop at MA and call it "IQ". MA is an age; IQ is a ratio. A 9-year-old with MA 7y4m has a ratio IQ of ~76, not "76 months" and not "7.4".

13 · SD 18.7 → 15 Adjusted IQ

BKT was standardized with a standard deviation of 18.7. To compare a BKT IQ with tests that use SD 15 (Wechsler, modern Stanford-Binet), rescale it. This is the formula from the figure you supplied:

IQ Calculation — adjustment formula
Adjusted IQ = 100 − [ ( (100 − BKT IQ) ÷ 18.7 ) × 15 ]
This converts a score expressed on an SD-18.7 scale onto an SD-15 scale, keeping the mean at 100.
Worked example (BKT IQ = 76 from §12)
100 − BKT IQ = 100 − 76 = 24 24 ÷ 18.7 = 1.283 (SD units below mean) 1.283 × 15 = 19.25 Adjusted IQ = 100 − 19.25 = 80.75 ≈ 81
So a BKT IQ of 76 (SD 18.7) ≈ an SD-15 IQ of ~81.
Clinical Pearl Because SD 18.7 spreads scores more widely, extreme BKT IQs pull toward 100 when rescaled to SD 15. Always state which scale a reported IQ is on.

14 · Terminal Age & Prorating

Terminal Age (TA) — for older testees

Ratio IQ fails for adults because MA plateaus. BKT solves this by capping the denominator at a Terminal Age (the maximum chronological age the scale credits). The recording sheet's adult formula is:

IQ Calculation — adult / terminal formula
IQ = ( MA ÷ TA ) × 100
Use CA for children; substitute TA once CA exceeds the scale's terminal age.

Prorating (when an item/subtest is spoiled)

"Prorating" means estimating a total from the portion actually completed, when part of the test could not be given validly (e.g. a picture card is missing, or one timed item was invalidated). It is not the same thing as computing IQ from mental age.

Important — distinction
  • IQ from mental age = the normal route: MA/CA×100. Always done.
  • Prorating = a repair used only when a component is missing/invalid, by distributing the expected credit across the items that were validly administered. Used cautiously, flagged in the report.
Worked example — prorating a spoiled item
At Year VIII the child validly attempted only 5 of the 6 items (1 item spoiled: card missing). Passed 3 of the 5 valid items. Estimated pass proportion = 3 / 5 = 0.60 Expected passes over all 6 items = 0.60 × 6 = 3.6 → round to 4 items credited Credit = 4 × 2 months = 8 months at Year VIII
This prorated 8-month credit is then added into MA as usual. Note the proration in the report.
Scoring Alert — when NOT to prorate Never prorate from a single item, never prorate to "rescue" a clearly invalid administration, and never prorate an entire age level. Prefer re-testing the spoiled item if at all possible.

17 · Domain-wise Scoring & What Each Domain Means

Beyond the single IQ, BKT items can be re-grouped into six cognitive domains. The classification you attached is adapted from Lezak, M. D. (1983), Neuropsychological Assessment (2nd ed.), Oxford University Press, pp. 288–291 (Prasad Psycho Corporation sheet). Each cell of that sheet lists, for a given age level, which item numbers load on which domain (A1/A2/A3 = alternative items).

What each domain means (in simple language)

The six domains are simply different kinds of thinking that the everyday BKT items tap into. Here is each one explained plainly, with what it tells you clinically and the exact BKT tasks that measure it.

1 · Language In simple words: how well the person understands and uses words — knowing the names of things, explaining what words mean, having a good vocabulary, and following what is said to them.
Everyday example: a child who can name objects, tell you a spoon is "for eating" and follow "put the pencil on the book" has good language.
What weakness here can mean: limited schooling, a different home language, hearing problems, or a language/learning difficulty — not automatically low intelligence.
BKT items: naming familiar objects, definitions in terms of use, vocabulary lists (20/30/45 words), comprehension questions, naming colours.
2 · Memory (meaningful · non-meaningful · non-verbal) In simple words: the ability to hold information in mind for a moment and give it back.
  • Meaningful memory — remembering things that make sense, such as a whole sentence or a short story (meaning helps you hold it).
  • Non-meaningful memory — remembering things with no meaning, such as a string of random digits (pure "span"). Digits reversed also loads working memory.
  • Non-verbal memory — remembering things you see, such as a shape, design or bead pattern, without words.
What weakness here can mean: attention problems, anxiety, tiredness, or a genuine memory weakness.
BKT items: repeating digits forward & reversed (non-meaningful), repeating sentences / syllables (meaningful), remembering designs / bead-chain / picture details (non-verbal).
3 · Reasoning (verbal · non-verbal · numerical) In simple words: working things out logically — figuring out the sensible answer rather than just recalling a fact.
  • Verbal reasoning — reasoning in words ("what should you do if you miss your train?").
  • Non-verbal reasoning — reasoning with pictures, shapes or objects (spotting what is wrong in an absurd picture, comparing weights/lines).
  • Numerical reasoning — reasoning with numbers (counting, making change).
What weakness here can mean: immature problem-solving, weak higher-order thinking, or slowed thinking.
BKT items: comprehension ("what would you do if…"), detecting absurdities, problem questions, comparison of lines/weights, counting coins and making change.
4 · Conceptual Thinking In simple words: understanding abstract ideas and the relationships between things — how two things are alike, what a category is, or the deeper "moral" behind a story.
Everyday example: knowing that a mango and a banana are alike because "both are fruit," or that the lesson of a fable is about honesty.
What weakness here can mean: concrete/literal thinking — a hallmark of lower intellectual ability or immaturity; it usually appears at the higher age levels.
BKT items: similarities, differences, defining abstract words (pity, envy, justice), interpreting fables.
5 · Visuo-Motor In simple words: coordinating what the eyes see with what the hands do — copying, drawing and putting pieces together correctly.
Everyday example: accurately copying a square, or fitting the pieces of a cut-up card back together.
What weakness here can mean: fine-motor difficulty, visual-spatial problems, or a developmental coordination issue — this domain is relatively language-free, so it is useful when language is limited.
BKT items: copying a square, pattern drawing, divided (cut-up) card, construction / form-board puzzle.
6 · Social Intelligence In simple words: everyday practical and social know-how — good common-sense judgement, and understanding people and situations.
Everyday example: knowing your own age and sex, saying what you would do in a common situation, or explaining what is happening in a picture of a social scene.
What weakness here can mean: restricted social exposure, developmental delay, or (with a matching history) social-communication difficulties.
BKT items: giving sex / name / age, comprehension of social situations, describing and interpreting pictures of everyday scenes.

Domain-wise scoring — two sources, one method

There are two ways the item-to-domain mapping is fixed. Both feed the same scoring method.

A · From the manual Kamat's manual describes what each item measures in its "Text and Procedure / Remarks" notes. Using these, each item is assigned to the domain it primarily taps (e.g. "repeating digits" → non-meaningful memory; "similarities" → conceptual thinking; "copying a square" → visuo-motor). This is the qualitative, manual-based grouping.
B · From the domain-wise response sheet The Prasad Psycho "Domain-wise classification of items adapted to BKT" response sheet gives a ready-made table: for each age level it prints the exact item numbers that belong to each domain (with A1/A2/A3 = alternate items). This is adapted from Lezak (1983), Neuropsychological Assessment, 2nd ed., pp. 288–291 and is the authority you should tick against in a practical.
Key Concept — how the scoring works (both sources) Pick a domain → find (from the sheet, or from the manual notes) which item numbers at each age level belong to it → tick those the testee passed → the highest age level at which the domain's items were passed is that domain's age level. Do this for all six domains to get a domain-wise age profile that sits beside the global MA/IQ.

Reading the response sheet — step by step

How to read the sheet (age levels run down the left: 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 14, 16, 19, 22) 1. Pick a domain column (e.g. MEMORY → "Meaningful"). 2. For each age row, read the item number(s) the sheet prints in that cell (e.g. at Age 5 the Meaningful-Memory cell shows item 3; "A1 / A2" mean the alternate items). 3. On the actual record sheet, tick which of those items the testee passed. 4. The highest age row whose domain items were passed = that domain's age level. 5. Repeat for all six domains, then compare each against the overall Mental Age.
Example profile output: Language ≈ 9 yr • Memory ≈ 8 yr • Reasoning ≈ 7 yr • Conceptual Thinking ≈ 6 yr • Visuo-Motor ≈ 8 yr • Social Intelligence ≈ 7 yr
Important — use the sheet, don't invent Where the response sheet and the manual both apply, treat the domain-wise response sheet (Lezak-adapted) as the primary authority for exactly which item number belongs to which domain at each age, and use the manual's notes to understand why. Never guess the mapping from memory in a practical — read it off the sheet.
Clinical Pearl A wide gap between domains (e.g. strong Memory but weak Conceptual Thinking) is often more clinically informative than the global IQ — it can point to specific learning difficulties, language deprivation, or uneven development.

18 · Behavioural Observation

Behaviour during testing is part of the data. Record it separately from interpretation.

Observe (describe) Rapport, cooperation, motivation, attention/distractibility, persistence vs giving up, impulsivity vs reflection, strategy & planning, trial-and-error, response to failure, need for encouragement, fatigue, speed vs accuracy, speech & language quality.
Interpret (later, cautiously) Only after describing behaviour do you infer meaning (e.g. "frequent giving-up on harder items may have lowered the ceiling and slightly under-estimated ability"). Keep the two clearly separated in the report.
Common Error Writing interpretation as if it were observation ("the child was anxious" is an inference; "the child fidgeted, avoided eye contact and asked to stop twice" is an observation).

19 · IQ Classification & Interpretation

Important — which classification to use BKT yields a ratio IQ on an SD-18.7 scale. Use the classification bands printed in your BKT manual/centre protocol. The table below is a widely used descriptive framework for orientation; because of the SD-18.7 scaling, prefer the adjusted (SD-15) IQ when applying any band borrowed from SD-15 tests, and always report which scale you used.

130 and aboveVery superiorWell above age-peers

Scoring Alert Never diagnose intellectual disability from an IQ number alone — DSM-5/ICD-11 require deficits in adaptive functioning too. BKT gives the intellectual estimate; pair it with an adaptive-behaviour scale (e.g. VSMS).

Profile interpretation

  • Report the global IQ and classification first, with a confidence caveat.
  • Then describe the basal→ceiling spread (a wide, scattered spread suggests uneven abilities; a narrow spread suggests consistent functioning).
  • Add the six-domain profile — relative strengths and weaknesses.
  • Integrate behavioural observations.
  • Explain why the IQ should not be read in isolation.
Low performance — differentials Genuine intellectual limitation, poor attention, low motivation, anxiety, language/cultural mismatch, motor or visual-spatial difficulty, neurological factors, fatigue, or an artificially low ceiling from early giving-up.
High performance — cautions Genuine strength, but also task familiarity, coaching/practice effects, rich educational opportunity, high motivation. Avoid over-claiming from a single test.

21 · Case Studies

All cases fictional; illustrate how interpretation changes with the picture.

Case 1 · Average functioning 9y-old, referred for school review. CA 108 m, MA 106 m → IQ ≈ 98. Even basal→ceiling spread; flat domain profile. Good rapport, reflective. Impression: average intellectual functioning; no cognitive barrier to learning — look elsewhere for the school concern.
Case 2 · Borderline / below-average 10y-old. CA 120 m, MA 92 m → IQ ≈ 77 (adjusted ≈ 82). Wide scatter; weak Conceptual Thinking. Impression: borderline range; recommend adaptive-behaviour assessment and educational support.
Case 3 · Markedly low 8y-old. CA 96 m, MA 54 m → IQ ≈ 56. Low ceiling reached early across all domains. Impression: functioning in the ID range; confirm with adaptive measures before any diagnosis; parent counselling.
Case 4 · Uneven profile 11y-old. IQ ≈ 95 (average) but Memory at 11y level and Conceptual Thinking only at 7y level. Impression: average global IQ masks a specific reasoning weakness — investigate for a specific learning difficulty.
Case 5 · Language-limited 7y-old, home language differs from test language; verbal items depressed, visuo-motor intact. Impression: verbal IQ likely under-estimates true ability; re-test in mother-tongue and add a performance test.
Case 6 · Poor cooperation (child) 5y-old, fidgety, gave up early → premature ceiling. Impression: obtained IQ is a minimum estimate; recommend re-assessment when settled.
Case 7 · High anxiety 12y-old, visibly anxious, self-corrected constantly, slow. Timed items failed on speed not ability. Impression: anxiety likely lowered timed-item performance; interpret IQ as a conservative estimate.
Case 8 · Possible neurocognitive difficulty 14y-old post-illness. Marked visuo-motor and memory drop vs preserved language. Impression: pattern suggests acquired difficulty; refer for neuropsychological work-up.

23 · Psychometric Properties

  • Standardization: Indian (Kannada- & Marathi-speaking) sample of ~1074 children; mean IQ ≈ 99.8, SD 18.7.
  • Validity: the manual reports item–mental-age correlations (e.g. individual items around 0.83 with MA); good face and content validity for general ability.
  • Reliability: reasonable for an age scale, though based on decades-old data.
  • Cultural fit: a major strength — content and language localized to Indian children.
  • Limitations: ratio-IQ metric, wide SD (18.7), ageing norms, restricted standardization languages. Do not overstate the psychometric evidence.

24 · Ethical & Clinical Considerations

  • Qualified use: administer/interpret only if trained (RCI-relevant in India).
  • Standardization: follow exact procedure, timing and language; do not coach.
  • Informed consent / assent: from guardian and, appropriately, the child.
  • Confidentiality: secure records; share results appropriately.
  • No diagnostic overreach: an IQ is not a diagnosis; integrate history, adaptive functioning and other data.
  • Test security: protect items and scoring keys.

25 · Viva — Key Points to Remember

Kept short, as requested — the essentials most likely to be asked.

Must-know facts
  • BKT = Binet-Kamat Test, V. V. Kamat's Indian revision of the Binet-Simon scale.
  • Age range 3–22; individually administered age scale.
  • Yields a ratio IQ = MA/CA × 100; SD 18.7, mean ≈ 100 (N ≈ 1074).
  • Both verbal & non-verbal items → not a pure performance test.
  • Six domains: Language, Memory, Reasoning, Conceptual Thinking, Visuo-Motor, Social Intelligence.
  • Indian children tested in Kannada/Marathi, never the English version.
Trap questions — crisp answers
  • MA vs CA? MA = mental level reached on the test; CA = actual age since birth.
  • What is prorated IQ? An IQ where a missing/invalid component's credit was estimated from the validly completed items — not the same as IQ-from-MA.
  • Prorating vs IQ from MA? IQ-from-MA is the normal calculation; prorating is a repair used only when part of the test is spoiled.
  • When prorate? Only when a component can't be validly given; never from one item; flag it.
  • One item unfinished — IQ from it? No; you need the basal→ceiling pattern.
  • Why not a pure performance test? Because it contains strong verbal items too.
  • Main limitation? Ratio IQ + wide SD (18.7) → less suitable for adults and for precise disability cut-offs; old, language-specific norms.
  • Basal age? Highest level where all items pass. Ceiling? Level where all fail.
