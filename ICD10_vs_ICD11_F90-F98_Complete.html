The F90–F98 block: what holds it together

ICD-10 gathers here the behavioural and emotional disorders with onset usually occurring in childhood and adolescence — a heterogeneous block whose unifying thread is simply age of onset rather than a shared mechanism. The block runs from F90 to F98 (with F99 “unspecified mental disorder” closing Chapter V).

The territories are: F90 hyperkinetic disorders (the ICD-10 name for what is widely called ADHD); F91 conduct disorders; F92 mixed disorders of conduct and emotions; F93 emotional disorders with onset specific to childhood; F94 disorders of social functioning (selective mutism, attachment disorders); F95 tic disorders; and F98 other behavioural/emotional disorders (enuresis, encopresis, feeding disorder, pica, stereotyped movements, stuttering, cluttering).

Terms in plain language. Hyperkinetic = overactive; inattention = difficulty sustaining focus; impulsivity = acting without thinking; conduct disorder = a persistent pattern of rule-breaking/aggressive behaviour; oppositional = defiant/argumentative but not seriously rule-violating; ego-syntonic = feels part of oneself; tic = a sudden, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic movement or vocalisation; coprolalia = involuntary utterance of obscene words; echolalia/echopraxia = repeating others' words/actions; enuresis = involuntary wetting; encopresis = involuntary soiling; pica = eating non-food substances; selective (elective) mutism = consistent failure to speak in specific situations despite speaking in others.

ICD-10 vs ICD-11 — the biggest change: the block dissolves

ICD-10 kept an age-defined block for “childhood and adolescent” disorders. ICD-11 took a deliberately different philosophy: mental disorders should be grouped by their nature (phenomenology), not by the age at which they usually start. So the F90–F98 block is dismantled and its members are relocated to lifespan groupings alongside the adult conditions they most resemble. This is the single most examinable point.

ICD-10 — one age-based block

  • All these disorders sit together because they usually begin in childhood.
  • Hyperkinetic disorder (ADHD), conduct/oppositional disorders, childhood anxiety, attachment disorders, tics, and elimination/feeding disorders share the block.
  • Childhood anxiety (e.g. separation anxiety) is separated from adult anxiety disorders.

ICD-11 — dispersed by phenomenology

  • ADHD (6A05) → Neurodevelopmental disorders (with autism and intellectual developmental disorders).
  • Conduct-dissocial disorder (6C91) and oppositional defiant disorder (6C90) → a new grouping, Disruptive behaviour or dissocial disorders, with new callous-unemotional and chronic-irritability qualifiers.
  • Separation anxiety (6B05) and selective mutism (6B06) → Anxiety or fear-related disorders (no longer “childhood” anxiety — recognised across the lifespan).
  • Reactive attachment disorder (6B44) and disinhibited social engagement disorder → Disorders specifically associated with stress.
  • Tic disorders including Tourette (8A05) → Chapter 08, Diseases of the nervous system (neurology), cross-listed under neurodevelopmental disorders.
  • Enuresis (6C00) and encopresis (6C01) → a dedicated Elimination disorders grouping.
  • Feeding/pica → Feeding and eating disorders (6B8x); stuttering → developmental speech fluency disorder (6A01.1); stereotyped movement disorder (6A06) → neurodevelopmental.

The one-line exam answer

ICD-10 keeps a single age-based block (F90–F98) for childhood-onset behavioural and emotional disorders. ICD-11 abolishes the block and reclassifies each disorder by its phenomenology across the lifespan: ADHD and stereotyped movement to neurodevelopmental; conduct/oppositional to disruptive behaviour or dissocial disorders; separation anxiety and selective mutism to anxiety; attachment disorders to stress-related; tics to neurology; enuresis/encopresis to elimination disorders; stuttering to speech fluency. The principle: classify by what the disorder is, not by when it starts.

Etiology, epidemiology & comorbidities (shared themes)

Etiology

Kaplan & Sadock These disorders arise from an interaction of genetic/temperamental vulnerability with psychosocial factors. ADHD and tic disorders are strongly heritable and neurodevelopmental (dopaminergic and fronto-striatal systems); conduct and attachment disorders are more strongly shaped by adverse environments (harsh/inconsistent parenting, neglect, abuse, family adversity, deprivation); childhood emotional disorders reflect temperament (behavioural inhibition) interacting with attachment and stress.

Psychological theories

  • Attachment theory (Bowlby / Ainsworth). Other evidence Early caregiving quality shapes the child's internal working model of relationships — central to separation anxiety and to the attachment disorders (which follow grossly inadequate or disrupted care).
  • Social-learning theory (Bandura / Patterson). Aggressive/antisocial behaviour is learned and maintained through modelling and coercive family cycles — the basis of parent-management training for conduct problems.
  • Executive-function / self-regulation models of ADHD (Barkley). Core deficit in behavioural inhibition and self-regulation, not simply “attention”.
  • Behavioural model of tics. Tics are worsened by tension and temporarily relieved by performing them — the rationale for habit-reversal training.

Epidemiology

  • ADHD: roughly 5% of school-age children; male predominance; often persists (in attenuated form) into adulthood.
  • Conduct disorder: around 5%; more common in boys; a strong predictor of later antisocial (dissocial) personality disorder and substance use.
  • Childhood anxiety (incl. separation anxiety): among the commonest childhood disorders (several percent).
  • Tic disorders: transient tics are common in childhood; Tourette syndrome affects up to ~1%, boys more than girls.
  • Enuresis: common, declining with age (about 10% at age 5, falling steadily); more common in boys.

Comorbidities

These disorders co-occur heavily. ADHD co-occurs with conduct/oppositional disorder, learning disorders, tics and anxiety. Conduct disorder co-occurs with ADHD, mood disorders and substance use. Tourette co-occurs with ADHD and OCD. Childhood emotional disorders co-occur with each other and predict adult anxiety/depression. ICD-11's move to allow multiple diagnoses across groupings (e.g. ADHD + conduct-dissocial + a tic disorder) fits this reality better than the old block did.

Treatment (shared principles)

  • Psychosocial first for most: parent training/behavioural management (conduct, ADHD, ODD), CBT (childhood anxiety), habit-reversal training (tics), and bladder/bowel behavioural programmes (enuresis/encopresis).
  • Medication where indicated: stimulants (methylphenidate) or atomoxetine for ADHD; antipsychotics (e.g. risperidone, aripiprazole) or clonidine for severe/impairing tics; desmopressin/enuresis alarm for enuresis. Medication is generally adjunctive for conduct and emotional disorders.
  • Treat comorbidity, support the family and school, and safeguard where maltreatment underlies attachment disorders.

F90 · Hyperkinetic disorders (ADHD)

Group: F90 is the hyperkinetic/attention group of F90–F98. In ICD-11 it becomes 6A05 Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, moved into the Neurodevelopmental disorders grouping.

Characterised by a combination of overactive, poorly modulated behaviour with marked inattention and lack of persistent task involvement, that is pervasive over situations and persistent over time. Constitutional (neurodevelopmental) abnormalities are thought crucial. ICD-10 deliberately used “hyperkinetic disorder” rather than “attention deficit disorder”, but the two describe the same clinical picture.

ICD-10 general guidelines (F90)

The cardinal features are impaired attention and overactivity — both are necessary and must be evident in more than one situation (e.g. home, classroom, clinic):

  • Impaired attention: prematurely breaking off tasks, leaving activities unfinished, changing frequently from one activity to another. Diagnosed only if excessive for the child's age and IQ.
  • Overactivity: excessive restlessness — running/jumping, getting up when expected to stay seated, excessive talkativeness/noisiness, fidgeting — judged excessive against the situation and other children of the same age and IQ.
  • Impulsivity (a supporting feature): social disinhibition, recklessness in danger, intruding/interrupting, answering before questions are complete, difficulty waiting turns.

Onset before age 6 and long duration. Learning disorders and motor clumsiness (F80–F89) are noted separately, not as part of the diagnosis.

ICD-11 (6A05)

ADHD requires a persistent pattern (≥6 months) of inattention and/or hyperactivity-impulsivity, directly impairing functioning, with onset in the developmental period (typically before age 12), present across multiple situations, and excessive for age/developmental level. Presentations:

  • 6A05.0 predominantly inattentive
  • 6A05.1 predominantly hyperactive-impulsive
  • 6A05.2 combined

Changes from ICD-10: onset threshold relaxed to before 12 (not 6); an inattentive-only presentation is explicitly recognised; ADHD may be diagnosed together with autism and other neurodevelopmental disorders.

Unique / identifying features

The requirement that symptoms be pervasive across settings (not just at school or just at home) and developmentally excessive. Reckless, accident-prone, socially disinhibited behaviour and unpopularity with peers; frequent comorbid learning and motor problems. Symptoms often attenuate but persist into adulthood.

Differential diagnosis

From age-appropriate high activity; from conduct disorder (F91) (rule-breaking is deliberate/defiant there, versus unthinking here — the two often coexist); from anxiety/agitated depression (restlessness situational and mood-driven); from autism (F84); from hyperthyroidism, hearing problems and the effects of an understimulating or chaotic environment. In ICD-11, ADHD and autism can be coded together.

Remember — the ADHD triad

I–H–I: Inattention + Hyperactivity + Impulsivity, pervasive (>1 setting) and early-onset (ICD-10: before 6; ICD-11: before 12). ICD-10 needs BOTH inattention and overactivity; ICD-11 allows an inattentive-only presentation.

Treatment

  • Psychosocial: parent training/behavioural management, classroom behavioural strategies, and (in older children) organisational/CBT skills.
  • Pharmacological: stimulants (methylphenidate, lisdexamfetamine) first-line for moderate-severe ADHD; atomoxetine or guanfacine as alternatives; combine with behavioural support.

F90.0 · Disturbance of activity and attention

Group: F90 Hyperkinetic disorders → hyperkinesis without conduct disorder.

ICD-10 (F90.0)

Used when the overall criteria for hyperkinetic disorder (F90) are met but the criteria for conduct disorder (F91) are not. Includes: attention deficit disorder/hyperactivity disorder without conduct disorder.

ICD-11

Coded as 6A05 ADHD at the appropriate presentation; any co-occurring conduct-dissocial disorder is coded separately (6C91) rather than combined into one code.

Differential diagnosis

From hyperkinetic conduct disorder (F90.1) (conduct disorder also present); from conduct disorder alone (F91) without the pervasive inattention/overactivity.

Remember

“ADHD, no conduct disorder” — hyperkinesis on its own.

F90.1 · Hyperkinetic conduct disorder

Group: F90 Hyperkinetic disorders → hyperkinesis plus conduct disorder — the combination that predicts a worse outcome.

ICD-10 (F90.1)

Used when the overall criteria for both hyperkinetic disorder (F90) and conduct disorder (F91) are met. The presence or absence of conduct disorder is the basis for subdividing hyperkinetic disorder in ICD-10.

ICD-11

Coded as 6A05 ADHD + 6C91 Conduct-dissocial disorder (two separate diagnoses) — ICD-11 does not use a single combined code.

Differential diagnosis

From F90.0 (no conduct disorder) and from conduct disorder alone (F91) (no pervasive hyperkinesis). This combined presentation carries a higher risk of progression to antisocial outcomes.

Remember

“ADHD + conduct disorder together” — ICD-11 splits it into two codes; worse prognosis.

F91 · Conduct disorders

Group: F91 is the conduct group of F90–F98. In ICD-11 it moves to a new grouping, Disruptive behaviour or dissocial disorders (oppositional defiant disorder 6C90; conduct-dissocial disorder 6C91).

Characterised by a repetitive and persistent pattern of dissocial, aggressive, or defiant conduct that, at its most extreme, amounts to major violations of age-appropriate social expectations — more severe than ordinary childish mischief or adolescent rebelliousness. Isolated dissocial or criminal acts are not grounds for the diagnosis, which implies an enduring pattern. It may proceed to dissocial personality disorder (F60.2) in adulthood.

ICD-10 general guidelines (F91)

Diagnosis takes the child's developmental level into account. Examples on which the diagnosis is based include: excessive fighting or bullying; cruelty to animals or people; severe destructiveness to property; fire-setting; stealing; repeated lying; truancy and running away; unusually frequent/severe temper tantrums; defiant provocative behaviour; persistent severe disobedience. Any one of these, if marked, is sufficient; isolated acts are not. Duration of 6 months or longer is required.

Excludes: conduct disorders associated with emotional disorders (F92.-) or hyperkinetic disorders (F90.-); mood disorders (F30–F39); pervasive developmental disorders (F84.-); schizophrenia (F20.-).

ICD-11 (6C91)

Conduct-dissocial disorder: a repetitive, persistent pattern of behaviour that violates the basic rights of others or major age-appropriate societal norms/rules (aggression, destruction, deceit/theft, serious rule violation), sustained over a substantial period. Specified by childhood-onset vs adolescent-onset, and importantly by a “with limited prosocial emotions” qualifier (the callous-unemotional trait: lack of remorse/empathy) that marks a more severe, persistent subgroup.

Psychological understanding

Other evidence Coercive family-interaction cycles (Patterson) and social-learning of aggression maintain conduct problems; the callous-unemotional variant has a stronger heritable/temperamental basis and predicts persistence — which is why ICD-11 added the “limited prosocial emotions” qualifier.

Comorbidities

ADHD (very common), mood and anxiety disorders, substance use, and learning disorders. Conduct disorder is the strongest childhood predictor of adult dissocial personality disorder and substance use.

Differential diagnosis (general)

From mixed disorder of conduct and emotions (F92) (significant coexisting emotional disorder); from hyperkinetic conduct disorder (F90.1) (if ADHD criteria are also met, code that); from conduct symptoms secondary to mood disorder, PDD/autism or schizophrenia (code the underlying disorder); from isolated delinquent acts without an enduring pattern.

Remember — conduct disorder “≥6 months, major violations”

Enduring (≥6 months) pattern of aggression to people/animals, destruction, deceit/theft, serious rule violation. ICD-11 adds the callous-unemotional (“limited prosocial emotions”) qualifier.

F91.0 · Conduct disorder confined to the family context

Group: F91 Conduct disorders → conduct problems limited to the home.

ICD-10 (F91.0)

Dissocial or aggressive behaviour (not merely oppositional/defiant) that is entirely or almost entirely confined to the home and/or interactions with the immediate family, while the overall F91 criteria are met. There may be stealing from the home (often targeting one or two individuals), deliberate destruction of specific family members' possessions, violence against family members, or fire-setting confined to the home. Requires no significant conduct disturbance outside the family and normal-range social relationships outside it.

ICD-11

Subsumed within 6C91 Conduct-dissocial disorder; the context is described clinically rather than given its own code.

Differential diagnosis

From unsocialized/socialized conduct disorder (F91.1/F91.2) (behaviour extends beyond the family); from a purely relational/family problem without meeting F91 criteria; from adjustment reactions (e.g. to a new step-parent).

Remember

“Trouble only at home, normal outside” — conduct disorder confined to the family.

F91.1 · Unsocialized conduct disorder

Group: F91 Conduct disorders → conduct problems plus a pervasive failure to integrate with peers.

ICD-10 (F91.1)

Persistent dissocial/aggressive behaviour combined with a significant pervasive abnormality in the individual's relationships with other children — the child is isolated from, rejected by, or unpopular with peers and lacks close reciprocal friendships. Behaviour is typically solitary (bullying, fighting, defiance carried out alone). Includes: unsocialized aggressive disorder; solitary aggressive type.

ICD-11

Subsumed within 6C91 Conduct-dissocial disorder; the socialised/unsocialised distinction is not a separate code but may be described (and overlaps with the “limited prosocial emotions” qualifier for the more callous, isolated group).

Differential diagnosis

From socialized conduct disorder (F91.2) (there the child is integrated into a peer/delinquent group); from autism-related social isolation (F84); from depression-related withdrawal.

Remember

“Aggressive AND a loner” — unsocialized: rejected by peers, acts alone.

F91.2 · Socialized conduct disorder

Group: F91 Conduct disorders → conduct problems in a child who is integrated with a peer group.

ICD-10 (F91.2)

Persistent dissocial/aggressive/defiant behaviour in individuals who are generally well integrated into a peer group — often a delinquent peer group whose activities include the antisocial behaviour. The child typically has adequate, lasting friendships with peers of similar age. Delinquency in company, truancy and (often) group offending are characteristic. Includes: group delinquency; offences in the context of gang membership; stealing/truancy in company.

ICD-11

Subsumed within 6C91 Conduct-dissocial disorder.

Differential diagnosis

From unsocialized conduct disorder (F91.1) (peer-rejected, solitary); from delinquency that is a normative, transient adolescent-group phenomenon without meeting full criteria; from conduct symptoms secondary to another disorder.

Remember

“Delinquent, but has friends / a gang” — socialized: integrated peer group, often better prognosis than unsocialized.

F91.3 · Oppositional defiant disorder (ODD)

Group: F91 Conduct disorders → the milder, younger-child variant. ICD-11 gives it its own code, 6C90 Oppositional defiant disorder.

ICD-10 (F91.3)

Characteristically seen in children below age 9–10. Defined by markedly defiant, disobedient, provocative behaviour and by the absence of the more severe dissocial or aggressive acts that violate the law or the rights of others. The overall F91 criteria must be met; even severely naughty behaviour is not in itself sufficient. Essential feature: a pattern of persistently negativistic, hostile, defiant, provocative and disruptive behaviour, clearly outside the normal range for the child's age and sociocultural context. Many regard ODD as a less severe form of conduct disorder rather than a qualitatively distinct type; caution is advised in using it for older children.

ICD-11 (6C90)

Oppositional defiant disorder: a persistent pattern (≥6 months) of markedly defiant, disobedient, provocative or spiteful behaviour, more frequent/intense than expected for age. ICD-11 adds a “with chronic irritability-anger” qualifier (a predominantly angry/irritable mood presentation) and a “with limited prosocial emotions” qualifier.

Differential diagnosis

From conduct disorder (F91.0–F91.2) (which includes serious rights-violating/aggressive acts); from normal developmental defiance; from irritability in a mood or anxiety disorder or in ADHD (often comorbid); from disruptive mood dysregulation presentations (captured by ICD-11's chronic irritability-anger qualifier).

Remember

“Defiant & provocative, but no serious law/rights violations” — ODD is the milder, younger form; ICD-11 adds irritability-anger and callous qualifiers.

F92 · Mixed disorders of conduct and emotions

Group: F92 bridges conduct and emotional disorders of F90–F98 — used when both are prominent together.

ICD-10 (F92)

Characterised by the combination of persistent aggressive, dissocial or defiant behaviour (meeting conduct-disorder criteria, F91) with overt and marked symptoms of depression, anxiety or other emotional disturbance (meeting an F93 emotional-disorder or F40–F48 neurotic-disorder criteria).

  • F92.0 Depressive conduct disorder: conduct disorder (F91) combined with persistent and marked depression of mood (e.g. excessive misery, loss of interest and pleasure, self-blame, hopelessness), disturbance of sleep or appetite.
  • F92.8 Other mixed disorders of conduct and emotions: conduct disorder (F91) combined with persistent and marked emotional symptoms such as anxiety, fears, obsessions/compulsions, depersonalisation/derealisation, phobias or hypochondriasis.

ICD-11

No single “mixed” code: ICD-11 codes the conduct-dissocial disorder (6C91) and the mood/anxiety disorder separately, consistent with allowing multiple diagnoses.

Differential diagnosis

From conduct disorder alone (F91) (minor emotional upset does not qualify); from an emotional/mood disorder alone (F93/F30–F39) without a conduct disorder; from adjustment disorder.

Remember

“Conduct disorder + a full emotional disorder” (e.g. depressive conduct disorder) — ICD-11 codes the two components separately.

F93 · Emotional disorders with onset specific to childhood

Group: F93 is the childhood-emotional group of F90–F98. In ICD-11 these are mostly folded into the lifespan Anxiety or fear-related disorders (separation anxiety 6B05; the phobic/social forms into 6B01/6B04).

The key ICD-10 concept: these are largely exaggerations of normal developmental trends rather than qualitatively abnormal phenomena, and they differ from adult neurotic disorders. Many resolve; they are distinguished from normal childhood fears/worries by their severity, persistence and resulting impairment.

Differential diagnosis (whole F93 group)

From normal developmental fears/anxieties (which are milder, transient, non-impairing); from adult-type anxiety disorders (F40–F48) with childhood onset; from a pervasive developmental disorder (F84) or a mood disorder; from mixed conduct-and-emotions (F92) where conduct disorder coexists.

F93.0 · Separation anxiety disorder of childhood

Group: F93 Childhood emotional → the prototype. ICD-11: 6B05 Separation anxiety disorder (now a lifespan anxiety diagnosis).

ICD-10 (F93.0)

Some separation anxiety is normal in toddlers/preschoolers. This is diagnosed only when fear over separation is the focus of the anxiety, it arises in the early years, and it is of a severity that is statistically unusual (including abnormal persistence beyond the usual age) with significant impairment in social functioning. The anxiety may take the form of:

  • (a) unrealistic worry about harm befalling attachment figures, or that they will leave and not return;
  • (b) worry that an event (being lost, kidnapped, hospitalised, killed) will separate the child from a major attachment figure;
  • (c) reluctance/refusal to go to school because of separation fear (not other reasons);
  • (d) reluctance/refusal to go to sleep without being near an attachment figure;
  • (e) persistent inappropriate fear of being alone/without the attachment figure at home;
  • (f) repeated nightmares about separation;
  • (g) repeated physical symptoms (nausea, stomachache, headache, vomiting) on occasions of separation (e.g. leaving for school);
  • (h) excessive recurrent distress in anticipation of, during, or after separation.

ICD-11 (6B05)

Separation anxiety disorder: marked, developmentally inappropriate fear/anxiety about separation from specific attachment figures, with worry about harm to them or about events causing separation, reluctance to separate, and physical symptoms. ICD-11 recognises it across the lifespan (not only childhood).

Differential diagnosis

From normal separation anxiety (age-appropriate, non-impairing); from generalized anxiety (worry not focused on separation); from school refusal due to other causes (e.g. bullying, social anxiety); from autism-related distress at change.

Remember

“Fear is specifically about separation from attachment figures” — school refusal, sleep refusal, nightmares and tummy-aches on parting. ICD-11 makes it a lifespan anxiety disorder.

Treatment: CBT (graded exposure, parent involvement, return-to-school planning); treat comorbid anxiety/depression; SSRIs only for severe cases.

F93.1 · Phobic anxiety disorder of childhood

Group: F93 Childhood emotional → developmentally-phase-specific fears in exaggerated form.

ICD-10 (F93.1)

Fears in childhood that show a marked developmental-phase specificity (e.g. fears of animals, the dark, monsters) and arise at a broadly appropriate age, but are abnormal in degree and associated with significant impairment. It is distinguished from a normal developmental fear by severity/persistence, and from adult-type specific phobia by its developmental-phase-appropriate content.

ICD-11

Folded into 6B01 Specific phobia (a single lifespan diagnosis); ICD-11 does not keep a separate “of childhood” category.

Differential diagnosis

From normal developmental fears (mild, transient); from adult-type specific phobia (F40.2); from separation anxiety (F93.0) where the fear is separation-focused.

Remember

“Age-typical fear, but excessive” — ICD-11 just calls it specific phobia.

F93.2 · Social anxiety disorder of childhood

Group: F93 Childhood emotional → wariness of strangers/social situations beyond the normal phase.

ICD-10 (F93.2)

A persistent or recurrent fear and/or avoidance of strangers (adults and/or peers) that is beyond the normal developmental phase (some stranger-wariness is normal in infancy) and is associated with significant social impairment. The child shows social wariness or distress in new, strange or socially threatening situations. Onset is in the developmental-appropriate period.

ICD-11

Folded into 6B04 Social anxiety disorder (lifespan); no separate childhood category.

Differential diagnosis

From normal stranger-wariness of infancy; from selective mutism (F94.0) (there the child speaks in some settings but not others); from autism (F84) (pervasive social-communication deficit); from separation anxiety.

Remember

“Excessive wariness of strangers/social situations” — ICD-11 calls it social anxiety disorder.

F93.3 · Sibling rivalry disorder

Group: F93 Childhood emotional → emotional disturbance following the birth of a younger sibling.

ICD-10 (F93.3)

Some degree of emotional disturbance following the birth of an immediately younger sibling is normal. Sibling rivalry disorder is diagnosed when the disturbance is unusual in degree and/or persistence and is associated with psychosocial problems. Features may include: marked rivalry or jealousy, unusually negative feelings toward the younger sibling, deliberate harm/hostility, attention-seeking, and regression (loss of previously acquired skills such as bowel/bladder control, reverting to baby-like behaviour, and often oppositional behaviour with the parents), with anxiety, misery or social withdrawal.

ICD-11

No specific equivalent code. Such presentations are assessed as an adjustment disorder or captured under relational/psychosocial problem codes; ICD-11 did not retain a “sibling rivalry disorder” category.

Differential diagnosis

From normal sibling jealousy (mild, transient); from a broader emotional or conduct disorder; from an adjustment disorder to family change.

Remember

“Excessive jealousy + regression after a new baby” — ICD-11 drops the specific category (uses adjustment/relational codes).

F94 · Disorders of social functioning with onset specific to childhood and adolescence

Group: F94 covers abnormalities of social functioning beginning in childhood, without the pervasive constitutional deficit of autism. ICD-11 disperses them: selective mutism → anxiety (6B06); the attachment disorders → disorders associated with stress (6B44 / 6B45).

Differential diagnosis (whole F94 group)

From autism spectrum disorder (F84) (there the social deficit is pervasive and constitutional, not situational or deprivation-related); from intellectual disability; from primary anxiety disorders.

F94.0 · Elective (selective) mutism

Group: F94 Social functioning → consistent failure to speak in specific situations. ICD-11: 6B06 Selective mutism (now within anxiety disorders).

ICD-10 (F94.0)

A marked, emotionally determined selectivity in speaking, such that the child demonstrates language competence in some situations but fails to speak in other (definable) situations. Most often first manifest in early childhood, occurring with about equal frequency in the two sexes, and usually associated with marked personality features involving social anxiety, withdrawal, sensitivity or resistance. Typically the child speaks at home or with close friends but is mute at school or with strangers.

ICD-11 (6B06)

Selective mutism: consistent selectivity in speaking — adequate language in some social situations (typically home) but a consistent failure to speak in others (typically school), of sufficient duration and severity to impair functioning. ICD-11 places it among the anxiety or fear-related disorders, recognising its anxiety basis.

Differential diagnosis

From a developmental language disorder (F80) (there language is impaired in all settings); from autism (F84); from transient mutism when starting school or after migration/new language (allow an adjustment period); from social anxiety disorder (overlaps — ICD-11 classes mutism as anxiety-related).

Remember

“Talks at home, silent at school” — selective mutism; the language ability is intact, the failure is situational and anxiety-driven.

Treatment: behavioural/CBT approaches (graded exposure, stimulus fading, positive reinforcement of speaking); an SSRI may help in persistent cases.

F94.1 · Reactive attachment disorder of childhood

Group: F94 Social functioning → the “inhibited/withdrawn” attachment pattern after grossly inadequate care. ICD-11: 6B44 Reactive attachment disorder (within disorders associated with stress).

ICD-10 (F94.1)

Occurring in infants and young children, characterised by persistent abnormalities in the child's pattern of social relationships associated with emotional disturbance and reactive to changes in circumstances. Fearfulness and hypervigilance that do not respond to comforting are characteristic; poor social interaction with peers, aggression toward self and others, misery, and in some cases growth failure. It probably arises as a direct result of severe parental neglect, abuse or serious mishandling.

ICD-11 (6B44)

Reactive attachment disorder: grossly abnormal, inhibited/emotionally withdrawn attachment behaviour in a young child (rarely seeks or responds to comfort), with reduced positive affect and episodes of unexplained fearfulness/irritability, developing in the context of grossly inadequate care (neglect, deprivation). Placed among the stress-related disorders because a defined pathogenic care experience is required.

Differential diagnosis

From disinhibited attachment disorder (F94.2) (over-familiar, indiscriminate — the opposite pattern); from autism (F84) (constitutional, not caused by neglect; social reciprocity deficit present even with good care); from intellectual disability; from depression.

Remember

“Neglected child, withdrawn & fearful, won't be comforted” — reactive (inhibited) attachment.

F94.2 · Disinhibited attachment disorder of childhood

Group: F94 Social functioning → the “over-familiar/indiscriminate” attachment pattern. ICD-11: 6B45 Disinhibited social engagement disorder (within disorders associated with stress).

ICD-10 (F94.2)

A particular pattern of abnormal social functioning arising in the first five years, characterised by diffuse, non-selectively focused attachment behaviour, attention-seeking and indiscriminately friendly behaviour. Typically the young child shows clinging in infancy that gives way to indiscriminate friendliness and attention-seeking in early/middle childhood, with poorly modulated peer interactions. It tends to arise following institutional rearing or repeated changes of caregiver. Includes: affectionless psychopathy; institutional syndrome.

ICD-11 (6B45)

Disinhibited social engagement disorder: grossly abnormal social behaviour in which the child approaches and interacts with unfamiliar adults with a lack of the normal reticence (over-familiarity, willingness to go off with a stranger), developing after grossly inadequate care. Placed among the stress-related disorders.

Differential diagnosis

From reactive attachment disorder (F94.1) (withdrawn/inhibited — opposite pattern); from ADHD (impulsive social intrusion but not the indiscriminate-attachment quality); from autism (F84). The over-familiar approach to strangers is the signature.

Remember

“Indiscriminately friendly, goes off with strangers” — disinhibited (over-familiar) attachment; classic after institutional rearing.

F95 · Tic disorders

Group: F95 is the tic group of F90–F98. In ICD-11 tic disorders move to Chapter 08, Diseases of the nervous system (8A05) — recognised as neurological — and are cross-listed under neurodevelopmental disorders.

A tic is an involuntary, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic motor movement (usually involving a discrete muscle group) or vocal production that is sudden in onset and serves no apparent purpose. Tics tend to be experienced as irresistible but can usually be suppressed for varying periods, are exacerbated by stress, and typically disappear during sleep. Common simple motor tics: eye-blinking, neck-jerking, shoulder-shrugging, facial grimacing. Common simple vocal tics: throat-clearing, sniffing, grunting.

ICD-10 general guidelines (F95)

The major distinctions are between (1) single vs multiple tics, (2) motor vs vocal tics, and (3) duration (transient vs chronic). Onset is almost always in childhood/adolescence.

ICD-11 (8A05)

Tic disorders (including Tourette syndrome) are classified as neurological conditions in Chapter 08, cross-listed in the neurodevelopmental grouping — reflecting their basis in fronto-striatal circuitry.

Differential diagnosis (whole F95 group)

From stereotypies (F98.4 — rhythmic, prolonged, often soothing, seen in intellectual disability/autism) — tics are sudden and non-rhythmic; from chorea, dystonia, myoclonus and other movement disorders; from compulsions in OCD (goal-directed, to neutralise an obsession); from medication-induced movements.

F95.0 · Transient tic disorder

Group: F95 Tic disorders → the briefest, commonest form.

ICD-10 (F95.0)

Meets the general criteria for a tic disorder but the tics do not persist for longer than 12 months. Usually takes the form of eye-blinking, facial grimacing or head-jerking. The commonest tic disorder, most frequent around age 4–5 years.

ICD-11

Corresponds to a provisional/transient tic disorder within 8A05.

Differential diagnosis

From chronic tic disorder (F95.1) and Tourette (F95.2) (duration >1 year); from stereotypies; from habit movements.

Remember

“Tics < 12 months” — transient, usually simple, often self-limiting.

F95.1 · Chronic motor or vocal tic disorder

Group: F95 Tic disorders → long-lasting tics of one type only.

ICD-10 (F95.1)

Meets the general criteria for a tic disorder, with motor OR vocal tics (but not both), either single or multiple (but usually multiple), that persist for more than a year. The presence of both motor and vocal tics would instead indicate Tourette syndrome (F95.2).

ICD-11

Chronic motor or vocal tic disorder within 8A05 (single-modality tics persisting >1 year).

Differential diagnosis

From Tourette (F95.2) (needs both multiple motor and vocal tics); from transient tic disorder (F95.0) (<1 year); from other movement disorders.

Remember

“Motor OR vocal (not both), > 1 year” — chronic tic disorder.

F95.2 · Combined vocal and multiple motor tic disorder (Tourette's syndrome)

Group: F95 Tic disorders → the most severe, defining form. ICD-11: Tourette syndrome (8A05.00) in the nervous-system chapter.

ICD-10 (F95.2)

A form of tic disorder in which there are, or have been, multiple motor tics and one or more vocal tics, although these need not have occurred concurrently. Onset is almost always in childhood or adolescence; a history of motor tics before the development of vocal tics is common; symptoms frequently worsen during adolescence and commonly persist into adult life. The vocal tics are often multiple, with explosive repetitive vocalisations, throat-clearing and grunting, and there may be use of obscene words or phrases (coprolalia). Sometimes there is associated gestural echopraxia, which may also be obscene (copropraxia).

ICD-11 (8A05.00)

Tourette syndrome: combined multiple motor and one or more phonic (vocal) tics (not necessarily concurrent) persisting >1 year, with childhood onset — classified as a neurological disease, cross-listed with neurodevelopmental disorders.

Unique features

The combination of multiple motor + vocal tics, waxing-and-waning course, worsening in adolescence, and the (often over-emphasised, actually minority) feature of coprolalia. Strong comorbidity with ADHD and OCD.

Differential diagnosis

From chronic motor OR vocal tic disorder (F95.1) (single modality); from OCD (compulsions are goal-directed); from other movement disorders (chorea, dystonia, myoclonus); from stereotypies.

Remember

“Multiple motor + ≥1 vocal tic, >1 year, childhood onset” — Tourette; think comorbid ADHD + OCD; coprolalia is characteristic but not required or common.

Treatment (tic disorders)

  • Psychoeducation and watchful waiting for mild tics; habit-reversal training / comprehensive behavioural intervention for tics (CBIT) first-line behavioural treatment.
  • Medication for impairing tics: alpha-2 agonists (clonidine, guanfacine) or antipsychotics (risperidone, aripiprazole, haloperidol); treat comorbid ADHD and OCD.

F98 · Other behavioural and emotional disorders with onset usually in childhood and adolescence

Group: F98 is the mixed “other” group of F90–F98 — elimination, feeding, movement and speech-fluency problems. ICD-11 disperses them: enuresis/encopresis → elimination disorders; feeding/pica → feeding & eating; stereotypies → neurodevelopmental; stuttering → speech fluency.

F98.0 · Nonorganic enuresis

Group: F98 Other → involuntary wetting. ICD-11: 6C00 Enuresis (own elimination-disorders grouping).

ICD-10 (F98.0)

Involuntary voiding of urine, by day and/or by night, that is abnormal in relation to the child's mental age, and is not a consequence of neurological disorder, epileptic attacks or any structural abnormality of the urinary tract. It may be primary (present from birth — an abnormal extension of infantile incontinence) or secondary (following a period of acquired bladder control, usually beginning about age 5–7). Not usually diagnosed under a mental age of about 4 (chronological age about 5). May be monosymptomatic or associated with a wider emotional/behavioural disorder.

ICD-11 (6C00)

Enuresis: repeated voiding of urine into clothes or bed, voluntary or involuntary, in a child whose developmental age makes bladder control expected, not due to a physical condition. Specified as 6C00.0 nocturnal, 6C00.1 diurnal, 6C00.2 nocturnal and diurnal.

Differential diagnosis

From wetting due to a urinary-tract abnormality, urinary infection, diabetes, or neurological disorder/epilepsy (excluded before this diagnosis); from developmentally normal wetting below the expected age.

Remember

“Wetting past the expected age, no physical cause” — primary (never dry) vs secondary (relapse after being dry). ICD-11 splits nocturnal/diurnal/both.

Treatment: reassurance, fluid/toileting advice, enuresis alarm (best long-term), desmopressin for short-term/nocturnal control; treat any comorbid emotional disorder.

F98.1 · Nonorganic encopresis

Group: F98 Other → involuntary soiling. ICD-11: 6C01 Encopresis.

ICD-10 (F98.1)

Repeated voluntary or involuntary passage of faeces, usually of normal or near-normal consistency, in places not appropriate for that purpose in the child's sociocultural setting. It may be an abnormal continuation of normal infantile incontinence, or a loss of continence after a period of bowel control, or the deliberate deposition of faeces in inappropriate places despite normal control. It may occur alone or with a wider emotional/behavioural disorder, and is not due to an organic condition. Usually diagnosed from a mental age of about 4.

ICD-11 (6C01)

Encopresis: repeated passage of faeces in inappropriate places, in a child of a developmental age at which bowel control is expected, not due to a physical condition (other than one producing overflow around constipation, which is noted).

Differential diagnosis

From soiling due to organic constipation with overflow, anorectal malformation, or a neurological/gastrointestinal disease; from developmentally normal lack of control below the expected age.

Remember

“Soiling past the expected age, no organic cause” — always check for constipation-with-overflow.

Treatment: treat constipation (disimpaction + maintenance laxatives), toileting programme, positive reinforcement; address emotional factors.

F98.2 · Feeding disorder of infancy and childhood

Group: F98 Other → a feeding problem of early childhood. ICD-11: absorbed into Feeding and eating disorders (e.g. 6B83 ARFID).

ICD-10 (F98.2)

A feeding disorder of varying manifestations usually specific to infancy and early childhood, generally involving food refusal and extreme faddiness in the presence of an adequate food supply, a reasonably competent caregiver, and the absence of organic disease. There may be associated rumination (repeated regurgitation without nausea/GI illness). Not diagnosed where the feeding difficulty is due to organic disease or to another disorder.

ICD-11

Reframed within feeding/eating disorders — most such presentations map to 6B83 Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID), recognised across ages, or to rumination-regurgitation disorder (6B85).

Differential diagnosis

From feeding problems due to organic disease (GI, oromotor); from pica (F98.3); from an eating disorder in older children; from feeding problems secondary to autism or intellectual disability.

Remember

“Food refusal/faddiness, no organic cause” — ICD-11 largely calls it ARFID.

F98.3 · Pica of infancy and childhood

Group: F98 Other → eating non-nutritive substances. ICD-11: 6B84 Pica (feeding & eating disorders, all ages).

ICD-10 (F98.3)

The persistent eating of non-nutritive substances (soil, paint flakes, etc.). It may occur as one symptom of a wider psychiatric disorder (such as autism) or as a relatively isolated behaviour; it is coded here only when it is essentially the isolated psychopathology. The phenomenon is commonest in children with intellectual disability.

ICD-11 (6B84)

Pica: regular eating of non-nutritive, non-food substances that is persistent/severe enough to warrant clinical attention and is developmentally inappropriate — now recognised across the lifespan within feeding and eating disorders.

Differential diagnosis

From culturally sanctioned practices; from developmentally normal mouthing in infants; from iron/zinc deficiency states (investigate and treat); from pica occurring within autism or intellectual disability (code the context).

Remember

“Eating non-food substances” — check for iron/zinc deficiency; ICD-11 makes pica a lifespan eating-disorder diagnosis.

F98.4 · Stereotyped movement disorders

Group: F98 Other → repetitive, non-functional movements. ICD-11: 6A06 Stereotyped movement disorder (neurodevelopmental).

ICD-10 (F98.4)

Voluntary, repetitive, stereotyped, non-functional (and often rhythmic) movements that are not part of any recognised psychiatric or neurological condition. Examples: body-rocking, head-rocking, hair-plucking, hair-twisting, finger-flicking, and hand-flapping. Self-injurious stereotypies include repeated head-banging, face-slapping, eye-poking and biting of the hands, lips or other body parts. These occur most in children with intellectual disability. When they occur as part of another disorder, only the overall disorder is coded.

ICD-11 (6A06)

Stereotyped movement disorder: persistent (typically early-onset) voluntary, repetitive, non-functional and often rhythmic movements, with a specifier for with vs without self-injury — placed among neurodevelopmental disorders.

Differential diagnosis

From tics (F95) (sudden, non-rhythmic, suppressible — stereotypies are rhythmic, prolonged and often soothing); from compulsions in OCD; from movements within autism or a neurological disorder; from trichotillomania (hair-pulling as an impulse/body-focused behaviour).

Remember

“Rhythmic, soothing, repetitive (rocking, flapping, head-banging)” — stereotypies, not tics; specify with/without self-injury.

F98.5 · Stuttering (stammering)

Group: F98 Other → a disorder of speech fluency. ICD-11: 6A01.1 Developmental speech fluency disorder (moved to the neurodevelopmental speech/language group).

ICD-10 (F98.5)

Speech that is characterised by frequent repetition or prolongation of sounds, syllables or words, or by frequent hesitations or pauses that disrupt the rhythmic flow of speech. It should be classified as a disorder only if its severity is such as to markedly disturb the fluency of speech. May be accompanied by associated movements of the face and/or other body parts.

Excludes: tics (F95.-); cluttering (F98.6).

ICD-11 (6A01.1)

Developmental speech fluency disorder: frequent, marked disruptions of the rhythm/flow of speech (sound/syllable repetitions and prolongations, blocking, word substitutions, hesitations) with onset in the developmental period, inconsistent with age — classified among the developmental speech/language (neurodevelopmental) disorders.

Differential diagnosis

From cluttering (F98.6) (rapid, disorganised, arrhythmic speech with the person often unaware); from normal developmental dysfluency (mild, transient); from tics; from acquired (neurogenic) stuttering after brain injury.

Remember

“Repetitions/prolongations/blocks disrupting flow” — ICD-11 renames it developmental speech fluency disorder and moves it to the neurodevelopmental group.

F98.6 · Cluttering

Group: F98 Other → a fluency disorder distinct from stuttering.

ICD-10 (F98.6)

A rapid rate of speech with breakdown in fluency, but no repetitions or hesitations, of a severity that leads to reduced speech intelligibility. Speech is erratic and dysrhythmic, with rapid jerky spurts that usually involve faulty phrasing patterns. Unlike stuttering, the person is often unaware of the problem.

Excludes: stuttering (F98.5); tics (F95.-).

ICD-11

Addressed within developmental speech fluency disorder (6A01.1), which encompasses the range of fluency disturbances.

Differential diagnosis

From stuttering (F98.5) (repetitions/prolongations/blocks, with awareness) — cluttering is rapid, disorganised and typically without awareness; from a general language disorder.

Remember

“Too fast, jumbled, unaware” — cluttering (versus the blocking/repetition and awareness of stuttering).
