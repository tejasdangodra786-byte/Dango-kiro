Part I · What is ICD?

The International Classification of Diseases (ICD) is the World Health Organization's (WHO) global standard for classifying diseases, disorders and health conditions. ICD-11 / WHO ICD-10 / WHO

What ICD is for

  • Diagnostic classification — a shared, structured taxonomy of disorders with agreed names and codes.
  • Clinical communication — a common language so a diagnosis means the same thing across clinicians and countries.
  • Epidemiology & health-service planning — counting disease burden, planning services, allocating resources.
  • Research — comparable case definitions across studies and nations.
  • Coding & documentation — statistical reporting, reimbursement, mortality/morbidity records.

The mental-disorders portion of ICD is issued in dedicated clinical volumes: for ICD-10 the Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Guidelines (CDDG) — the "Blue Book", and for ICD-11 the Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR).

The single biggest conceptual shift for this block

ICD-10 grouped everything with a demonstrable cerebral/systemic cause under one "organic" umbrella (F00–F09). ICD-11 splits that umbrella in two: (1) Neurocognitive disorders (delirium, mild neurocognitive disorder, amnestic disorder, dementias — where cognition is the core feature), and (2) Secondary mental or behavioural syndromes (6E60–6E69 — where mood, psychosis, anxiety, catatonia, or personality change caused by a medical condition are the core feature). Part II · F00–F09 Overview — What "Organic, including symptomatic" means

ICD-10 / WHO In ICD-10, this block gathers disorders that share a common, demonstrable cause in cerebral disease, brain injury, or other insult leading to cerebral dysfunction. Two key definitions:

  • "Organic" — the syndrome can be attributed to an independently diagnosable cerebral or systemic disease. It does not imply other disorders are "non-organic" or without a brain substrate.
  • "Symptomatic" — cerebral involvement is secondary to a systemic (extracerebral) disease (e.g. hepatic encephalopathy, thyroid disease).

The dysfunction may be primary (disease/injury acting directly on the brain, e.g. Alzheimer's, head injury) or secondary (systemic disease affecting the brain as one of several organs, e.g. hypothyroidism, uraemia).

Two clinical clusters within the block ICD-10 / WHO

  1. Cognitive/sensorium cluster — the invariable, prominent feature is disturbed cognition (memory, intellect, learning) or disturbed sensorium (consciousness, attention): dementia, delirium, amnesic syndrome.
  2. Symptomatic cluster — the prominent feature is in perception (hallucinations), thought content (delusions), mood/emotion, or personality/behaviour, with minimal cognitive disturbance: organic hallucinosis, organic mood/anxiety/psychotic disorder, organic personality change.

ICD-11 / WHO ICD-11 conceptualises the same clinical territory but re-labels the first cluster "Neurocognitive disorders" (acquired conditions where cognition is the main affected area) and moves the second cluster into "Secondary mental or behavioural syndromes" (6E6x). The word "organic" is deliberately retired because all mental disorders have a biological substrate; "neurocognitive" and "secondary … due to a medical condition" are considered more precise.

Boundary note — alcohol and drugs. In both systems, mental disorders caused by alcohol/psychoactive substances are placed with the substance-use disorders, not here — ICD-10 F10–F19, ICD-11 6C4x. That is why several F00–F09 titles explicitly say "not induced by alcohol and other psychoactive substances". ICD-10 / WHO

Part III · Dementia — the general concept (applies to F00–F03 / 6D80–6D85)

ICD-10 / WHO ICD-10 gives one general description of dementia that sets the minimum requirement for diagnosing dementia of any type, before the type-specific guidelines.

ICD-10 general definition of dementia ICD-10 / WHO

A syndrome due to disease of the brain, usually chronic or progressive, with disturbance of multiple higher cortical functions — memory, thinking, orientation, comprehension, calculation, learning capacity, language and judgement. Consciousness is not clouded (this separates it from delirium). Cognitive impairment is commonly accompanied — and occasionally preceded — by deterioration in emotional control, social behaviour or motivation. There is appreciable decline in intellectual functioning and usually interference with activities of daily living (washing, dressing, eating, hygiene, toileting).

Diagnostic caution built into ICD-10: avoid false positives — depression, motor slowness and physical frailty (not loss of intellect) may explain poor performance. Do not use change in job role as a criterion because of cross-cultural differences.

ICD-11 general definition of dementia ICD-11 / WHO

An acquired chronic condition characterised by significant cognitive decline from a previous level, in two or more cognitive domains (memory, attention, executive function, language, social cognition, psychomotor speed, visuoperceptual/visuospatial ability), severe enough to interfere with independence in activities of daily living. If cognitive symptoms occur only during a depressive episode, dementia is generally not diagnosed (though depression can be superimposed on dementia).

What changed in the general dementia concept?

  • Domain count made explicit: ICD-11 states "two or more cognitive domains"; ICD-10 said "multiple higher cortical functions" without a number.
  • Broader domain list: ICD-11 explicitly adds executive function, social cognition, psychomotor speed alongside memory/language — reflecting that memory need not be the first or worst domain (important for frontotemporal and vascular presentations).
  • Severity now coded separately via extension codes: XS5W mild, XS0T moderate, XS25 severe. ICD-11 / WHO
  • Behavioural/psychological disturbances get their own codes (6D86) instead of the ICD-10 fifth character (.x1 delusional, .x2 hallucinatory, .x3 depressive, .x4 mixed).
  • Both retain: consciousness/awareness is not primarily clouded (distinguishing dementia from delirium) and both require double coding (syndrome + cause).
🧭 General dementia signature: think dementia when there is acquired, persistent, multi-domain cognitive decline with clear consciousness and loss of daily-living independence, in the context of an identifiable brain/systemic disease.

B. Basic definition

ICD-10 concept ICD-10 / WHO — a primary degenerative cerebral disease of unknown aetiology with characteristic neuropathological and neurochemical features; insidious onset, slow steady progression over years.

ICD-11 concept ICD-11 / WHO — dementia (multi-domain, acquired, functionally impairing) in which the pattern and course are consistent with Alzheimer disease; requires the etiological code linking to Alzheimer disease.

Simple explanation: a slowly progressive brain disease that erodes memory first, then language, orientation and reasoning, until the person can no longer manage daily life.

C. Core clinical concept

Amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tangles accumulate, cholinergic neurons are lost, and the person shows an insidious, gradually progressive amnestic-predominant dementia — early difficulty forming new memories, later spreading to language, praxis and executive function, with clear consciousness throughout.

WHAT CHANGED? (F00 → 6D80)

  • Added subtype: 6D80.2 "mixed with cerebrovascular disease" and 6D80.3 "mixed with other non-vascular aetiologies" formalise mixed pathology (ICD-10 folded mixed cases into F00.2).
  • Severity externalised: mild/moderate/severe via XS5W/XS0T/XS25 rather than being implicit.
  • BPSD coded separately (6D86) rather than by ICD-10 fifth characters.
  • Terminology: "Dementia in Alzheimer's disease" → "Dementia due to Alzheimer disease" (drops the possessive apostrophe, "due to" clarifies causation).
  • Unchanged: insidious onset, gradual progression, early/late-onset distinction, requirement to exclude other causes, double coding.

Duration

Neither system fixes a single numeric minimum, but both require a course consistent with a chronic, progressive dementia (months–years). ICD-10 notes the illness may run as short as 2–3 years or considerably longer. ICD-10 / WHO ICD does not specify a fixed numeric minimum duration for Alzheimer dementia; the requirement is a persistent, progressive course.

Clinical features

Cognitive

  • Memory: early anterograde amnesia — difficulty forming new memories; recent memory worse than remote.
  • Language: word-finding difficulty, later aphasia — impaired language.
  • Visuospatial: getting lost, dressing/constructional apraxia — impaired skilled movement.
  • Executive: poor planning, judgement, problem-solving.
  • Orientation: time first, then place, then person (late).

Behavioural / emotional / functional

  • Apathy, depression, irritability early; later agitation, wandering, psychosis (BPSD → 6D86).
  • Progressive loss of ADLs: finances/shopping → dressing/hygiene → feeding/continence.
  • Consciousness remains clear until very late.

How to recognise this disorder

  • Older adult, insidious onset over years.
  • Memory-led decline (loses recent conversations, repeats questions).
  • Smooth, gradual progression (not stepwise).
  • Preserved alertness; no early focal neuro signs.
  • Often anosognosia (limited insight into deficits).
  • Neuroimaging: medial-temporal/hippocampal atrophy.
🧭 One-line signature: Think Alzheimer dementia when you see insidious + gradually progressive + memory-first multi-domain decline in an alert older adult with no early focal signs. (A guide to recognition, not an absolute rule.)

Etiology & pathophysiology Kaplan & Sadock ICD-10 / WHO

Pathology: extracellular β-amyloid plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (hyperphosphorylated tau), neuronal loss especially hippocampus, temporoparietal and frontal cortex, and the cholinergic nucleus basalis. Neurochemistry: marked reduction in choline acetyltransferase and acetylcholine (the rationale for cholinesterase-inhibitor therapy). Risk factors: age (strongest), family history/genetics (APOE ε4; autosomal-dominant APP/PSEN1/PSEN2 in early-onset), Down syndrome (very high risk), vascular risk factors, lower cognitive reserve.

Epidemiology Kaplan & Sadock

Commonest cause of dementia (~50–60% of cases); prevalence roughly doubles every ~5 years after age 65; late-onset far more common than early-onset; slight female preponderance (partly longevity).

Assessment & tests

  • History (onset/course, ADLs, vascular risk, family history) + collateral history (essential — patient insight often poor).
  • MSE and cognitive examination: MMSE, MoCA (more sensitive to mild/executive), ACE-III; clock-drawing; delayed recall.
  • Neurological + general medical exam; bloods (TFTs, B12/folate, glucose, U&E, calcium) to exclude reversible causes.
  • Neuroimaging (MRI/CT) — atrophy pattern, exclude vascular/structural cause.
  • Neuropsychological battery for pattern & baseline; functional assessment of ADLs.
Screening ≠ diagnosis. No single screen (MMSE/MoCA) establishes the diagnosis; they quantify impairment and track change within a full clinical evaluation.

  • Psychoeducation, caregiver-burden support, communication strategies, home-safety, wandering precautions, medication supervision, advance care planning, respite.

Prognosis

Progressive and, at present, irreversible (ICD-10 states this explicitly). ICD-10 / WHO Treatments slow symptoms rather than cure; median survival from diagnosis is several years, shorter with earlier/more rapid onset. Do not overstate certainty for the individual.

Part IV · High-Yield Syndromes

DELIRIUM IN 60 SECONDS

  • What: acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention + awareness due to a medical cause.
  • When to suspect: any sudden change in behaviour/cognition in a medically unwell or elderly patient.
  • Bedside test: attention (months backwards, digit span); check for fluctuation and altered arousal.
  • Three motor types: hyperactive (obvious), hypoactive (missed!), mixed.
  • Do first: find & treat the cause; non-pharmacological measures; drugs only for severe distress/danger.
  • Never forget: delirium is a medical emergency and a marker of poor outcomes.
"DELIRIUMS" — the core picture
Disordered attention · Electrolyte/metabolic cause · Level of consciousness fluctuates · Illusions/hallucinations (visual) · Rapid onset · Infection (common trigger) · Unstable emotions · Medications · Sleep–wake reversal.

DEMENTIA RECOGNITION FRAMEWORK

  1. Establish the syndrome: acquired, persistent decline in ≥2 cognitive domains with loss of ADL independence and clear consciousness.
  2. Establish the course: insidious/gradual (AD, FTD, DLB) vs stepwise/post-stroke (vascular) vs rapid (CJD).
  3. Read the leading domain: memory (AD) · executive/processing (vascular) · behaviour/language (FTD) · fluctuation+visual hallucinations+parkinsonism (DLB).
  4. Exclude reversible causes (VITAMINS DE): thyroid, B12, NPH, depression, medications, infection.
  5. Grade severity (ICD-11: mild/moderate/severe) and code BPSD (6D86) if present.

AMNESTIC SYNDROME

Selective failure of encoding/consolidation of new declarative memory with preserved attention and general intellect. Trace the memory process:

  • Encoding — registering new information (impaired: can't learn a word list).
  • Storage/consolidation — stabilising memory (impaired: forgets within minutes; medial-temporal/diencephalic damage).
  • Retrieval — recall vs recognition (in amnesia both are impaired for new material).
  • Anterograde (post-onset new learning) usually worse than retrograde (temporal gradient).
  • Confabulation may fill gaps (classic Korsakoff).
Clinical example: a malnourished patient develops confusion and ataxia (Wernicke). After acute treatment, a persistent state remains in which he chats normally but cannot recall the nurse he met five minutes ago and invents plausible accounts of his day — an amnestic (Korsakoff) syndrome; give thiamine early.

ORGANIC / SECONDARY PSYCHOTIC SYNDROMES

Psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) can arise secondary to a physiological/neurological condition. Distinguish from primary psychosis:

FeatureSecondary (organic) psychosisPrimary psychotic disorder
Consciousness/attentionMay fluctuate (esp. if delirious)Clear
Hallucination typeOften visualOften auditory
Onset age/contextAtypical age; active medical illness/medicationTypically late teens–30s; no medical trigger
CourseTracks the medical causeIndependent of medical illness

ORGANIC / SECONDARY MOOD SYNDROMES

  • Depressive presentation — e.g. hypothyroidism, stroke, Parkinson, steroids-withdrawal, pancreatic cancer.
  • Manic presentation — e.g. corticosteroids, hyperthyroidism, frontal lesions, some medications.
  • Mixed/other — combinations, or emotional lability.

The clinical context changes reasoning: an older adult with a first manic episode and an abnormal exam should prompt a search for a secondary cause before diagnosing a primary bipolar disorder. ICD-11 codes these as 6E62 secondary mood syndrome with a symptom qualifier. ICD-11 / WHO

ORGANIC / SECONDARY PERSONALITY & BEHAVIOURAL SYNDROMES

  • Personality change from a baseline (not lifelong).
  • Behavioural disinhibition (frontal), impaired judgement, socially inappropriate behaviour.
  • Apathy / reduced goal-directed activity.
  • Irritability / emotional lability ("emotional incontinence").
  • Executive dysfunction — poor planning, perseveration, set-shifting difficulty.
🧭 Frontal-system presentations: "same intellect, different person." Cognition can test near-normal while behaviour and social conduct are transformed.

Part VII · Mnemonics

F00–F09 hierarchy — "Doctors Very Precisely Uncover Amnesia, Delirium, Other Personality Unknowns"
Dementia Alzheimer (F00) · Vascular dementia (F01) · Pick's/other-disease dementia (F02) · Unspecified dementia (F03) · Amnesic syndrome (F04) · Delirium (F05) · Other mental disorders/organic (F06) · Personality & behavioural (F07) · Unspecified organic (F09).

Application: walk the block in numeric order — the three dementias, then amnesia, delirium, and the symptomatic/personality cluster.

Application: the 6D7x "syndromes" then 6D8x "dementias by cause", ending in 6D86 behavioural symptoms.

Dementia assessment — "MIND FRAME"
Memory & other cognition (MMSE/MoCA/ACE-III) · Informant/collateral history · Neurological exam · Daily-living function · Fluids/bloods (TFT, B12, glucose, U&E, Ca) · Radiology (MRI/CT) · Affect/mood (screen depression) · Medication review · Exclude reversible causes.
Reversible cognitive-impairment causes — "DEMENTIA-R"
Drugs/toxins · Endocrine (thyroid) · Metabolic/electrolytes · Emotional (depression) · Nutritional (B12, thiamine) · Tumour/structural · Infection · Alcohol · R = normal-pressure hydRocephalus.
Dementia differential by pattern — "FAST-LV"
Frontotemporal (behaviour/language, young) · Alzheimer (memory, insidious) · Stroke = vascular (stepwise, focal) · Treatable/reversible (screen!) · Lewy (fluctuation + visual hallucinations + parkinsonism) · Variant fast = prion (rapid + myoclonus).
Delirium management — "TIME"
Think of it & screen attention · Investigate & treat the cause · Manage non-pharmacologically first (orientation, sensory aids, sleep) · Engage family & reassess (drugs only for severe distress/danger).

Unique Memory Techniques

Contrast pairs (learn by opposite)

  • Delirium = acute + attention ↔ Dementia = chronic + memory.
  • Amnesia = memory only ↔ Dementia = many domains.
  • Alzheimer = smooth ↔ Vascular = steps.
  • Lewy = fluctuates + sees things ↔ Alzheimer = steady + forgets things.

"If you see X, think Y" triggers

  • Visual hallucinations + parkinsonism → Lewy body.
  • Young + disinhibition → frontotemporal.
  • Rapid dementia + myoclonus → CJD/prion.
  • Gait + dementia + incontinence → NPH (treatable!).
  • Confabulation + can't learn + alcohol → Korsakoff (thiamine!).
  • First mania in old age → secondary mood syndrome.

Brain-region association

  • Hippocampus/diencephalon → memory (amnesia, Alzheimer).
  • Frontal lobe → behaviour/executive (personality change, FTD).
  • Subcortical/white matter → slowing (vascular, subcortical dementia).
  • Diffuse/global → delirium.

Timeline method

  • Hours–days = delirium.
  • Weeks–months, mood-linked = depression.
  • Months–years, smooth = Alzheimer.
  • Sudden steps = vascular.
  • Weeks, relentless + myoclonus = prion.

Diagnostic decision tree (bedside)

1. Is attention/awareness acutely disturbed & fluctuating? → Delirium (find the cause). → If no:
2. Is there multi-domain decline with ADL loss? → Dementia (then subtype by course + leading domain + neuro features). → If no:
3. Is the deficit isolated to memory? → Amnestic disorder. → If no:
4. Is the prominent picture a psychiatric syndrome caused by a medical condition? → Secondary (organic) syndrome (psychotic/mood/anxiety/catatonia/personality change).

Case Vignettes (fictional — for teaching only)

Vignette 1. A 72-year-old retired teacher is brought by her daughter with 3 years of gradually worsening forgetfulness — repeating questions, misplacing items, getting lost on familiar routes. Alert and cooperative; MoCA shows impaired delayed recall and orientation; no focal neurological signs; MRI shows medial-temporal atrophy. Bloods normal.

Which diagnosis should be considered?

Show reasoning & answer

Dementia due to Alzheimer disease (ICD-11 6D80 / ICD-10 F00.1 late-onset). Insidious, gradually progressive, memory-led multi-domain decline in an alert older adult with no focal signs and hippocampal atrophy; reversible causes screened out. Consider severity coding (e.g. XS5W mild) and BPSD codes if present.

Vignette 2. An 80-year-old man on a surgical ward 2 days after hip surgery becomes acutely confused overnight — pulling at his lines, seeing "insects" on the wall, drowsy by day and agitated at night. Attention is grossly impaired (cannot recite months backwards); the picture fluctuates hour to hour. He has a urinary catheter and a low-grade fever.

Which diagnosis should be considered?

Show reasoning & answer

Delirium (ICD-11 6D70 / ICD-10 F05). Acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with visual hallucinations and sleep–wake reversal, in a post-operative patient with a probable infective trigger (UTI). Manage as an emergency: investigate/treat the cause, non-pharmacological measures first.

Vignette 3. A 58-year-old man with a long history of alcohol use is admitted with confusion, unsteadiness and eye-movement abnormality. After acute treatment he is alert and conversational but cannot remember events from minute to minute and produces detailed but false accounts of recent activities. Attention and general knowledge are relatively intact.

Which diagnosis should be considered?

Show reasoning & answer

Amnestic disorder (Korsakoff), alcohol-related — ICD-11 6D72.10 (ICD-10 would code the alcohol-induced amnesic syndrome in F10). Isolated prominent memory impairment with confabulation and preserved attention/intellect, following Wernicke encephalopathy. Give thiamine early.

Vignette 4. A 77-year-old woman has an 18-month history of cognitive decline with day-to-day fluctuation, recurrent detailed visual hallucinations of children in the house, mild resting tremor and rigidity, and a history of acting out dreams at night. A previous antipsychotic caused a severe adverse reaction.

Which diagnosis should be considered?

Show reasoning & answer

Dementia due to Lewy body disease (ICD-11 6D82). Fluctuating cognition, well-formed visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism, REM-sleep behaviour disorder and neuroleptic sensitivity. Note this is a new stand-alone category in ICD-11 (was under ICD-10 F02.8 "other"). Avoid typical antipsychotics.

Vignette 5. A 60-year-old previously mild-mannered accountant develops, over a year, socially disinhibited behaviour, tactless remarks, apathy toward work, and impulsive spending. Memory testing is relatively preserved; executive tasks are impaired. MRI shows frontal and anterior-temporal atrophy.

Which diagnosis should be considered?

Show reasoning & answer

Frontotemporal dementia, behavioural variant (ICD-11 6D83 + 8A23). Early behaviour/personality change with disinhibition and apathy, relative early memory sparing, younger onset, frontotemporal atrophy — contrast with Alzheimer's memory-led picture. (If cognition were near-normal and this were purely a post-injury personality change, consider secondary personality change 6E68.)

Vignette 6. A 68-year-old man with newly diagnosed hypothyroidism develops low mood, anhedonia, poor concentration and hopelessness over two months. There is no prior psychiatric history; thyroid function is markedly abnormal. Mood symptoms improve substantially as thyroid replacement normalises his levels.

Which diagnosis should be considered?

Show reasoning & answer

Secondary mood syndrome, with depressive symptoms (ICD-11 6E62.0 / ICD-10 F06.32 organic depressive disorder). A depressive syndrome directly attributable to a medical condition, temporally linked and improving as the cause is treated. Code the syndrome plus the hypothyroidism.

Vignette 7. A 74-year-old woman has slowly progressive difficulty walking (broad-based, "magnetic" gait), urinary urgency/incontinence and cognitive slowing over a year. CT shows enlarged ventricles out of proportion to sulcal atrophy.

Which diagnosis should be considered?

Show reasoning & answer

Dementia due to normal-pressure hydrocephalus (ICD-11 6D85.6). The classic triad — gait disturbance, cognitive impairment, urinary incontinence — with ventriculomegaly. Important because it is potentially reversible with CSF shunting; a key "don't-miss" cause.
