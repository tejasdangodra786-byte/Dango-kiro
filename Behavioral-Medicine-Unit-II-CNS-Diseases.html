1 The central nervous system & how brain damage causes disturbance

Before we study each disease, we need one simple map. The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain plus the spinal cord — the body's control centre. When any part of it is damaged, the result is not random: the damage shows up in a few predictable ways, and the problems it causes fall into four groups the syllabus names — cognitive (thinking), personality (the person's usual self), behavioral (what they do), and emotional (how they feel). Learn this frame once and every later topic becomes easy to write.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. State the psychological reading — a clinical psychologist studies brain damage through its consequences for the mind: thinking, personality, behaviour and feeling.
  2. Define the four disturbance domains — cognitive, personality, behavioral, emotional — in one easy line each; these are the four headings you will use for every disease.
  3. Show how a lesion becomes a psychological change — the four ways damage shows itself (destructive, irritative, release, compensation) as the bridge from injury to the mind.
  4. Make the mind–body interaction explicit — the brain shapes the mind, and mood, coping, family and stigma feed back into the illness and its recovery.
  5. Give the principles that decide the picture — focal vs diffuse, acute vs progressive, left vs right hemisphere.
  6. Add the medical background in brief — just enough anatomy (brain, cord, grey/white matter, protection) to understand the psychology.

The detailed notes below follow exactly this order, so you can read top-to-bottom and write the answer in the same sequence.

Detailed notes

1. The four disturbance domains — the heart of the clinical-psychology reading

A clinical psychologist does not study a brain lesion for its own sake; the interest is in what the damage does to the person's mind and life. So the syllabus asks for four kinds of disturbance in every disease. Keep these simple definitions and use them as headings each time, and always describe them as a lived experience, not just a label:

  • Cognitive — thinking skills (attention, memory, language, planning, seeing-and-making-sense of things). Lived as forgetting a daughter's visit, losing the thread of a sentence, or no longer being able to plan a meal.
  • Personality — the person's usual, stable way of being (their typical character and manner). Lived as "he is not the man I married" — becoming blunt, apathetic, rigid, suspicious, or childlike compared with the old self.
  • Behavioral — the actions you can actually see (what the person does, and how they manage daily tasks). Lived as acting without a brake, angry outbursts, wandering, or needing help to dress, wash and eat.
  • Emotional — the person's feelings and mood (sadness, fear, anger, flatness). Lived as depression, fear, irritability, or sudden crying/laughing the person cannot stop.
Common confusion

Personality change and behaviour change overlap but are not the same. Personality is the inner character ("he has become cold and uninterested"); behaviour is the outward act ("he swears at strangers and gambles"). In exams, give at least one clear example under each so the examiner sees you can tell them apart.

2. From lesion to psychological change — the four ways damage shows

How does a physical injury become a change in thinking or mood? The link runs through four basic patterns of nervous-system disturbance. Treat them as the bridge from the lesion to the four domains above, not as an end in themselves. Most real patients show a mix at once.

  1. Destructive or deficiency phenomena — tissue is killed or blocked, so a function is lost (e.g. loss of speech after a stroke → the cognitive and emotional toll of not being understood). From infarction (tissue death from lost blood supply), a tumour or trauma.
  2. Irritative phenomena — nerve tissue fires too much (seizures, pins-and-needles) from pathologic depolarisation (nerve cells wrongly switching on in bursts) → fear and unpredictability for the person.
  3. Release phenomena — when higher control is lost, lower systems run unchecked: over-brisk reflexes, spasticity (stiff, tight muscles), and the uncontrolled crying or laughing that embarrasses stroke survivors. This is how a lesion directly produces an emotional symptom.
  4. Compensation phenomena — the person adapts, well or badly. Psychologically this is where coping, effort and motivation enter: a good adaptation (a new way to walk, a memory notebook) or a harmful one (giving up, or a joint stiffening into a contracture (a stuck, shortened joint)).

3. The mind–body interaction — the theme of the whole unit

This unit is built on a two-way street that will reappear in every topic:

  • Body → mind. The lesion changes the brain, so the mind changes: a frontal injury blunts personality, a release phenomenon produces sudden tears, a dopamine shortage slows thinking. The psychological symptom is a real, brain-based consequence, not weakness of will.
  • Mind → body. The person's mood, coping style, motivation, beliefs, family support and the stigma around them feed back into the illness: they decide whether the patient practises their exercises, takes their medicine, keeps appointments and stays engaged — and so they shape the speed and ceiling of recovery. Depression after a brain injury, for example, slows rehabilitation even when the lesion is unchanged.

Because of this loop, the clinical psychologist is not an optional extra: by lifting mood, teaching coping, supporting the family and reducing stigma, psychological work changes the physical and functional outcome. Keep this interaction explicit in every disease answer.

4. What decides the picture you will see

  • Focal vs diffuse damage. Focal (one small spot) damage gives specific losses tied to that spot's job (for example, loss of speech). Diffuse (spread all over) damage gives general slowing — poorer concentration, mental speed and overall efficiency, as seen after a severe closed-head injury. [Kolb & Whishaw]
  • Acute vs progressive course. An acute (sudden) event such as a stroke causes a sharp loss that may then partly recover. A progressive (slowly worsening) disease such as Parkinson's or Alzheimer's keeps getting worse over months and years.
  • Localisation. Different jobs sit in different places: a dead area in a language zone harms speech, while the same-size area elsewhere may harm movement or vision. The site of damage, not just its size, predicts the symptoms.
  • Left vs right hemisphere. For most people the left half of the brain leads language (speaking, understanding, reading, writing) and skilled, step-by-step action; the right half leads spatial awareness (sense of space and the left side of the world), attention to the whole scene, and emotional tone. This is why a left-brain stroke tends to cause speech loss, while a right-brain stroke tends to cause neglect of one side and poor spatial judgement. [Kolb & Whishaw]

5. Medical background in brief

Just enough anatomy to follow the psychology. The central nervous system (CNS) (the brain and the spinal cord together — the body's command centre) takes in sensory information and responds to it: the spinal cord carries signals up and down and runs simple reflexes (automatic quick responses), while the brain coordinates the body and is the seat of thinking, feeling and learning. Because nerve tissue is soft, it is protected by bone (the cranium (skull) and the vertebrae (spine bones)), bathed in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (a clear fluid that cushions it), and chemically guarded by the blood–brain barrier (BBB) (a tight filter in its tiny vessels). It is built of grey matter (nerve-cell bodies) and white matter (myelin-coated wiring), and its largest part is the cerebrum (the big wrinkled part, with the thinking surface called the cerebral cortex). The one point to carry forward: because each region has its own job, damage anywhere in the CNS shows up as a real disturbance of thinking and behaviour. [Kolb & Whishaw]

Supplement: how to tell the four manifestation patterns apart in an exam

When a question asks how nervous-system damage shows itself, sort the signs into the four patterns and give one example of each:

  • Destructive / deficiency — ask "is a function missing?" Tissue is killed or blocked, so something is lost (weakness, loss of speech after a stroke).
  • Irritative — ask "is tissue firing too much?" Over-firing gives extra activity (a seizure, pins-and-needles).
  • Release — ask "has a brake been removed?" When higher control is lost, a lower system runs unchecked (over-brisk reflexes, spasticity, the reappearance of baby-like reflexes).
  • Compensation — ask "is the body adapting?" The person works around the loss, helpfully (a high-stepping walk for a dropped foot) or unhelpfully (a joint stiffening into a contracture (a stuck, shortened joint)).

Quick test: two questions sort almost everything — "is a function lost or extra?" separates destructive from irritative, and "is a brake released or is the body adapting?" separates release from compensation. Remember most patients show a mix, so name more than one.

2 Cerebrovascular disorders: stroke & vascular dementia

For the clinical psychologist, a stroke is not mainly a blood-vessel event — it is a sudden, frightening crisis of the mind and the self. In one moment a person may lose their words, half their world, their independence and their old personality, and must then grieve and rebuild. The physical lesion (a blocked or burst vessel starving brain tissue) matters because it explains which psychological changes appear, but the heart of this topic is how the person thinks, feels, copes and relates afterwards — and how that, in turn, shapes their recovery. When many small strokes accumulate, the resulting thinking-and-mood decline is vascular dementia.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Frame stroke as a psychological crisis — a sudden, frightening loss that reshapes thinking, mood, identity and family life.
  2. Give the four domains as lived experience — cognitive, personality, behavioral and emotional disturbances after stroke (aphasia, neglect, post-stroke depression, emotional lability, pseudobulbar affect, catastrophic reaction).
  3. Describe the adjustment — grief, loss of role and independence, and how coping style shapes the outcome.
  4. Make the mind–body interaction explicit — the lesion produces the mood change, and mood/coping/family feed back into recovery.
  5. Add vascular dementia — the step-by-step decline and its mood and attention changes.
  6. Finish with psychological rehabilitation — psychoeducation, mood and coping work, CIMT, gait retraining, VR/mirror/imagery, speech therapy for aphasia.
  7. Keep the medical background brief — the WHO groups, ischaemic vs haemorrhagic, and by-artery/by-side mapping, as supporting context.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition A stroke (also called a cerebral vascular accident, CVA (an "accident" in the brain's blood vessels)) is "the sudden appearance of neurological symptoms as a result of interruption of blood flow." It often produces an infarct (an area of dead or dying tissue) where blood supply was cut off. [Kolb & Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

classification The WHO (1970) grouped cerebrovascular disease partly by the pathological change in the brain (subarachnoid haemorrhage, intracerebral haemorrhage, and cerebral ischaemic necrosis — which includes vascular dementia) and partly by the clinical stage (including the transient ischaemic attack). [World Health Organization, 1970]

Detailed notes

1. The four disturbance domains after stroke — as lived experience

A stroke happens in an instant and the person must live its consequences for years. These are the four domains that a clinical psychologist assesses and treats, described as the person actually experiences them:

  • Cognitive. Aphasia (loss of the ability to speak or understand words) can be the cruellest loss — an intelligent adult trapped, unable to say their own name or follow a conversation. Add memory, attention and executive (planning) problems and the half-vision loss of hemianopia (loss of half the field of sight), and everyday life becomes effortful and confusing.
  • Personality. Inattention and lasting personality change — becoming apathetic, blunted or disinhibited, so the family feels the person has changed. A right-hemisphere stroke can cause anosognosia (not realising one is ill or paralysed), which looks like unconcern but is a brain-based unawareness, and it makes the person hard to engage in rehab.
  • Behavioral. Apraxia (losing the practised know-how for actions like dressing or using tools) and loss of ADL (activities of daily living — washing, dressing, feeding, toileting), so a once-independent adult must now be helped with intimate daily tasks — a deep blow to dignity.
  • Emotional. Post-stroke depression is very common and is not merely "understandable sadness" — it has a brain-based component and it directly slows recovery. There is also emotional lability (moods swinging quickly and uncontrollably), the related pseudobulbar affect (sudden crying or laughing that does not match the feeling) which embarrasses the person in public, and the catastrophic reaction (a sudden burst of distress, tears or anger when faced with a task the person can no longer do). [Robinson & Price on post-stroke mood disorder]

2. Adjusting to a stroke — the patient's experience of illness

Beyond the specific symptoms sits the whole task of adjustment. A stroke usually arrives without warning and can turn an independent breadwinner into someone who needs help to stand. People commonly go through grief for the lost body and lost way of life, fear of another stroke, a threatened sense of identity and role (as spouse, parent, worker), and a loss of control. How well a person adjusts depends less on the size of the lesion than on their coping style, pre-stroke personality, mood and the support around them. Psychoeducation, realistic hope ("setbacks are part of recovery") and active coping protect against despair; isolation and untreated depression feed it.

3. The mind–body interaction in stroke

  • Body → mind. The lesion itself produces psychological symptoms: a frontal/subcortical stroke yields post-stroke depression and apathy; a release phenomenon drives the uncontrollable crying of pseudobulbar affect; a right-hemisphere lesion produces neglect and anosognosia. These are consequences of the damage, not weakness of character.
  • Mind → body. Depression, poor motivation and family strain reduce how much the person practises their exercises, keeps appointments and takes blood-pressure medicine — which worsens physical recovery and raises the risk of another stroke. Treating mood and engaging the family therefore improves motor and functional outcomes, not just happiness. This is why mood screening and coping support belong at the centre of stroke care.

4. Vascular dementia

Vascular dementia (a lasting decline in thinking caused by many strokes or poor brain blood supply) is part of the WHO "cerebral ischaemic necrosis" group. Its hallmark is a step-wise decline: the person is stable, then drops suddenly with each new small stroke, then is stable again — a staircase going down, rather than the smooth slow slide of Alzheimer's disease. Early signs often include slowed thinking, poor attention, and executive problems, with mood changes. The Hachinski Ischemic Score (a simple points checklist) is used to judge how likely a dementia is vascular (abrupt start, step-wise course, history of strokes and high blood pressure) rather than degenerative. Vascular dementia carries a particularly heavy load of depression and apathy, so mood is always part of the picture. [Hachinski Ischemic Score]

5. Psychological intervention and rehabilitation

Rehabilitation should start as early as possible, continue at home, and treat the whole person — mood and motivation as much as the arm and the leg, because (as the mind–body loop shows) the two determine each other. The psychologist leads on the first two points below and supports the rest.

  • Psychoeducation and coping support. Teaching the patient and the family about the stroke so they understand and cope with it; the person's own strengths, resources and coping skills are reinforced, and they learn that setbacks are part of recovery. This reduces fear and helplessness and keeps the person engaged in the harder physical work.
  • Treating mood. Screening for and treating post-stroke depression and anxiety (supportive therapy and CBT, see Topic 9) is central, because untreated low mood is one of the biggest brakes on physical recovery.
  • Constraint-Induced Movement Therapy (CIMT) (tying up the good arm so the weak arm is forced to work). Developed as a clinical treatment by Taub & Uswatte and Wolf and colleagues, it has two parts: restraining the less-affected arm, and shaping — practising skilled tasks set just at the patient's ability, raising difficulty as they improve, with plenty of feedback and encouragement (a motivational, psychological element).
  • Constraint-Induced Movement Therapy (CIMT) (tying up the good arm so the weak arm is forced to work). Developed as a clinical treatment by Taub & Uswatte and Wolf and colleagues, it has two parts: restraining the less-affected arm, and shaping — practising skilled tasks set just at the patient's ability, raising difficulty as they improve, with plenty of feedback and encouragement.
  • Gait (walking) retraining. Most survivors want to walk again. Leg strengthening alone may not improve walking speed in those who can already walk a little. Body-weight-support treadmill training (BWSTT) (walking on a treadmill in a harness that holds part of your weight) allows lots of repeated practice of a more normal walking pattern.
  • Virtual reality, mirror therapy and mental imagery. Virtual reality (VR) (computer-made practice environments) gives many engaging repetitions. In mirror therapy (MT) (a mirror makes the moving good limb look like the weak limb moving) the brain is "tricked" into movement practice; first used for phantom-limb pain, now used for one-sided weakness. Mental imagery / mental practice (rehearsing a movement in the mind without doing it), guided by a therapist, can improve dexterity.
  • Speech therapy for aphasia. Impairment-based therapy directly stimulates listening, speaking, reading and writing. Communication-based (also called consequence-based) therapy aims to get the message across by any means and trains caregivers to support communication in real-life situations.
Supplement: the main types of aphasia after a left-hemisphere stroke

Aphasia (loss of the ability to use words) comes in different patterns depending on exactly where the damage is. Knowing the common types makes the "cognitive" domain answer richer:

  • Broca's (expressive) aphasia (damage toward the front) — understanding is fairly good, but speech is slow and effortful, with short, telegram-like sentences. The person usually knows what they want to say but cannot get it out.
  • Wernicke's (receptive) aphasia (damage further back) — speech is fluent but makes little sense, with wrong or made-up words, and understanding is poor. The person often does not realise their speech is muddled.
  • Global aphasia (large stroke affecting both areas) — both speaking and understanding are severely lost.
  • Anomic aphasia (mild, word-finding problem) — speech and understanding are mostly fine, but the person keeps getting "stuck" searching for names of things.

Matching the therapy to the pattern matters: Broca's work focuses on getting words out, Wernicke's work on checking understanding, and anomic work on word-finding strategies.

6. Medical background in brief

Just enough to ground the psychology. A stroke's symptoms appear suddenly — numbness down one side, confusion, trouble talking, vision or balance problems. The WHO (1970) grouped cerebrovascular disease by (I) the pathological change — subarachnoid haemorrhage (bleed around the brain), intracerebral haemorrhage (bleed into brain tissue), and cerebral ischaemic necrosis (tissue dying from lost blood supply — includes vascular dementia) — and by (II) the clinical stage, including the transient ischaemic attack (TIA) (a brief "mini-stroke" under 24 hours that warns of a future one). By mechanism, stroke is either ischaemic (a blockage from thrombosis (a clot forming in place) or embolism (a travelling plug, often to the left middle cerebral artery)) or haemorrhagic (a burst vessel, usually from hypertension (high blood pressure)). [WHO, 1970; Kolb & Whishaw]

3 Developmental disorder: cerebral palsy

Cerebral palsy (CP) is often described as a movement disorder, but for a clinical psychologist it is about a child growing up with a visible disability — and how that shapes their thinking, self-image, friendships, schooling and the whole family's life. The underlying fact (fixed, non-progressive damage to the immature brain around birth) is important background, but the real questions are psychological: does this child have intelligence trapped behind a movement problem? How is their confidence and social participation? How is the family coping? Those questions lead this topic; the medical detail supports them.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Frame CP as a lifelong developmental challenge — a child grows up with a visible disability that shapes thinking, self-image, schooling, friendships and the whole family.
  2. Give the four domains and self-image — cognitive, personality and emotional, behavioral — with the key point that normal intelligence is often trapped behind a movement problem.
  3. Describe child and family adjustment — frustration, self-esteem, social participation, and the parents' and siblings' experience.
  4. Make the mind–body interaction explicit — the early lesion limits movement, and confidence, motivation and family support then shape participation and development.
  5. End with psychological rehabilitation — a team approach: OT, PT (incl. aqua and hippotherapy), speech therapy, assistive equipment, biofeedback, spasticity management, and support for the child and family.
  6. Keep the medical background brief — the types of CP by brain area, and the primary/secondary/tertiary effects.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition Cerebral palsy can be defined as "a central motor dysfunction affecting muscle tone, posture and movement resulting from a permanent, non-progressive defect or lesion of the immature brain." It is an umbrella term for a group of non-progressive, non-contagious motor conditions; the vast majority of cases are congenital (present at or around the time of birth). [standard definition of cerebral palsy]

Detailed notes

1. The four disturbance domains and self-image

Cerebral palsy is present from the start of life, so its deepest effects are not just on muscles but on how the child thinks, feels about themselves, and takes part in the world. The single most important point for a clinical psychologist: many children with CP have normal intelligence trapped behind a movement problem, so never read low ability from the way a child moves or speaks.

  • Cognitive. Some children have attention-span problems, vision problems (astigmatism (blurred vision), strabismus (squint), "lazy eye"), hearing loss, speech-and-language difficulty, learning difficulty and, where present, intellectual impairment, often with seizures/epilepsy alongside. But a large group have fully intact thinking — which makes being misjudged as "slow" especially painful.
  • Personality / Emotional. Repeated struggle with movement and communication can breed frustration, low self-esteem and difficulties in social and emotional adjustment. A visibly different child may be teased, left out of play, or over-protected, all of which shape self-image. These are understandable reactions to living with a visible disability, not a fixed "CP personality".
  • Behavioral. Limits in ADL (activities of daily living) and in motor control affect dressing, feeding, play and communication, so the child needs help or adapted methods — which can slow the normal push toward independence that every child needs.

2. Child and family adjustment

CP is a family condition. Parents often move through shock, grief and guilt before reaching acceptance, and they carry a long-term caregiving and financial load; siblings may feel overlooked. The child's tasks of childhood — making friends, succeeding at school, building independence and a sense of worth — all have to be met while managing a disability and other people's reactions to it. Good psychological outcomes depend on building the child's confidence, supporting inclusion at school and in play, and helping the family cope, grieve and advocate. Social participation, not just walking, is the real goal.

3. The mind–body interaction in cerebral palsy

  • Body → mind. The early, fixed lesion limits movement and sometimes thinking, which can restrict a child's exploration, learning and social contact at the very age when these build the mind — so a motor problem can have knock-on effects on confidence and development.
  • Mind → body. A child's motivation, confidence and enjoyment, and the family's expectations and support, strongly shape how much the child practises, moves and takes part — which in turn improves function and prevents avoidable complications (like contractures from not moving). Encouragement, play-based therapy and inclusion therefore have real physical pay-offs; discouragement and over-protection do the reverse.

4. Assessment and screening tools

Early developmental screening helps catch delays. Common tools:

  • Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) (a parent-filled checklist of a child's skills by age).
  • BRIGANCE Screens (quick screens of developmental skills).
  • Child Development Inventories (parent-report inventories such as the Denver II and the Minnesota Child Development Inventory).
  • Parents' Evaluation of Developmental Status (PEDS) (uses parents' concerns to flag problems).
  • Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (M-CHAT) (a short screen for autism risk).

These are useful but may miss subtle problems or milder cases, so they are best used routinely and alongside clinical judgement.

5. Psychological & team rehabilitation for cerebral palsy

Rehabilitation is a team effort aimed at function, participation and quality of life. The psychologist supports the child's confidence, mood and behaviour and works with the family throughout, while the medical and therapy team addresses movement.

  • Neurosurgical procedures (to reduce spasticity). By site: segmental (acting on the spinal-cord circuit that keeps muscle tone) and suprasegmental (acting on higher control centres — nuclei or tracts). By nature: ablative / neurolytic (making a permanent cut or lesion, e.g. rhizotomy, thalamotomy) and non-ablative (reversible stimulation, e.g. spinal cord stimulation).
  • Occupational therapy (OT). Improves fine motor skills (hand and arm movements for dressing, grooming, eating) and the visuo-motor and visuo-perceptual skills (hand–eye and seeing-and-understanding skills needed for reading and writing).
  • Physical therapy (PT). Builds gross motor skills — balance, coordination and strength for walking. It includes:
    • Aqua therapy (exercise in water): water's buoyancy supports the body so movement is safe and gentle on joints; warm water also relaxes muscles.
    • Hippotherapy (therapy using a horse's movement): the rhythm and sway of a walking horse drives the rider's muscles and balance, improving physical and cognitive function (different from ordinary horse-riding lessons).
  • Speech and language therapy. Assesses speech and swallowing (moving food safely from mouth to stomach); if speaking is hard, augmentative communication (back-up methods such as sign language, picture boards or voice-output devices) is used.
  • Adaptive equipment and assistive technology. A team fits equipment that manages tone and supports independence: adaptive bath/toilet chairs; orthoses (braces), casts and splints to improve alignment and reduce contractures; and specialised beds, wheelchairs and walkers.
  • Biofeedback. Biofeedback (giving the body's hidden signals back to the person as sound or light so they can learn to control them) has been used for abnormal movements — e.g. sensors in a helmet give a sound and lights when head posture drifts, teaching the child to hold the head steady.
  • Management of spasticity. Starts with clear, reachable goals (improve function, prevent complications, relieve pain): positioning and seating (a balanced, symmetrical, stable, comfortable posture is the single most important step); splinting and casting (prevents contractures; serial casting slowly stretches a stiff joint); and oral medication (limited use because of side effects like drowsiness and weakness).
  • Neurosurgical procedures (to reduce spasticity). By site: segmental (acting on the spinal-cord circuit for muscle tone) and suprasegmental (acting on higher control centres). By nature: ablative / neurolytic (a permanent cut or lesion, e.g. rhizotomy) and non-ablative (reversible, e.g. spinal cord stimulation).

6. Medical background in brief

CP is classified by where the immature brain was injured and the kind of muscle-tone problem it causes: spastic (cortex → stiff muscles (spasticity), sub-typed by limbs — diplegia, hemiplegia, quadriplegia); athetoid/dyskinetic (basal ganglia (deep movement-smoothing clusters) → writhing involuntary movements); dystonic (basal ganglia → fluctuating tone (tone that keeps changing)); ataxic (cerebellum (the balance centre) → poor coordination); and mixed. The effects are grouped as primary (directly from the injury: abnormal tone, poor motor control), secondary (build up over time: poor muscle growth, malformed bones/joints), and tertiary (the child's coping responses (the ways they adjust, e.g. in walking)).

Supplement: telling the types of cerebral palsy apart by what you see

Because each type comes from a different injured area, each produces a movement picture you can learn to recognise at the bedside:

  • Spastic (cortex) — muscles look and feel stiff; limbs are held tight and resist being moved, and movements are jerky. This is the most common pattern, named further by which limbs are involved (diplegia, hemiplegia, quadriplegia).
  • Athetoid / dyskinetic (basal ganglia) — slow, writhing, involuntary movements that the child cannot stop; they get worse with effort or excitement and disturb speech, feeding and grasp.
  • Dystonic (basal ganglia) — muscle tone keeps changing, often floppy at rest but suddenly tightening into twisted postures when the child moves or feels emotion.
  • Ataxic (cerebellum) — shaky, poorly-coordinated movement with a wide, unsteady walk and trouble with precise actions, because balance and timing are off.
  • Mixed — a combination (commonly spastic plus athetoid) when more than one area is injured.

Quick cue: stiff → spastic; writhing → athetoid; changing tone → dystonic; wobbly/unbalanced → ataxic. Remember a child may have normal intelligence trapped behind the movement problem, so never assume low ability from the way they move.

  • Adjustment is a family affair: parents' grief and caregiving load, the child's schooling, friendships, independence and self-worth.
  • Mind–body: the early lesion limits movement and exploration; confidence, motivation and family support then shape participation and prevent complications.

4 Degenerative diseases: Parkinson's, with Alzheimer's & Huntington's

Degenerative diseases such as Parkinson's (our main example, with Alzheimer's and Huntington's for contrast) are usually pictured through their movements, but their heaviest toll is psychological: slowed thinking, apathy, personality change, depression, and the lifelong task of living with inevitable, worsening decline. The biology (a slow loss of brain cells, progressive and mostly irreversible) explains the symptoms, but this topic leads with what the person and family actually experience — the shared cluster of cognitive, mood, psychotic, sleep, fatigue and behavioural changes — and how coping shapes quality of life.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Frame the psychological burden — a slow, progressive disease steadily changes thinking, mood, personality and behaviour, and forces the person to live with inevitable decline.
  2. Give the four domains in Parkinson's — cognitive (bradyphrenia, executive), personality (rigidity, apathy, impulse-control change), behavioral (gambling, punding, wandering), emotional (depression, anxiety, psychosis).
  3. Describe living with a progressive disease — anticipatory grief, loss of control, caregiver burden, demoralisation.
  4. Make the mind–body interaction explicit — the dopamine loss drives the mood/cognitive change, and mood, stress and the medication itself feed back into symptoms and behaviour.
  5. Contrast Alzheimer's and Huntington's in a short table (cortical vs subcortical dementia).
  6. End with psychological management — cognitive rehab, mood/behaviour work, family support, alongside drugs, exercise/gait and OT.
  7. Keep the medical background brief — the shared neurodegenerative profile and the dopamine/substantia-nigra mechanism with the four cardinal motor signs.

Detailed notes

1. The four disturbance domains in Parkinson's — as lived experience

Parkinson's is usually pictured as a tremor, but for the person and family the mind changes are often more disabling than the shaking. These are the four domains a clinical psychologist tracks:

  • Cognitive. Bradyphrenia (slowed thinking, the mental twin of slowed movement) makes conversation and decisions effortful; add executive dysfunction (trouble planning and switching), visuospatial problems, and in later stages a dementia. The person often knows they are slower, which is distressing in itself.
  • Personality. A drift toward rigidity (mental inflexibility) and apathy (loss of drive and interest) that families mistake for laziness or sulking; some people develop impulse-control changes — importantly, these are often caused by the dopamine-boosting medication, not moral failing.
  • Behavioral. The neuro-behavioural cluster — pathological gambling and shopping, punding (repeating a pointless activity over and over, like endlessly sorting objects), hypersexuality, and purposeless wandering — which can wreck finances and relationships and needs calm, non-blaming management.
  • Emotional. Depression and anxiety are very common (partly brain-based, partly a reaction to decline), along with psychosis (sometimes drug-related), sleep disorders and fatigue — one of the most disabling non-movement symptoms.

2. Living with a progressive disease — the experience of illness

Unlike a stroke, a degenerative disease offers no "recovery" to hope for, so the psychological task is living well with inevitable, slow decline. People face anticipatory grief (mourning losses before they fully arrive), a shrinking sense of control and independence, fear of becoming a burden, and often demoralisation. The caregiver carries an equally heavy load that grows over years. How the person and family cope — whether they stay active, connected and supported, or withdraw — strongly shapes quality of life even as the disease advances. This is where psychological care earns its place.

3. The mind–body interaction in Parkinson's

  • Body → mind. The loss of dopamine does not only slow movement; the same chemistry and circuitry slow thinking (bradyphrenia), flatten drive (apathy) and lower mood, and the treatment (dopamine agonists) can itself trigger impulse-control behaviour. The psychological symptoms are brain-based, not weakness.
  • Mind → body. Stress and low mood worsen tremor and make movement harder; depression and apathy sap the motivation needed for the exercise and gait practice that keep the body mobile. So treating mood, reducing stress and supporting engagement produce real motor and functional gains, and reviewing medication can resolve behavioural symptoms. The loop runs both ways.

5. Psychological management (with medical support)

Because these diseases cannot be cured, management aims to protect function, mood and quality of life for as long as possible, with the psychologist leading on cognition, mood, behaviour and family support. Assessment tools used for degenerative (and infectious) brain disease include: PET (positron emission tomography — a scan of brain activity); SPECT (single-photon emission computed tomography — a blood-flow scan); MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination — a quick 30-point memory-and-orientation test); 3MSE (Modified Mini-Mental State — a longer, more sensitive version); Hachinski Ischemic Score (tells vascular from degenerative dementia); Clock Drawing Test; Blessed Information-Memory-Concentration (a short memory and concentration scale); ADL (activities-of-daily-living) and the Blessed Functional Assessment Scale; Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) (a depression screen for older adults); and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory-Q (NPI-Q) (a carer-report checklist of behaviour and mood symptoms).

Management (symptoms vary with onset and course, so treatment is tailored):

  • Pharmacological. Most degenerative conditions are irreversible and the medicines slow decline rather than cure. For dementia and movement symptoms: cholinesterase inhibitors (boost a memory chemical in Alzheimer's), memantine (protects cells from over-excitation in dementia), dopamine agonists and levodopa (top up dopamine in Parkinson's), MAO-B inhibitors (slow dopamine breakdown) and anticholinergics (help tremor). Extra drugs treat the comorbid depression, anxiety, over-activity and sleep problems.
  • Cognitive rehabilitation. An individual programme of skills training plus metacognitive strategies (teaching the person to watch and correct their own thinking), in two approaches: the remedial (restorative) approach — drill and practice, verbal and visual mnemonics, the link method (joining items into a chain of images), the visual story method (turning a list into a vivid story); and the compensatory approach — changing the environment and using devices (alarm watches, diaries, voice recorders) to work around the deficit.
  • Physical exercise and gait training. Regular exercise maintains mobility, flexibility, strength, walking speed and quality of life; gentle rocking, slow rotations, rhythmic initiation, diaphragmatic breathing and meditation ease rigidity. Parkinson's gait problems (hypokinesia (slow, small movement), shuffling, reduced arm swing) are tackled with assistive equipment (pole and treadmill walking), verbal/visual/auditory cueing, and varied practice surfaces.
  • Occupational therapy (OT). Helps with daily living — tremor-management strategies, feeding skills, coordination exercises, and home-safety and equipment advice — tailored to the person's goals.

6. Medical background in brief

Degenerative diseases are marked by slow loss of neurons and secondary gliosis (scarring by support cells), without major inflammation — a progressive family that includes Alzheimer's, Parkinson's, Huntington's and AIDS dementia complex (Topic 7). Almost all share one profile that explains the domains above: cognitive decline (speed, attention, memory, executive, visuospatial), mood disorders, psychosis, sleep disorders, fatigue, and neuro-behavioural change (gambling, hypersexuality, wandering). Parkinson's disease specifically comes from loss of dopamine (a brain chemical for smooth movement)-making cells in the substantia nigra (a dark area in the basal ganglia), giving the four cardinal motor signs — resting tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia (slowness) and postural instability (poor balance). Medication (levodopa, dopamine agonists, MAO-B inhibitors, and for Alzheimer's the cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine) slows decline rather than cures. [Kolb & Whishaw]

Supplement: why functional (ADL) assessment matters as much as memory testing

Memory scores like the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination) tell you how damaged thinking is, but they do not tell you whether the person can still live safely. That is why degenerative-disease work-ups add a functional layer:

  • ADL — activities of daily living (the basic self-care tasks: washing, dressing, feeding, toileting, moving about). Losing these means the person needs hands-on care.
  • Blessed Functional Assessment Scale (a carer-rated scale of everyday performance) — it tracks how well the person still manages real-life tasks (handling money, finding their way, remembering recent events, dressing, feeding) and so measures the practical impact of the disease over time.
  • Blessed Information-Memory-Concentration (a short direct test of memory and concentration) — this pairs with the functional scale so you capture both what the person knows and what they can do.

Putting a cognitive score next to a functional score gives a fuller picture, guides how much support the family needs, and lets you measure change at the next review.

5 Trauma: traumatic brain injury (TBI) & spinal cord injury (SCI)

A physical injury from an outside force can change a person in ways that have little to do with the visible wound. In traumatic brain injury (TBI) (sudden damage to the brain from an outside force) it is the personality, behaviour and thinking changes — the sense that "the person came back different" — that most burden patients and families. In spinal cord injury (SCI) (damage to the cord that cuts signals below the injury) the paralysis is only the start; the real work is the grief, identity and emotional adjustment that follow. This topic leads with those psychological changes and the mind–body loop; how the injuries happen and how severity is scored are kept as brief background.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Say why the mind matters most — in TBI it is the personality, behaviour and thinking changes, not the physical ones, that most burden patients and families; in SCI it is the emotional adjustment.
  2. Give the four domains in TBI — cognitive (speed, memory, executive), personality (frontal change), behavioral (disinhibition, temper), emotional (depression, irritability) — explained as the "changed self".
  3. Describe SCI's grief and identity work — level and completeness, sudden dependence, body-image and grief.
  4. Make the mind–body interaction explicit — the frontal/temporal lesion changes personality, and mood, insight, coping and family then shape rehabilitation.
  5. End with psychological management and rehab — mood/pain CBT, attention and memory aids, behaviour management, family support, speech-swallowing.
  6. Keep the medical background brief — open vs closed injury, coup/countercoup/DAI, and the GCS/grades/PTA severity measures.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition In a closed-head injury, "there is damage at the site of the blow, a bruise (contusion) called a coup … the pressure that produces the coup may push the brain against the opposite side of the skull, producing an additional bruise, known as a countercoup." Twisting and shearing of fibres, hematoma and edema add further, often diffuse, damage. [Kolb & Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

concept Diffuse axonal injury (DAI) — widespread microscopic stretching and swelling of axons from the primary injury itself (not from later pressure). First named by Adams and earlier described by Strich. [Adams; Strich]

Detailed notes

1. The four disturbance domains in TBI — the changed self

Because closed-head injury hits the frontal and temporal lobes hardest, the picture centres on personality and self-control — and it is these changes, far more than any physical scar, that most burden patients and families, who often say "the body came back, but the person didn't". Painfully, bright, insightful people are often the most distressed, because they notice every drop in their own ability.

  • Cognitive. Reduced mental speed, poor concentration, memory problems, and executive difficulties (planning, organising, problem-solving) — the hallmark of diffuse damage.
  • Personality. Frontal / executive personality change — the person's character shifts (for example becoming blunt, tactless, apathetic, or childishly impulsive), often while raw intelligence scores still look normal.
  • Behavioral. Disinhibition (acting without the usual brake), anger and temper outbursts, and changed interpersonal behaviour that strains relationships and work.
  • Emotional. Depression, irritability and general emotional change; many severely injured people do not return to study or work, or return at a lower level. [Kolb & Whishaw]

2. Spinal cord injury (SCI): grief, identity and adjustment

In SCI, the medical facts of level and completeness decide what moves and what does not — but the real work for the clinical psychologist is the emotional adjustment to a suddenly changed body and life:

  • Complete injury = no movement or sensation below the injury. If the arms are spared, the person has paraplegia (both legs paralysed); if the arms are involved too, tetraplegia (all four limbs paralysed).
  • Incomplete injury = some function remains several segments below; the person has paraparesis (partial leg weakness) or tetraparesis (partial weakness of all four limbs).
  • The level of injury is the lowest intact spinal segment — everything below it is affected.

The adjustment is the heart of SCI care. The injury brings sudden physical dependence (paraplegics need both arms for transfers and wheelchair control) and a cascade of psychological tasks: grief for the lost body and way of life, a shattered body-image and identity (as an athlete, worker, partner, parent), depression and sometimes suicidality in the early months, and a long rebuilding of self-worth and quality of life. People often move through shock, denial, anger and bargaining before reaching a workable acceptance, and there is no fixed timetable. Coping style and personality shape how well the person adjusts, and good executive function matters because a safe, independent life now depends on planning and self-management. The psychologist's job is to support this grief, protect against depression, and help the person build a new but genuinely liveable identity.

3. The mind–body interaction in TBI and SCI

  • Body → mind. In TBI the frontal/temporal lesion directly rewrites personality and self-control — disinhibition and temper are brain-based, not bad character — and often damages the person's insight into their own change, which makes them hard to engage. In SCI the sudden physical loss triggers grief and depression.
  • Mind → body. Depression, poor insight, anger and family breakdown all reduce engagement in rehabilitation, slowing physical recovery, while good mood, motivation and family support speed it; in SCI, adjustment and self-management decide whether the person avoids complications (pressure sores, infections) and builds a full life. So psychological work is not a luxury added after the physical rehab — it is part of what makes the physical rehab succeed.

4. Psychological management and rehabilitation of TBI and SCI

  • Manage associated injuries. Treat other injuries well, since recovery depends on the whole person. Impaired personality, memory, concentration and intellect can change the outcome; joint damage and contractures and peripheral-nerve injuries (e.g. the brachial plexus (the nerve bundle to the arm)) are especially disabling for wheelchair users.
  • Pain. Musculo-skeletal and neurogenic (nerve-generated) pain are common after SCI. CBT for pain teaches the link between body (pain, tension), thoughts, feelings and behaviour; it uses cognitive restructuring (replacing unhelpful thoughts with helpful ones), behavioural activation (exercise, pacing), better sleep and coping skills (relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing, biofeedback), and sometimes hypnosis. Painkillers are used carefully because of addiction risk.
  • Memory and attention.
    • Direct attention training (practising the actual task while resisting distractions), building from simple to complex — but trained on the real task the person needs, because gains do not transfer well. [Sohlberg et al., 2003]
    • External memory aids (notebooks, maps, digital reminders in the real setting) are supported by evidence; internal "memory-retraining" exercises are not recommended. Aids must be relevant and acceptable to the person. [Sohlberg et al., 2007]
    • Behaviour management for anger and social situations, using either antecedent (set up before — scripts, routines, an "escape" routine for rising temper) or consequence (responses after — reinforcing good behaviour) strategies. The trend favours antecedent methods, because punishment can frustrate a person who has impaired reasoning and control; supports should be gradually withdrawn to build independence. [Ylvisaker, Turkstra & Coelho, 2005]
    • Learning new skills by leaning on intact procedural memory (memory for how to do things, learned by repetition) rather than weak declarative memory (memory for facts and events) — so many repetitions, not one-off instructions. [Ehlhardt et al., 2008] Plus metacognitive / awareness training for complex tasks.
  • Speech-swallowing rehabilitation. Assess and treat per the problem: augmentative-alternative communication (AAC) (back-up ways to communicate) for word-finding problems; exercises for apraxia/dysarthria (trouble programming or moving speech muscles); and dysphagia (swallowing difficulty) techniques such as careful positioning, a chin-tuck or supraglottic swallow to protect the airway, adapted utensils, and modified food/liquid textures.
Supplement: the two kinds of amnesia and post-concussion syndrome after a head injury

After a head injury the memory gap sits on both sides of the moment of impact, and it is worth separating them clearly:

  • Retrograde amnesia (looking backward) — loss of memories from the period just before the injury. It often shrinks as the person recovers, with older memories returning first and the last few seconds before impact sometimes lost for good.
  • Post-traumatic amnesia (PTA) (looking forward) — the stretch after the injury during which the person cannot lay down new day-to-day memories, even if they seem awake and talking. Its length is one of the best simple guides to severity: under 10 minutes is very mild, 10–60 minutes mild, 1–24 hours moderate, 1–7 days severe, and over 7 days very severe.

Post-concussion syndrome (the cluster that can linger after even a "mild" knock to the head) includes headache, dizziness, tiredness, poor concentration, memory lapses, irritability and low mood. It can appear with a normal scan and a GCS of 13–15, which is exactly why a near-normal GCS does not mean "no injury" — the person may still struggle for weeks or months.

5. Medical background in brief

Head injury harms the brain by direct damage, cut blood supply, bleeding and swelling that raise pressure, infection, and scarring. There are two types: open-head injury (the skull is penetrated; losses are often specific, like a small surgical removal) and closed-head injury (a blow sets off the coup (bruise under the impact), the countercoup (rebound bruise on the far side), shearing that causes diffuse axonal injury (DAI) (widespread microscopic tearing of the wiring) in the frontal and temporal lobes — which is exactly why personality and self-control are hit — then haematoma (a clot pressing on the brain) and edema (swelling) that raise pressure). Separate primary injury (impact moment) from secondary injury (later bleeding, swelling, ischaemia). [Kolb & Whishaw]

Severity is measured by the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS, 1974) (a bedside score adding eye, verbal and motor responses) and by post-traumatic amnesia (PTA) (the gap after injury when new memories cannot form). The longer the coma or amnesia, the worse the outlook.

6 Convulsive disorder: epilepsy

Epilepsy is a tendency to repeated seizures (brief electrical "storms" in the brain), but the seizures occupy only minutes of a person's life. What a clinical psychologist cares about is the rest of the time: the fear of the next seizure, the stigma and hiding, the limits on driving, work and independence, the depression and anxiety, and the changes to thinking and sometimes personality between seizures. This topic leads with that lived, interictal experience and with how mood and stress feed back onto the seizures themselves; the seizure types and phases are kept as brief background.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Frame epilepsy as living with unpredictability — the seizures are brief, but the fear, stigma and limits colour the person's whole life.
  2. Give the four domains — cognitive, personality (the interictal temporal-lobe pattern, handled carefully), behavioral, emotional — as the person experiences them.
  3. Describe stigma, fear and quality of life — how unpredictability, shame and others' reactions shape mood and identity.
  4. Make the mind–body interaction explicit — the electrical storm produces the symptoms, and stress, mood, sleep and adherence feed back onto seizure frequency.
  5. End with psychotherapy and AEDs — CBT, relaxation, biofeedback, education, family counselling, with anti-epileptic drugs as the medical backbone.
  6. Keep the medical background brief — Hughlings Jackson's definition, seizure types, and the three phases.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition Epilepsy is "an occasional, an excessive and a disorderly discharge of nervous tissue," brought on by any process that pathologically raises the chance of depolarisation (nerve cells switching on) and synchronised firing of groups of neurons. [John Hughlings Jackson, 1889]

Detailed notes

1. The four disturbance domains in epilepsy — as lived experience

A seizure itself may last a minute, but epilepsy shapes a person's whole life between seizures, through thinking, mood, behaviour and sometimes personality. For the clinical psychologist the heart of the topic is this interictal life, not the convulsion:

  • Cognitive. Thinking differs across phases — poor during and after a seizure (ictal/post-ictal), and sometimes mildly affected between seizures (interictal (the periods between seizures)), touching memory and attention. Anti-epileptic drugs can also dull thinking, which must be balanced against seizure control.
  • Personality. In some people with long-standing temporal-lobe epilepsy, writers describe an interictal personality pattern: hypergraphia (an urge to write a great deal), hyperreligiosity (intense religious or philosophical concern), viscosity (sticky, hard-to-end conversation and social contact), circumstantiality (over-detailed, roundabout talk), and altered sexuality. This should be presented carefully as something reported in the literature in a minority, not as a rule for all patients. [Bear & Fedio, 1977; Blumer]
  • Behavioral. Automatisms (automatic movements such as lip-smacking, chewing, hand-waving) and other behaviour change around seizures.
  • Emotional. Depression and anxiety are common; a fear/panic aura can be frightening; psychosis can occur; and the unpredictability of seizures lowers quality of life.

The interictal personality pattern must be handled with special care: it is reported in a minority of people with long-standing temporal-lobe epilepsy and must never be used to stereotype patients. Presenting it respectfully is itself part of good clinical-psychology practice.

2. Stigma, fear and quality of life

The hardest part of epilepsy for many people is not the seizure but never knowing when the next one will come. This unpredictability breeds anticipatory anxiety, hyper-vigilance, and avoidance of ordinary activities (swimming, driving, being alone). On top of this sits heavy social stigma: in many communities epilepsy is misunderstood or feared, so people hide the diagnosis, are passed over for jobs or marriage, and feel shame. The result is a real risk of depression, low self-esteem and social withdrawal, and a narrowed life. A clinical psychologist's work therefore targets fear, shame, self-concept and participation as much as the seizures themselves.

3. The mind–body interaction in epilepsy

  • Body → mind. The electrical over-firing directly produces the symptoms — a fear aura, post-ictal confusion and low mood, interictal changes in thinking and (in a minority) personality. These are brain events, not character.
  • Mind → body. Stress, anxiety, poor sleep and missed medication can trigger or increase seizures, so the person's emotional state and adherence feed straight back onto the physical disease. This is exactly why relaxation, stress management, CBT and good psychoeducation (which improve adherence) can reduce seizure frequency, not just distress — a clear, practical example of the mind shaping the body.

4. Psychotherapy and medical management

  • Psychotherapeutic approaches (chosen and modified to the person's need) do the psychological work and can even lower seizures:
    • Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) — effective for the depression and anxiety that often accompany epilepsy, by changing thought patterns and behaviour.
    • Relaxation therapy — calming the mind by releasing muscle tension, to cut the stress and anxiety that can trigger seizures.
    • Behaviour modification — working with a therapist to live a full life without ignoring, or being ruled by, the epilepsy.
    • Biofeedback — galvanic skin response (GSR) biofeedback (feedback from the skin's sweat response) has reduced seizure frequency; EEG biofeedback improved cognitive and motor function with the greatest seizure reduction.
    • Educational interventions — improve knowledge of epilepsy, coping, medication adherence and social skills, and reduce stigma.
    • Family counselling — a useful add-on that helps the family respond calmly and supportively.
  • Anti-epileptic drugs (AEDs) — the medical backbone. Commonly used medicines include carbamazepine (Tegretol), clonazepam (Klonopin), ethosuximide (Zarontin), gabapentin (Neurontin), lamotrigine (Lamictal), levetiracetam (Keppra), oxcarbazepine (Trileptal), phenobarbital (Luminal), phenytoin (Dilantin), pregabalin (Lyrica), topiramate (Topamax), valproic acid (Depakene) and zonisamide (Zonegran). A key psychological point: AEDs can dull thinking or affect mood, so seizure control must be balanced against cognitive and emotional side effects.

5. Medical background in brief

Hughlings Jackson's 1889 words (above) capture the core: an electrical over-firing of brain cells. Causes include metabolic disorders or anything that damages cortical tissue — trauma, haemorrhage, ischemia (lost blood supply), anoxia (lost oxygen), infection or scar tissue. By type, seizures are primary generalized (the whole cortex at once — tonic-clonic ("grand mal") (stiffen then jerk, with loss of consciousness) and absence ("petit mal") (brief blank staring spells)) or focal/localization-related (one cluster fires, possibly spreading; temporal-lobe seizures often show automatisms (automatic lip-smacking or chewing)). [John Hughlings Jackson, 1889]

Every seizure runs in three phases: pre-ictal / aura (a warning — déjà vu, odd smells, a stomach feeling, and classically fear), ictal (the seizure itself — blacking out, confusion, stiffening and jerking), and post-ictal (recovery — confusion, low mood, headache, tiredness). Assessment uses EEG (brain-wave recording; the key test), blood tests and PET (activity scan), the Bender Gestalt and Luria-Nebraska for cognition, the WADA test (briefly sleeping one half of the brain before surgery), and serum prolactin (a hormone that rises just after a true seizure).

  • Four domains: cognitive (phase-linked + AED effects), personality (interictal temporal-lobe pattern — a minority, present carefully), behavioral (automatisms), emotional (depression, anxiety, fear aura).
  • Adjustment: the unpredictability breeds anxiety and avoidance; stigma brings shame, hiding and social exclusion, risking depression and withdrawal.
  • Mind–body: the electrical storm drives the symptoms; stress, anxiety, poor sleep and missed medication feed back and raise seizures — so psychological work can reduce seizure frequency.
  • Treat with CBT, relaxation, biofeedback, education, family counselling, AEDs as the backbone. Background: Hughlings Jackson 1889; generalized vs focal; pre-ictal/ictal/post-ictal.

7 Infectious disorder: AIDS dementia complex / HAND

In advanced HIV/AIDS, the virus can reach the brain and cause a slow decline in thinking, movement and behaviour — AIDS dementia complex (ADC), now broadly termed HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND). But for a clinical psychologist this condition is defined by a double burden: a subcortical brain change (slowed thinking, apathy, low mood) layered on top of one of the most stigmatised diagnoses in the world. The psychological and social weight — stigma, secrecy, disclosure, demoralisation — leads this topic, because it also decides whether the person stays in treatment; the biology is kept as brief background.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Frame the double burden — a brain disease (slowed thinking, apathy, low mood) layered on top of the stigma and fear that surround HIV/AIDS.
  2. Give the four domains as lived experience — cognitive (psychomotor slowing), personality (apathy, withdrawal), behavioral (low initiative), emotional (depression, anxiety, stigma distress).
  3. Describe stigma, disclosure and demoralisation — the social and emotional weight the person carries.
  4. Make the mind–body interaction explicit — the virus produces the cognitive/mood change, and stigma, mood and support shape disclosure, adherence and the course.
  5. End with psychological management — demystifying infection, stigma education, cognitive rehab and psychosocial support, with antiretroviral treatment as the medical backbone.
  6. Keep the medical background brief — the acronyms, how HIV reaches the CNS, and the HAND staging.

Detailed notes

1. The four disturbance domains — as lived experience

This disorder carries a double burden: a brain disease on top of one of the most stigmatised diagnoses in the world. The domains below describe the brain-based change; the next section describes the social and emotional weight that sits alongside it.

  • Cognitive. Psychomotor slowing (both thinking and movement become slow), poor attention and concentration, memory problems and executive difficulty, which can progress to a full dementia. Everyday tasks take longer and feel harder, which the person often notices and finds frightening.
  • Personality. Apathy (loss of drive and interest) and social withdrawal — the person pulls back from people and activities, which families can misread as not caring.
  • Behavioral. Reduced initiative, general motor slowing and gait (walking) changes, so everyday activity falls off.
  • Emotional. Depression and anxiety, and a specific, heavy load of stigma-related distress and demoralisation, because HIV/AIDS is so widely and wrongly shamed.

2. Stigma, disclosure and demoralisation

For most patients the social experience of HIV is as hard as the illness. People face agonising choices about disclosure — whom to tell, and the risk of rejection by family, partners, employers or community if they do. Many carry secrecy, shame and fear of contagion from others, and internalise the stigma as self-blame. On top of this come grief about the diagnosis and, often, demoralisation (a sense that nothing can be done and that one is helpless). This social-emotional load, not just the virus, drives much of the depression, anxiety and withdrawal seen in these patients, so the psychologist works directly on disclosure decisions, shame, support networks and hope.

3. The mind–body interaction in HAND

  • Body → mind. HIV in the brain causes a subcortical pattern of slowed thinking and movement, apathy and low mood — a direct, biological effect of the infection, not weakness or "giving up".
  • Mind → body. Stigma, depression and demoralisation make people hide the diagnosis, avoid care and skip their antiretroviral medication — and poor adherence lets the virus damage the brain and body further. By contrast, reducing stigma, lifting mood and supporting disclosure improve adherence, which slows or even reverses the brain effects. Here the psychological and social are literally life-and-death medical factors.

4. Psychological management

  • Demystifying the infection. The therapist openly explains how HIV is and is not transmitted, clearing up misconceptions and discussing spread and prevention. This reduces fear and shame in the patient, family and care team.
  • Education about stigma. Because HIV/AIDS is heavily stigmatised, the therapist educates family members and health workers about the real problems patients face, so the person is supported rather than shamed.
  • Mood, adjustment and adherence work. Supportive therapy and CBT for depression, anxiety and demoralisation, help with disclosure decisions, and motivational work to support medication adherence (which protects the brain).
  • Cognitive rehabilitation and psychosocial support. The remedial-vs-compensatory cognitive rehab from Topic 4, plus family work and group support to protect quality of life and reduce isolation.
  • Antiretroviral context. Treatment of the underlying HIV infection is the medical backbone that slows or improves the brain effects; psychological care works alongside it, and (via adherence) makes it work.
  • Assessment. Neuropsychological screening, the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination), and functional / ADL (activities-of-daily-living) assessment to judge real-world impact.

5. Medical background in brief

The names spelled out: HIV — Human Immunodeficiency Virus (the virus that weakens immune defences); AIDS — Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (the advanced stage); ADC — AIDS Dementia Complex (the dementia HIV causes in the brain), now grouped under HAND — HIV-Associated Neurocognitive Disorder (the spectrum of HIV-caused thinking problems); CNS — central nervous system (brain and spinal cord). HIV crosses into the CNS and damages it by direct infection and the neuro-inflammation (brain inflammation from the immune response) it provokes, producing a subcortical dementia (slowed thinking and movement, apathy, low mood) rather than the memory-and-language loss of a cortical dementia like Alzheimer's. HAND is staged along a spectrum: asymptomatic (test-detectable only) → mild neurocognitive disorder (real but mild everyday problems) → HIV-associated dementia (marked memory, motor and behaviour problems).

  • Adjustment: disclosure dilemmas, shame, secrecy and demoralisation drive much of the distress.

8 The clinical psychologist's assessment

Assessment is where the clinical psychologist's work begins. The aim is not to "find the lesion" — the scanner does that — but to build a psychological formulation: which thinking abilities, emotions and behaviours have changed, how those changes affect the person's real life and relationships, and what they mean for treatment. Neuropsychological assessment (carefully measuring thinking and behaviour to map brain function) moves from referral and history, through domain-by-domain testing, to interpreting the whole profile, and always includes mood, personality and quality of life alongside cognition.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Start with referral and history — why the person was sent, and what their life and baseline were like before.
  2. Test each cognitive domain — orientation/attention, memory, language, visuospatial, executive, processing speed, intelligence/premorbid estimate — saying what each test measures and why it matters for the person.
  3. Interpret the profile — the pattern of spared vs impaired functions, and what it suggests (which side, cortical vs subcortical).
  4. Assess mood, personality and quality of life — depression/anxiety scales, personality change, QoL, and functional/ADL impact.
  5. Name the batteries and screens and place the medical work-up as supporting context for the formulation.

Detailed notes

1. Referral, history and the process

Assessment begins with the referral question — why is this person here? (For diagnosis? To plan rehab? To judge capacity to return to work or live alone?) The answer shapes what you test. Next comes a careful history: the person's story, the onset and course of the problem, and — crucially — their premorbid baseline (their education, job, language, and usual personality before the illness), because a "normal" score can still mean a big loss for a previously high-functioning person. The clinician also listens to the family, who often notice changes the patient cannot see. The process then runs: a brief screen → a fuller battery covering each cognitive domain → assessment of mood, personality and quality of life → a functional/ADL check of real daily life. Testing is done with sensitivity, because being examined on failing abilities is stressful and can itself lower performance.

2. Domain-by-domain cognitive testing

A full assessment samples each thinking domain in turn. For each, the point is not the score alone but what it means for the person's functioning:

  • Orientation and attention — can the person hold and direct focus? Poor attention undermines everything else (and everyday safety), so it is checked first.
  • Memory — learning and recalling new information. This is what families worry about most, and it decides whether the person can live independently and keep appointments and medication.
  • Language — naming, understanding, speaking, reading and writing; aphasia (loss of language) cuts the person off from others and must be known before any talking therapy.
  • Visuospatial and constructional skill — seeing and judging space and copying shapes; losses here cause getting lost, dressing problems and left-side neglect (ignoring one side).
  • Executive function — planning, organising, switching and self-control; the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) (a rule-switching task) is sensitive to frontal-lobe problems. Executive loss is what most disrupts work, relationships and independent living.
  • Processing speed — how fast the person takes in and acts on information; slowing is an early, disabling sign in subcortical conditions (Parkinson's, HAND).
  • Intelligence and premorbid estimate — current ability, read against an estimate of the person's pre-illness level, so the drop can be judged, not just the absolute score.

3. Reading the profile

The real skill is interpreting the whole pattern, not one test. The psychologist asks: which functions are spared and which impaired, and does the pattern point anywhere?

  • Lateralising inference. Language and verbal memory loss with preserved spatial skill suggests a left-hemisphere problem; spatial, constructional and neglect problems with preserved language suggests a right-hemisphere one.
  • Cortical vs subcortical signature. A cortical picture (as in Alzheimer's) leads with memory and language loss; a subcortical picture (Parkinson's, Huntington's, HAND) leads with slowed processing, attention and executive problems and mood change. Spotting which signature fits guides diagnosis and rehab.
  • Spared strengths matter as much as deficits, because rehabilitation is built on what still works (e.g. using intact procedural memory to relearn skills).

4. Mood, personality and quality-of-life assessment

Cognition is only half the picture. Because mood and personality change are so common and so treatable, the psychologist always assesses them:

  • Depression and anxiety — using scales suited to the person, such as the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) (a depression screen for older adults); mood is screened in every CNS patient because it is common and it slows recovery.
  • Personality and behaviour change — often rated by a carer, e.g. the Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI / NPI-Q) (a carer checklist of behaviour and mood symptoms), which captures apathy, disinhibition, agitation and the like.
  • Quality of life and functional impact — the Barthel Index (independence in daily activities) and other ADL scales ask the practical question that matters most to patient and family: can the person manage daily life, and how can we improve it?

Honest note on the PGI-BBD

The PGI Battery of Brain Dysfunction (Pershad & Verma) is the recognised Indian neuropsychological battery for detecting brain dysfunction. It is described here at the level of its purpose and standing; exact subtest names and cut-off scores are not invented here, so that nothing is misreported.

Supplement: "fixed battery" versus "flexible battery" — two ways to test

Comprehensive neuropsychological testing comes in two styles, and it helps to know the difference:

  • Fixed (standard) battery — the same full set of tests is given to everyone, in the same way. The Halstead-Reitan Battery (a classic fixed battery) is the typical example. Strength: results are standardised and easy to compare across people and over time. Cost: it is long and may test things a particular patient does not need.
  • Flexible (process) battery — the clinician picks and adds tests as they go, following the patient's actual problems; the Luria-Nebraska (built on Luria's qualitative approach) tradition leans this way. Strength: efficient and tailored, and it looks at how the person fails, not just the score. Cost: it depends more on the examiner's skill and is harder to compare between people.

In practice many clinicians blend the two: a short screen first (such as the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination)), then a core set of tests, then a few extra tests chosen for that person's picture — with a functional / ADL check at the end so the report says what the person can actually do day to day.

9 Psychological intervention & rehabilitation

This is the heart of the unit: what the clinical psychologist actually does. Rehabilitation (helping a person regain abilities or learn to live well despite a lasting disability) is where the mind–body loop is put to work — because treating thinking, mood, behaviour, family and stigma changes not only how the person feels but their physical and functional recovery too. The psychologist is a core member of the multidisciplinary team, working from a biopsychosocial view (biological damage, psychological adjustment and social context all matter together), and the work starts early and treats the whole person.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Place the psychologist in the team — the biopsychosocial rationale and why psychological work changes physical outcome.
  2. Cognitive rehabilitation — remedial/restorative vs compensatory, with the named techniques (errorless/procedural learning, external aids, attention training).
  3. Behavioural methods — behaviour modification/ABA, Positive Behaviour Support, antecedent vs consequence management.
  4. Psychotherapy, grief and identity — CBT and supportive therapy for adjustment and depression, IPT, relaxation/biofeedback, and grief/self-concept and motivation/adherence work.
  5. Family, group and psychosocial rehab — caregiver psychoeducation and support, group therapy, stigma reduction, and psychosocial and vocational rehabilitation.

Detailed notes

1. The psychologist in the rehabilitation team

Recovery has three strands: the nervous system's own spontaneous recovery in the weeks after injury; therapeutic rehabilitation that trains lost skills; and helping the person learn to live with any lasting handicap. The clinical psychologist contributes to all three, and the biopsychosocial rationale explains why this matters:

  • Why psychological work changes physical outcome. A person's mood, motivation, beliefs, coping and family support decide how much they engage in the hard physical and cognitive practice that recovery needs. Treating depression, building motivation and supporting the family therefore raise the ceiling of physical and functional recovery — the mind–body loop in action.
  • Start early and continue at home once the person is stable.
  • Multidisciplinary team. Psychologists, physicians, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech-language therapists and social workers work together, because the problems cut across movement, thinking, mood, communication and social life. The psychologist's special contributions are assessment, cognitive rehabilitation, behaviour change, psychotherapy for adjustment, and family support.

2. Cognitive rehabilitation

Cognitive rehabilitation (a programme to restore thinking skills or work around the losses) uses individualised skills training plus metacognitive strategies (teaching the person to watch and correct their own thinking), in two broad approaches:

  • Remedial (restorative) — tries to rebuild the weakened ability through drill and practice, verbal and visual mnemonics, the link method (joining items into a chain of linked images) and the visual story method (turning a list into a vivid story). Attention process training (graded attention exercises) is used for attention, but gains transfer best when trained on the real task the person actually needs.
  • Compensatory — works around the deficit by changing the environment (home, school, workplace) and using external memory aids and devices — notebooks, diaries, alarm watches, voice recorders and digital reminders — plus metacognitive strategies. These external aids are the best-supported memory approach, while internal "memory-retraining" drills have weak evidence. [Sohlberg et al., 2003, 2007]
  • Errorless and procedural learning — people with poor new-fact (declarative) memory can still learn by doing, so skills are taught in a way that prevents mistakes and relies on procedural memory (memory for how to do things, built by repetition), with many repetitions rather than one-off instructions. [Ehlhardt et al., 2008]

3. Behavioural methods

  • Behaviour modification / Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA). Proven techniques to increase helpful behaviours and reduce unhelpful ones through reinforcement (rewarding wanted behaviour), extinction (removing the reward that kept a behaviour going) and shaping.
  • Positive Behaviour Support (PBS) (shaping the environment and teaching skills so problem behaviour becomes less likely) — a respectful, modern approach that looks for the function a behaviour serves and meets that need a better way.
  • Antecedent vs consequence management. Antecedent strategies set things up before (scripts, routines, a planned "escape" for rising anger); consequence strategies respond after (reinforcing good behaviour). The trend favours antecedent methods, because punishment can backfire in a person with impaired reasoning and self-control, and supports are gradually faded to build independence. [Ylvisaker, Turkstra & Coelho, 2005]

4. Psychotherapy, grief and identity

  • CBT for adjustment and depression. Cognitive behaviour therapy helps the person identify and change the thoughts that feed anxiety and depression, with behavioural techniques (activation, graded exposure, relaxation) to face difficult situations; it is highly effective for the comorbid anxiety and depression so common in these patients, and (as the disease chapters showed) lifting mood also speeds physical recovery.
  • Supportive therapy. A warm, encouraging relationship that reinforces the patient's healthy, adaptive patterns and strengthens coping; trust between patient and clinician improves the treatment effect.
  • Grief and identity / self-concept work. Many of these patients are mourning a lost body, lost abilities or a lost sense of who they are. Therapy helps the person grieve the losses and rebuild a workable, valued identity and self-concept around what remains — central to stroke, TBI and SCI.
  • Motivation and adherence work. Motivational approaches support the person to keep practising exercises, attend appointments and take medication — the behaviours that, via the mind–body loop, determine physical outcome.
  • Interpersonal and relaxation methods. Interpersonal therapy (IPT) (a therapy focused on relationships and roles) for mood, and relaxation and biofeedback for tension, anxiety and pain.

5. Family, group and psychosocial rehabilitation

  • Family and caregiver psychoeducation and support. Caregivers carry a huge, often unseen load. The team encourages the family to share their feelings, explains the nature and prognosis of the illness, teaches practical management, and supports the carers' own wellbeing — all of which reduces family turmoil and, in turn, helps the patient.
  • Group therapy. Education groups and family groups let patients and families share experiences, reduce isolation and stigma, and learn from one another with a special sense of empathy.
  • Stigma reduction and demystifying illness. As in epilepsy and HIV, educating the person, family and community about the condition reduces shame and exclusion and supports disclosure and participation.
  • Psychosocial and vocational rehabilitation. Because the person must live and work within society, psychosocial rehabilitation rebuilds daily-living skills and social roles, and vocational rehabilitation supports a return to work or a new, suitable role — restoring purpose, income and identity.
  • Therapies for comorbid anxiety and depression. CBT, behavioural techniques (relaxation, deep breathing) and cognitive therapy together manage the anxiety and depression that so often accompany CNS disease.
Topic 9 at a glance
  • The psychologist is core to the biopsychosocial team: psychological work raises the ceiling of physical recovery (mind–body loop), starts early, treats the whole person.
  • Cognitive rehab = remedial (drill, mnemonics, link/visual-story, attention training) vs compensatory (environment + external aids); plus errorless/procedural learning.
  • Behavioural = ABA, Positive Behaviour Support, antecedent > consequence management.
  • Psychotherapy = CBT, supportive therapy, grief/identity work, motivation/adherence, IPT, relaxation/biofeedback; plus family psychoeducation, group therapy, stigma reduction, and psychosocial & vocational rehabilitation.
