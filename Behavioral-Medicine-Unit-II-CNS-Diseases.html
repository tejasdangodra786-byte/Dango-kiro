1 The central nervous system & how brain damage causes disturbance

Before we study each disease, we need one simple map. The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain plus the spinal cord — the body's control centre. When any part of it is damaged, the result is not random: the damage shows up in a few predictable ways, and the problems it causes fall into four groups the syllabus names — cognitive (thinking), personality (the person's usual self), behavioral (what they do), and emotional (how they feel). Learn this frame once and every later topic becomes easy to write.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Say what the CNS is — brain and spinal cord; name the protective features (bone, cerebrospinal fluid, the blood–brain barrier) and the two tissue types (grey and white matter).
  2. Explain the four ways damage shows itself — destructive/deficiency, irritative, release, and compensation phenomena, each with a plain example.
  3. Define the four disturbance domains — cognitive, personality, behavioral, emotional — in one easy line each; these are the four headings you will use for every disease.
  4. Give the general principles — focal vs diffuse damage, acute vs progressive course, and left vs right hemisphere jobs.
  5. End with a one-line summary — where in the brain the damage is, and how fast it happened, shapes what disturbance you will see.

The detailed notes below follow exactly this order, so you can read top-to-bottom and write the answer in the same sequence.

Detailed notes

1. What the central nervous system is

The central nervous system (CNS) (the brain and the spinal cord together — the body's command centre) has the job of taking in sensory information and responding to it. It has two parts:

  • The spinal cord — a thick cable of nerves that carries signals up and down between the brain and the rest of the body. It can also run simple reflexes (automatic quick responses, like pulling your hand off a hot pan) on its own, without asking the brain first.
  • The brain — it pulls together most sensory information and coordinates the body both consciously (things you choose) and unconsciously (things that run by themselves). Thinking, feeling and keeping the body steady all come from different parts of the brain.

Because living nerve tissue is soft — "the consistency of jelly" — the whole CNS is wrapped and cushioned:

  • Bone encases it: the brain sits inside the cranium (the skull) and the spinal cord runs inside a canal through the vertebrae (the bones of the spine).
  • Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (a clear, colourless fluid made inside the brain) bathes the whole CNS. It gives nerve tissue the right chemical bath and acts as a shock-absorber against knocks.
  • The blood–brain barrier (BBB) (a tight filter in the brain's tiny blood vessels) keeps the brain's chemical environment stable by letting only certain substances cross from blood into brain.

There are two kinds of tissue in the CNS:

  • Grey matter (looks greyish; made of nerve-cell bodies) contains the cell bodies, dendrites (the branches that receive signals) and axons. It is arranged in layers (as in the cerebral cortex) or in clusters called nuclei (groups of nerve cells deep in the brain).
  • White matter (looks whitish; the brain's wiring) is mostly axons (the long fibres that carry signals away). It looks white because the axons are coated in myelin (a fatty insulation that speeds up signals).

The top, largest part of the brain is the cerebrum (the big wrinkled part you picture when you think of a brain), which includes the outer cerebral cortex (the thin surface layer where thinking happens). The cerebrum handles conscious sensation, voluntary movement, thinking, learning and emotion. So any fault in a part of the CNS can cause a real disturbance in a person's behaviour and thinking — which is exactly what Unit II is about. [CNS introduction, teaching material; Kolb & Whishaw]

2. The four ways nervous-system damage shows itself

When the nervous system is disturbed, the signs fall into four basic patterns. Most real patients show a mix of them at once.

  1. Destructive or deficiency phenomena — something kills or blocks nerve tissue, so a function is lost. Caused by infarction (tissue death from lost blood supply), a tumour, trauma, or blocking agents like anaesthesia (drugs that switch off sensation). Example: weakness or loss of speech after a stroke.
  2. Irritative phenomena — nerve tissue fires too much. Seen in seizures and in the "pins-and-needles" or burning feelings of nerve disease. These come from pathologic depolarisation (nerve cells wrongly switching on and firing in bursts).
  3. Release phenomena — when higher control is lost, lower systems are "let loose" and become overactive. Examples: over-brisk reflexes and spasticity (stiff, tight muscles), the tremor of Parkinson's disease, uncontrolled crying/laughing after certain losses, and primitive reflexes (baby-like reflexes) that reappear in dementia.
  4. Compensation phenomena — the body adapts to the loss, sometimes well, sometimes badly. Helpful compensation: a high-stepping gait to avoid tripping with a dropped foot, or a broad-based walk to steady an unbalanced body. Unhelpful compensation: contractures (joints that stiffen and get stuck because a limb is not moved).

Combinations are the rule. For example, a person with a brain infarction (dead area from blocked blood) may be weak on one side (destructive), develop spasticity (release), possibly get seizures (irritative), and walk with a swinging-leg gait to cope (compensation). If rehabilitation is not done, the arm can stiffen into a useless bent position — an unhelpful compensation. [Teaching material on CNS disturbance]

3. The four disturbance domains — your answer headings

The syllabus asks for four kinds of disturbance in every disease. Keep these simple definitions and use them as headings each time:

  • Cognitive — thinking skills (attention, memory, language, planning, seeing-and-making-sense of things). Damage shows as forgetting, confusion, trouble with words, or poor planning.
  • Personality — the person's usual, stable way of being (their typical character and manner). Damage can make someone unusually blunt, apathetic, rigid, suspicious, or childlike compared with their old self.
  • Behavioral — the actions you can actually see (what the person does, and how they manage daily tasks). Damage shows as disinhibition (acting without a brake), aggression, wandering, or losing the ability to dress, wash and feed oneself.
  • Emotional — the person's feelings and mood (sadness, fear, anger, flatness). Damage shows as depression, anxiety, irritability, or sudden uncontrolled crying/laughing.
Common confusion

Personality change and behaviour change overlap but are not the same. Personality is the inner character ("he has become cold and uninterested"); behaviour is the outward act ("he swears at strangers and gambles"). In exams, give at least one clear example under each so the examiner sees you can tell them apart.

4. General principles that decide what you will see

  • Focal vs diffuse damage. Focal (one small spot) damage gives specific losses tied to that spot's job (for example, loss of speech). Diffuse (spread all over) damage gives general slowing — poorer concentration, mental speed and overall efficiency, as seen after a severe closed-head injury. [Kolb & Whishaw]
  • Acute vs progressive course. An acute (sudden) event such as a stroke causes a sharp loss that may then partly recover. A progressive (slowly worsening) disease such as Parkinson's or Alzheimer's keeps getting worse over months and years.
  • Localisation. Different jobs sit in different places: a dead area in a language zone harms speech, while the same-size area elsewhere may harm movement or vision. The site of damage, not just its size, predicts the symptoms.
  • Left vs right hemisphere. For most people the left half of the brain leads language (speaking, understanding, reading, writing) and skilled, step-by-step action; the right half leads spatial awareness (sense of space and the left side of the world), attention to the whole scene, and emotional tone. This is why a left-brain stroke tends to cause speech loss, while a right-brain stroke tends to cause neglect of one side and poor spatial judgement. [Kolb & Whishaw]
Supplement: how to tell the four manifestation patterns apart in an exam

When a question asks how nervous-system damage shows itself, sort the signs into the four patterns and give one example of each:

  • Destructive / deficiency — ask "is a function missing?" Tissue is killed or blocked, so something is lost (weakness, loss of speech after a stroke).
  • Irritative — ask "is tissue firing too much?" Over-firing gives extra activity (a seizure, pins-and-needles).
  • Release — ask "has a brake been removed?" When higher control is lost, a lower system runs unchecked (over-brisk reflexes, spasticity, the reappearance of baby-like reflexes).
  • Compensation — ask "is the body adapting?" The person works around the loss, helpfully (a high-stepping walk for a dropped foot) or unhelpfully (a joint stiffening into a contracture (a stuck, shortened joint)).

Quick test: two questions sort almost everything — "is a function lost or extra?" separates destructive from irritative, and "is a brake released or is the body adapting?" separates release from compensation. Remember most patients show a mix, so name more than one.

Topic 1 at a glance
  • CNS = brain + spinal cord; protected by bone, CSF and the blood–brain barrier; built of grey matter (cell bodies) and white matter (myelinated wiring).
  • Damage shows in four ways: destructive/deficiency, irritative, release, compensation — usually mixed.
  • It disturbs four domains: cognitive, personality, behavioral, emotional — your fixed answer headings.
  • Where (focal/diffuse, left/right) and how fast (acute/progressive) the damage happens predicts the picture.

2 Cerebrovascular disorders: stroke & vascular dementia

A stroke is what happens when the brain's blood supply suddenly fails — either a vessel is blocked or a vessel bursts. Brain tissue needs a non-stop supply of oxygen and sugar; if the flow stops for more than a few minutes, cells in that area die. Because each area of the brain has its own job, a stroke produces very different problems depending on which vessel and which side is hit. When many small strokes pile up over time, the thinking decline is called vascular dementia.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define stroke — sudden loss of brain function from interrupted blood flow; give the other name, cerebral vascular accident (CVA).
  2. Classify it — the World Health Organization (WHO, 1970) pathological groups (bleeds vs tissue death) and the clinical stage (the brief warning attack, TIA); then the two big mechanisms, ischaemic (blockage) and haemorrhagic (bleed).
  3. Map it by artery and by side — a short table of which artery and which hemisphere gives which syndrome.
  4. Give the four domains — cognitive, personality, behavioral, emotional disturbances after stroke.
  5. Describe vascular dementia — the step-by-step decline and the Hachinski score.
  6. Name the assessments — stroke scales plus the physical work-up done before neuropsychology.
  7. Finish with rehabilitation — psychoeducation, CIMT, gait retraining, VR/mirror/mental imagery, speech therapy for aphasia.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition A stroke (also called a cerebral vascular accident, CVA (an "accident" in the brain's blood vessels)) is "the sudden appearance of neurological symptoms as a result of interruption of blood flow." It often produces an infarct (an area of dead or dying tissue) where blood supply was cut off. [Kolb & Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

classification The WHO (1970) grouped cerebrovascular disease partly by the pathological change in the brain (subarachnoid haemorrhage, intracerebral haemorrhage, and cerebral ischaemic necrosis — which includes vascular dementia) and partly by the clinical stage (including the transient ischaemic attack). [World Health Organization, 1970]

Detailed notes

1. Definition and classification

A stroke's symptoms usually appear suddenly. The person may feel unwell, look pale, complain of a sudden headache, have sudden numbness in the face or limbs down one side, become confused, have trouble talking or understanding, have vision problems, and trouble walking or balancing. Sometimes a seizure (a fit) or loss of consciousness occurs.

The WHO (1970) classification has two parts:

  • I. Pathological change in the brain — (a) subarachnoid haemorrhage (bleeding into the thin space around the brain); (b) intracerebral haemorrhage (bleeding into the brain tissue itself); (c) cerebral ischaemic necrosis (brain tissue dying from lack of blood supply) — this group includes vascular dementia.
  • II. Clinical stage — the transient cerebral ischaemic attack (TIA) (a brief "mini-stroke" that fully recovers): a focal loss of brain function usually lasting a few minutes and never more than 24 hours, leaving no lasting damage, but tending to come back. A warning attack of migraine does not count here. A TIA is an important warning sign of a future full stroke.

By mechanism, strokes are of two broad types:

  • Ischaemic (a blockage) — ischaemia (not enough blood reaching the tissue) from a blocked vessel. Causes: thrombosis (a clot forming and staying where it formed); embolism (a clot or plug that travels from a bigger vessel and jams a smaller one — it most often lodges in the left middle cerebral artery); and cerebral arteriosclerosis (thickening and hardening of the arteries that narrows them). [Kolb & Whishaw]
  • Haemorrhagic (a bleed) — a vessel bursts. Cerebral haemorrhage (heavy bleeding into the brain) is most often caused by hypertension (high blood pressure); it starts abruptly and can be quickly fatal. Weak spots that can bleed include angiomas (tangles of abnormal vessels present from birth) and aneurysms (balloon-like swellings on a weak vessel wall). [Kolb & Whishaw]

A special early-adult type is the migraine stroke (a stroke linked to severe migraine attacks), rare overall but responsible for a notable share of strokes in people under 40, especially women; its likely trigger is vasospasm (blood vessels tightly squeezing shut), most often in the posterior cerebral artery. [Kolb & Whishaw]

2. Stroke by artery and by hemisphere

Which artery is blocked, and which side of the brain it feeds, decides the symptoms. Note the cross-over rule: each half of the brain controls the opposite side of the body, so a left-brain stroke weakens the right side, and vice versa.

[WHO stroke list; teaching material]

3. The four disturbance domains after stroke

  • Cognitive. Aphasia (loss of the ability to speak or understand words), hemianopia (half-vision loss), and problems with memory, attention and executive function (planning and problem-solving). Repeated strokes can build into dementia.
  • Personality. Inattention and lasting personality change (for example becoming apathetic, blunted or disinhibited), and amnesic syndromes (states where forming new memories is badly impaired). A right-hemisphere stroke can cause anosognosia, where the person seems unconcerned because they do not realise they are ill.
  • Behavioral. Apraxia (losing the practised know-how for actions like dressing or using tools) and loss of ADL (activities of daily living — washing, dressing, feeding, toileting), so the person becomes dependent on others for everyday tasks.
  • Emotional. Post-stroke depression is very common. There is also emotional lability (moods swinging quickly and uncontrollably), the related pseudobulbar affect (sudden crying or laughing that does not match the feeling), and the catastrophic reaction (a sudden burst of distress, tears or anger when faced with a task the person can no longer do). [teaching material; Robinson & Price on post-stroke mood]

4. Vascular dementia

Vascular dementia (a lasting decline in thinking caused by many strokes or poor brain blood supply) is part of the WHO "cerebral ischaemic necrosis" group. Its hallmark is a step-wise decline: the person is stable, then drops suddenly with each new small stroke, then is stable again — a staircase going down, rather than the smooth slow slide of Alzheimer's disease. Early signs often include slowed thinking, poor attention, and executive problems, with mood changes. The Hachinski Ischemic Score (a simple points checklist) is used to judge how likely a dementia is vascular (abrupt start, step-wise course, history of strokes and high blood pressure) rather than degenerative. [teaching material; Hachinski Ischemic Score]

5. Assessment of stroke and its consequences

Before any neuropsychological testing, a physical work-up is done, because it guides diagnosis and prognosis (the likely future course): a history of earlier episodes, physical examination, laboratory tests (urine, blood, electrocardiogram, ECG (a tracing of the heart's electrical activity)), cerebral angiography (an X-ray dye study of the brain's blood vessels), examination of the CSF (cerebrospinal fluid), and EEG (electroencephalogram — a recording of the brain's electrical waves). Brain imaging — CT (computerised tomography — a layered X-ray) and MRI (magnetic resonance imaging — a detailed scan using magnets) — shows where and how big the stroke is.

Then specific scales measure severity and the resulting disability:

  • National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS, 1989) (a standard score of how severe a stroke is right now).
  • Canadian Stroke Scale (1986) (another bedside severity scale).
  • Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS, 1974) (measures level of consciousness — covered in Topic 5).
  • Rankin Scale (1957) (rates overall disability after stroke, from none to severe).
  • Barthel Index (1965) (scores how independent the person is in daily activities).
  • SINS — Screening Instrument for Neuropsychological Impairments in Stroke (a quick screen for thinking problems after stroke).
  • Clock Drawing Test (asking the person to draw a clock — a fast check of planning and spatial skill).

6. Psychological intervention and rehabilitation

Strokes affect people differently, so old skills are relearned and physical condition is maintained. Rehabilitation should start as early as possible and may continue at home.

  • Psychoeducation. Teaching the patient and the family about the illness so they understand and cope with it; the person's own strengths, resources and coping skills are reinforced, and they learn that setbacks are part of recovery.
  • Constraint-Induced Movement Therapy (CIMT) (tying up the good arm so the weak arm is forced to work). Developed as a clinical treatment by Taub & Uswatte and Wolf and colleagues, it has two parts: restraining the less-affected arm, and shaping — practising skilled tasks set just at the patient's ability, raising difficulty as they improve, with plenty of feedback and encouragement.
  • Gait (walking) retraining. Most survivors want to walk again. Leg strengthening alone may not improve walking speed in those who can already walk a little. Body-weight-support treadmill training (BWSTT) (walking on a treadmill in a harness that holds part of your weight) allows lots of repeated practice of a more normal walking pattern.
  • Virtual reality, mirror therapy and mental imagery. Virtual reality (VR) (computer-made practice environments) gives many engaging repetitions. In mirror therapy (MT) (a mirror makes the moving good limb look like the weak limb moving) the brain is "tricked" into movement practice; first used for phantom-limb pain, now used for one-sided weakness. Mental imagery / mental practice (rehearsing a movement in the mind without doing it), guided by a therapist, can improve dexterity.
  • Speech therapy for aphasia. Impairment-based therapy directly stimulates listening, speaking, reading and writing. Communication-based (also called consequence-based) therapy aims to get the message across by any means and trains caregivers to support communication in real-life situations.
Supplement: the main types of aphasia after a left-hemisphere stroke

Aphasia (loss of the ability to use words) comes in different patterns depending on exactly where the damage is. Knowing the common types makes the "cognitive" domain answer richer:

  • Broca's (expressive) aphasia (damage toward the front) — understanding is fairly good, but speech is slow and effortful, with short, telegram-like sentences. The person usually knows what they want to say but cannot get it out.
  • Wernicke's (receptive) aphasia (damage further back) — speech is fluent but makes little sense, with wrong or made-up words, and understanding is poor. The person often does not realise their speech is muddled.
  • Global aphasia (large stroke affecting both areas) — both speaking and understanding are severely lost.
  • Anomic aphasia (mild, word-finding problem) — speech and understanding are mostly fine, but the person keeps getting "stuck" searching for names of things.

Matching the therapy to the pattern matters: Broca's work focuses on getting words out, Wernicke's work on checking understanding, and anomic work on word-finding strategies.

Topic 2 at a glance
  • Stroke (CVA) = sudden loss of brain function from interrupted blood flow; ischaemic (blockage) or haemorrhagic (bleed); TIA is the warning mini-stroke.
  • Left stroke → aphasia, apraxia, right weakness; right stroke → neglect, anosognosia, spatial loss, left weakness.
  • Four domains: cognitive (aphasia, memory), personality (inattention, amnesic syndrome), behavioral (apraxia, ADL loss), emotional (depression, lability, catastrophic reaction).
  • Vascular dementia declines step-wise (Hachinski score). Rehab: psychoeducation, CIMT, BWSTT gait, VR/mirror/imagery, aphasia therapy.

3 Developmental disorder: cerebral palsy

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a group of movement and posture problems caused by damage to the immature (developing) brain, around the time of birth. The key words are non-progressive (the brain injury itself does not get worse over time) and developmental (it starts very early in life). The damage is fixed, but as the child grows, the effects on muscles, bones and daily life can change, so treatment aims to help the child function and prevent avoidable complications.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define CP — a non-progressive motor (movement) disorder of the immature brain; mostly congenital (present at or near birth).
  2. Give the types — spastic, athetoid/dyskinetic, dystonia, ataxic, mixed; link each to the brain area damaged.
  3. List the effects — primary, secondary, tertiary.
  4. Cover the four domains — cognitive, personality, behavioral, emotional.
  5. Name the screening tools used to detect developmental problems.
  6. End with rehabilitation — neurosurgery, OT, PT (incl. aqua and hippotherapy), speech therapy, assistive equipment, biofeedback, spasticity management.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition Cerebral palsy can be defined as "a central motor dysfunction affecting muscle tone, posture and movement resulting from a permanent, non-progressive defect or lesion of the immature brain." It is an umbrella term for a group of non-progressive, non-contagious motor conditions; the vast majority of cases are congenital (present at or around the time of birth). [standard CP definition, teaching material]

Detailed notes

1. Types of cerebral palsy

Doctors classify CP by where the brain was damaged, which body parts are affected, and the kind of muscle tone problem.

  • Spastic (the most common) — injury to the cerebral cortex causes abnormally stiff muscles (spasticity); it can cause bone deformities and shortened muscles (contractures (permanently tightened, stuck muscles)). It is sub-divided by the limbs affected:
    • Diplegia (both legs affected more than the arms — common in premature babies).
    • Hemiplegia (one side of the body — common after a baby's stroke or brain injury).
    • Quadriplegia (all four limbs — common after an interruption of oxygen).
    • Monoplegia (one limb) and Triplegia (three limbs).
  • Athetoid (dyskinetic) — injury to the basal ganglia (deep brain clusters that smooth out movement) causes involuntary, writhing movements that disturb speaking, feeding, grasping and walking.
  • Dystonia — also from basal-ganglia injury; causes fluctuating muscle tone (tone that keeps changing), often low at rest but rising sharply when the child tries to move or feels strong emotion.
  • Ataxic — injury to the cerebellum (the "balance computer" at the back of the brain) causes poor coordination, so balance, posture and steady walking are affected.
  • Mixed — injury to several areas (often cortex plus basal ganglia) gives more than one tone problem together, for example spasticity with dystonia.

2. Effects and the four disturbance domains

The effects are grouped as:

  • Primary effects — directly from the brain injury: abnormal muscle tone, poor motor control, abnormal reflexes, balance and movement problems, muscle weakness.
  • Secondary effects — build up over time: inadequate muscle growth, malformed bones and joints.
  • Tertiary effects — the coping responses (the ways the child adjusts, especially in walking, to work around the primary and secondary problems).

Now the four domains:

  • Cognitive. Attention-span problems; vision problems (astigmatism (blurred vision from an uneven eye surface), long/short sight, strabismus (squint), "lazy eye"); hearing loss; speech-and-language difficulty; learning difficulty and, where present, intellectual impairment; and seizures/epilepsy as a common companion. Not every child with CP has intellectual impairment — many have normal intelligence trapped behind a movement problem.
  • Personality / Emotional. Repeated struggle with movement and communication can lead to frustration, low self-esteem, and difficulties in social and emotional adjustment. These are understandable reactions to living with a visible disability, not a fixed "CP personality".
  • Behavioral. Limits in ADL (activities of daily living) and in motor control affect dressing, feeding, play and communication, so the child may need help or adapted methods to do everyday actions.

3. Assessment and screening tools

Early developmental screening helps catch delays. Common tools:

  • Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ) (a parent-filled checklist of a child's skills by age).
  • BRIGANCE Screens (quick screens of developmental skills).
  • Child Development Inventories (parent-report inventories such as the Denver II and the Minnesota Child Development Inventory).
  • Parents' Evaluation of Developmental Status (PEDS) (uses parents' concerns to flag problems).
  • Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers (M-CHAT) (a short screen for autism risk).

These are useful but may miss subtle problems or milder cases, so they are best used routinely and alongside clinical judgement.

4. Rehabilitation for cerebral palsy

  • Neurosurgical procedures (to reduce spasticity). By site: segmental (acting on the spinal-cord circuit that keeps muscle tone) and suprasegmental (acting on higher control centres — nuclei or tracts). By nature: ablative / neurolytic (making a permanent cut or lesion, e.g. rhizotomy, thalamotomy) and non-ablative (reversible stimulation, e.g. spinal cord stimulation).
  • Occupational therapy (OT). Improves fine motor skills (hand and arm movements for dressing, grooming, eating) and the visuo-motor and visuo-perceptual skills (hand–eye and seeing-and-understanding skills needed for reading and writing).
  • Physical therapy (PT). Builds gross motor skills — balance, coordination and strength for walking. It includes:
    • Aqua therapy (exercise in water): water's buoyancy supports the body so movement is safe and gentle on joints; warm water also relaxes muscles.
    • Hippotherapy (therapy using a horse's movement): the rhythm and sway of a walking horse drives the rider's muscles and balance, improving physical and cognitive function (different from ordinary horse-riding lessons).
  • Speech and language therapy. Assesses speech and swallowing (moving food safely from mouth to stomach); if speaking is hard, augmentative communication (back-up methods such as sign language, picture boards or voice-output devices) is used.
  • Adaptive equipment and assistive technology. A team fits equipment that manages tone and supports independence: adaptive bath/toilet chairs; orthoses (braces), casts and splints to improve alignment and reduce contractures; and specialised beds, wheelchairs and walkers.
  • Biofeedback. Biofeedback (giving the body's hidden signals back to the person as sound or light so they can learn to control them) has been used for abnormal movements — e.g. sensors in a helmet give a sound and lights when head posture drifts, teaching the child to hold the head steady.
  • Management of spasticity. Starts with clear, reachable goals (improve function, prevent complications, relieve pain): positioning and seating (a balanced, symmetrical, stable, comfortable posture is the single most important step); splinting and casting (prevents contractures; serial casting slowly stretches a stiff joint); and oral medication (limited use because of side effects like drowsiness and weakness).
Supplement: telling the types of cerebral palsy apart by what you see

Because each type comes from a different injured area, each produces a movement picture you can learn to recognise at the bedside:

  • Spastic (cortex) — muscles look and feel stiff; limbs are held tight and resist being moved, and movements are jerky. This is the most common pattern, named further by which limbs are involved (diplegia, hemiplegia, quadriplegia).
  • Athetoid / dyskinetic (basal ganglia) — slow, writhing, involuntary movements that the child cannot stop; they get worse with effort or excitement and disturb speech, feeding and grasp.
  • Dystonic (basal ganglia) — muscle tone keeps changing, often floppy at rest but suddenly tightening into twisted postures when the child moves or feels emotion.
  • Ataxic (cerebellum) — shaky, poorly-coordinated movement with a wide, unsteady walk and trouble with precise actions, because balance and timing are off.
  • Mixed — a combination (commonly spastic plus athetoid) when more than one area is injured.

Quick cue: stiff → spastic; writhing → athetoid; changing tone → dystonic; wobbly/unbalanced → ataxic. Remember a child may have normal intelligence trapped behind the movement problem, so never assume low ability from the way they move.

Topic 3 at a glance
  • CP = non-progressive movement disorder of the immature brain, mostly congenital.
  • Types by site: spastic (cortex), athetoid/dystonic (basal ganglia), ataxic (cerebellum), mixed.
  • Four domains: cognitive (attention, vision/hearing, speech, seizures, variable intellect), personality/emotional (frustration, low self-esteem), behavioral (ADL and motor limits).
  • Rehab: neurosurgery, OT, PT (aqua + hippotherapy), speech therapy, assistive tech, biofeedback, spasticity management.

4 Degenerative diseases: Parkinson's, with Alzheimer's & Huntington's

Degenerative diseases slowly destroy brain cells over years, with scarring but no major inflammation. They are progressive (steadily worsening) and mostly irreversible. We take Parkinson's disease as the main example and briefly contrast Alzheimer's disease and Huntington's disease. Although each disease is different, they share a common cluster of problems — thinking decline, mood changes, psychosis, sleep problems, fatigue and odd behaviours — so once you learn that shared profile, you can describe any of them.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define degenerative disease — slow loss of neurons with scarring; progressive; list common examples.
  2. Give the shared neurodegenerative profile — the cognitive, mood, psychosis, sleep, fatigue and neuro-behavioural symptoms that nearly all of them share.
  3. Describe Parkinson's — dopamine loss in the substantia nigra; the four cardinal motor signs.
  4. Give the four domains in Parkinson's — cognitive, personality, behavioral, emotional.
  5. Contrast Alzheimer's and Huntington's in a short table.
  6. End with assessment and management — drugs, cognitive rehab (remedial vs compensatory), exercise/gait, OT.

Detailed notes

1. What a degenerative disease is, and the shared profile

These diseases are marked by the loss of neurons and secondary gliosis (scarring by support cells), without major inflammation or tissue death — a large family of slowly progressive diseases of mostly unknown cause. Common examples: Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, spinocerebellar ataxia, Pick's disease, muscular dystrophy, prion diseases, and AIDS dementia complex (covered in Topic 7). Almost all of them share this symptom profile:

  • Cognitive impairment — across executive function, information-processing speed, attention, memory, language, visuospatial/visuoperceptual (seeing and judging space and shapes) skill, and general intelligence.
  • Mood disorders — depression and various anxiety disorders.
  • Psychosis — among the most disabling and distressing symptoms (losing contact with reality, e.g. delusions or hallucinations).
  • Sleep disorders — insomnia (can't sleep), hypersomnia (too much sleep), parasomnia (abnormal events during sleep), and REM sleep behaviour disorder (acting out dreams).
  • Fatigue — one of the most common, distressing and disabling non-movement symptoms.
  • Neuro-behavioural problems — hypersexuality (abnormally high sexual drive), preoccupation with taking machines apart, hypomania/mania, aggression and irritability, an urge to walk long distances aimlessly, pathological gambling and shopping, and food cravings.

2. Parkinson's disease

Parkinson's disease comes from the loss of dopamine (a brain chemical that helps smooth, start and control movement)-making cells in the substantia nigra (a dark area within the basal ganglia). With too little dopamine, movement circuits misfire. The four cardinal motor signs are:

  • Resting tremor (a rhythmic shaking that is worst when the limb is at rest).
  • Rigidity (stiff, resistant muscles).
  • Bradykinesia / hypokinesia (slowness and poverty of movement).
  • Postural instability (poor balance, with a tendency to fall).

3. The four disturbance domains in Parkinson's

  • Cognitive. Bradyphrenia (slowed thinking, the mental version of slowed movement), executive dysfunction (trouble planning and switching tasks), visuospatial problems, and in later stages a dementia.
  • Personality. A tendency toward rigidity (mental inflexibility) and apathy (loss of drive and interest); some people develop impulse-control changes, often linked to the dopamine-boosting medication itself.
  • Behavioral. The neuro-behavioural cluster — hypersexuality, pathological gambling and shopping, punding (repeating a pointless activity over and over, like endlessly sorting objects), and purposeless wandering.
  • Emotional. Depression and anxiety are very common, along with psychosis (sometimes drug-related), sleep disorders and fatigue.

4. Contrast: Alzheimer's and Huntington's disease

5. Assessment and management

Assessment tools used for degenerative (and infectious) brain disease include: PET (positron emission tomography — a scan of brain activity); SPECT (single-photon emission computed tomography — a blood-flow scan); MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination — a quick 30-point memory-and-orientation test); 3MSE (Modified Mini-Mental State — a longer, more sensitive version); Hachinski Ischemic Score (tells vascular from degenerative dementia); Clock Drawing Test; Blessed Information-Memory-Concentration (a short memory and concentration scale); ADL (activities-of-daily-living) and the Blessed Functional Assessment Scale; Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) (a depression screen for older adults); and the Neuropsychiatric Inventory-Q (NPI-Q) (a carer-report checklist of behaviour and mood symptoms).

Management (symptoms vary with onset and course, so treatment is tailored):

  • Pharmacological. Most degenerative conditions are irreversible and the medicines slow decline rather than cure. For dementia and movement symptoms: cholinesterase inhibitors (boost a memory chemical in Alzheimer's), memantine (protects cells from over-excitation in dementia), dopamine agonists and levodopa (top up dopamine in Parkinson's), MAO-B inhibitors (slow dopamine breakdown) and anticholinergics (help tremor). Extra drugs treat the comorbid depression, anxiety, over-activity and sleep problems.
  • Cognitive rehabilitation. An individual programme of skills training plus metacognitive strategies (teaching the person to watch and correct their own thinking), in two approaches: the remedial (restorative) approach — drill and practice, verbal and visual mnemonics, the link method (joining items into a chain of images), the visual story method (turning a list into a vivid story); and the compensatory approach — changing the environment and using devices (alarm watches, diaries, voice recorders) to work around the deficit.
  • Physical exercise and gait training. Regular exercise maintains mobility, flexibility, strength, walking speed and quality of life; gentle rocking, slow rotations, rhythmic initiation, diaphragmatic breathing and meditation ease rigidity. Parkinson's gait problems (hypokinesia (slow, small movement), shuffling, reduced arm swing) are tackled with assistive equipment (pole and treadmill walking), verbal/visual/auditory cueing, and varied practice surfaces.
  • Occupational therapy (OT). Helps with daily living — tremor-management strategies, feeding skills, coordination exercises, and home-safety and equipment advice — tailored to the person's goals.
Supplement: why functional (ADL) assessment matters as much as memory testing

Memory scores like the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination) tell you how damaged thinking is, but they do not tell you whether the person can still live safely. That is why degenerative-disease work-ups add a functional layer:

  • ADL — activities of daily living (the basic self-care tasks: washing, dressing, feeding, toileting, moving about). Losing these means the person needs hands-on care.
  • Blessed Functional Assessment Scale (a carer-rated scale of everyday performance) — it tracks how well the person still manages real-life tasks (handling money, finding their way, remembering recent events, dressing, feeding) and so measures the practical impact of the disease over time.
  • Blessed Information-Memory-Concentration (a short direct test of memory and concentration) — this pairs with the functional scale so you capture both what the person knows and what they can do.

Putting a cognitive score next to a functional score gives a fuller picture, guides how much support the family needs, and lets you measure change at the next review.

  • Parkinson's = dopamine loss (substantia nigra) → tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, postural instability.
  • Four domains: cognitive (bradyphrenia, executive, later dementia), personality (rigidity, apathy, impulse changes), behavioral (gambling, punding, wandering), emotional (depression, anxiety, psychosis).

5 Trauma: traumatic brain injury (TBI) & spinal cord injury (SCI)

Trauma means a physical injury from an outside force — a car crash, a fall, a blow. Traumatic brain injury (TBI) (sudden damage to the brain from an outside force) is a leading cause of long-term disability, and it is the behaviour and thinking changes, more than the physical ones, that most burden patients and families. Spinal cord injury (SCI) (damage to the spinal cord that cuts signals below the injury) causes paralysis and loss of sensation, and brings its own heavy emotional adjustment. We cover how these injuries happen, how severity is measured, the four domains, and management.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define TBI and SCI and say why the behavioural outcome matters most.
  2. Explain TBI mechanisms — open vs closed head injury, coup/countercoup, diffuse axonal injury, haematoma/edema/raised pressure, secondary injury.
  3. Measure severity — the Glasgow Coma Scale, the grade 1–4 classification, and post-traumatic amnesia; put severity in a table.
  4. Give the four domains in TBI — cognitive, personality, behavioral, emotional.
  5. Describe SCI — level and completeness, dependence, and the emotional adjustment.
  6. End with management and rehab — associated injuries, pain, memory/attention, behaviour management, speech-swallowing.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition In a closed-head injury, "there is damage at the site of the blow, a bruise (contusion) called a coup … the pressure that produces the coup may push the brain against the opposite side of the skull, producing an additional bruise, known as a countercoup." Twisting and shearing of fibres, hematoma and edema add further, often diffuse, damage. [Kolb & Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

concept Diffuse axonal injury (DAI) — widespread microscopic stretching and swelling of axons from the primary injury itself (not from later pressure). First named by Adams and earlier described by Strich. [Adams; Strich]

Detailed notes

1. TBI: how the brain is injured

Head injury harms the brain by direct damage, by cutting blood supply, by bleeding and swelling that raise pressure inside the skull, by letting in infection, and by scarring (which can later cause seizures). There are two main types:

  • Open-head injury — the skull is penetrated (gunshot, missile, bone fragments). The person may not lose consciousness; the losses are often specific to the damaged spot, like a small surgical removal, and may recover.
  • Closed-head injury — a blow without penetration sets off several forces: the coup (bruise under the impact), the countercoup (rebound bruise on the far side), shearing of fibres causing diffuse axonal injury (DAI) (widespread microscopic tearing of the brain's wiring) especially in the frontal and temporal lobes and across the corpus callosum (the bridge joining the two halves of the brain), then haematoma (a growing clot of trapped blood pressing on the brain) and edema (swelling) that push up the pressure inside the skull.

It helps to separate primary injury (the damage at the moment of impact — contusions, shearing) from secondary injury (what follows — bleeding, swelling, raised pressure, ischaemia (reduced blood/oxygen), herniation). Closed-head injuries in traffic crashes are especially severe because the moving head multiplies the number and severity of tiny lesions. [Kolb & Whishaw]

2. Measuring severity: GCS, grades and amnesia

The most widely used tool for level of consciousness is the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS, 1974) (a bedside score of consciousness), which rates three things — eye opening, motor (movement) response, and verbal (speech) response — and adds them up. The longer a coma or amnesia lasts, the worse the likely outcome. X-rays, CT and MRI scans are also used.

There is also retrograde amnesia (loss of memories from just before the injury). [Kolb & Whishaw; teaching grade classification]

Complications include post-traumatic epilepsy, post-concussion syndrome (lingering headache, dizziness, poor concentration and irritability after a knock to the head), cognitive and memory impairments (retrograde and post-traumatic amnesia), and behavioural, personality, interpersonal and emotional changes.

3. The four disturbance domains in TBI

Because closed-head injury hits the frontal and temporal lobes hardest, the picture centres on personality and self-control. Interestingly, bright people are often most affected because they notice every drop in their own ability.

  • Cognitive. Reduced mental speed, poor concentration, memory problems, and executive difficulties (planning, organising, problem-solving) — the hallmark of diffuse damage.
  • Personality. Frontal / executive personality change — the person's character shifts (for example becoming blunt, tactless, apathetic, or childishly impulsive), often while raw intelligence scores still look normal.
  • Behavioral. Disinhibition (acting without the usual brake), anger and temper outbursts, and changed interpersonal behaviour that strains relationships and work.
  • Emotional. Depression, irritability and general emotional change; many severely injured people do not return to study or work, or return at a lower level. [Kolb & Whishaw]

4. Spinal cord injury (SCI)

In SCI, the key ideas are level and completeness:

  • Complete injury = no movement or sensation below the injury. If the arms are spared, the person has paraplegia (both legs paralysed); if the arms are involved too, tetraplegia (all four limbs paralysed).
  • Incomplete injury = some function remains several segments below; the person has paraparesis (partial leg weakness) or tetraparesis (partial weakness of all four limbs).
  • The level of injury is the lowest intact spinal segment — everything below it is affected.

Physical and emotional adjustment. SCI brings sudden physical dependence (paraplegics need both arms for transfers and wheelchair control). The psychological / emotional load is heavy: depression, body-image change, grief for the lost way of life, and a hit to quality of life; coping style and personality shape how well the person adjusts. Good executive function matters a lot, because a well-integrated, safe life depends on planning and self-management.

5. Management and rehabilitation of TBI and SCI

  • Manage associated injuries. Treat other injuries well, since recovery depends on the whole person. Impaired personality, memory, concentration and intellect can change the outcome; joint damage and contractures and peripheral-nerve injuries (e.g. the brachial plexus (the nerve bundle to the arm)) are especially disabling for wheelchair users.
  • Pain. Musculo-skeletal and neurogenic (nerve-generated) pain are common after SCI. CBT for pain teaches the link between body (pain, tension), thoughts, feelings and behaviour; it uses cognitive restructuring (replacing unhelpful thoughts with helpful ones), behavioural activation (exercise, pacing), better sleep and coping skills (relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing, biofeedback), and sometimes hypnosis. Painkillers are used carefully because of addiction risk.
  • Memory and attention.
    • Direct attention training (practising the actual task while resisting distractions), building from simple to complex — but trained on the real task the person needs, because gains do not transfer well. [Sohlberg et al., 2003]
    • External memory aids (notebooks, maps, digital reminders in the real setting) are supported by evidence; internal "memory-retraining" exercises are not recommended. Aids must be relevant and acceptable to the person. [Sohlberg et al., 2007]
    • Behaviour management for anger and social situations, using either antecedent (set up before — scripts, routines, an "escape" routine for rising temper) or consequence (responses after — reinforcing good behaviour) strategies. The trend favours antecedent methods, because punishment can frustrate a person who has impaired reasoning and control; supports should be gradually withdrawn to build independence. [Ylvisaker, Turkstra & Coelho, 2005]
    • Learning new skills by leaning on intact procedural memory (memory for how to do things, learned by repetition) rather than weak declarative memory (memory for facts and events) — so many repetitions, not one-off instructions. [Ehlhardt et al., 2008] Plus metacognitive / awareness training for complex tasks.
  • Speech-swallowing rehabilitation. Assess and treat per the problem: augmentative-alternative communication (AAC) (back-up ways to communicate) for word-finding problems; exercises for apraxia/dysarthria (trouble programming or moving speech muscles); and dysphagia (swallowing difficulty) techniques such as careful positioning, a chin-tuck or supraglottic swallow to protect the airway, adapted utensils, and modified food/liquid textures.
Supplement: the two kinds of amnesia and post-concussion syndrome after a head injury

After a head injury the memory gap sits on both sides of the moment of impact, and it is worth separating them clearly:

  • Retrograde amnesia (looking backward) — loss of memories from the period just before the injury. It often shrinks as the person recovers, with older memories returning first and the last few seconds before impact sometimes lost for good.
  • Post-traumatic amnesia (PTA) (looking forward) — the stretch after the injury during which the person cannot lay down new day-to-day memories, even if they seem awake and talking. Its length is one of the best simple guides to severity: under 10 minutes is very mild, 10–60 minutes mild, 1–24 hours moderate, 1–7 days severe, and over 7 days very severe.

Post-concussion syndrome (the cluster that can linger after even a "mild" knock to the head) includes headache, dizziness, tiredness, poor concentration, memory lapses, irritability and low mood. It can appear with a normal scan and a GCS of 13–15, which is exactly why a near-normal GCS does not mean "no injury" — the person may still struggle for weeks or months.

  • Severity: GCS (≤8 severe, 9–12 moderate, 13–15 mild), grades 1–4, and post-traumatic amnesia duration.
  • Four domains (frontal/temporal): cognitive (speed, memory, executive), personality (frontal change), behavioral (disinhibition, temper), emotional (depression, irritability).

6 Convulsive disorder: epilepsy

Epilepsy is a tendency to have repeated seizures — sudden bursts of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. Think of it as an electrical "storm": groups of brain cells fire together, too much and out of order. Depending on where the storm starts and how far it spreads, a seizure can be a brief blank stare or a full convulsion. Epilepsy also leaves its mark between seizures, on thinking, mood, behaviour and even personality, so it is very much a Unit II topic.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define epilepsy — give Hughlings Jackson's 1889 definition and name the causes.
  2. Give the seizure types — primary generalized vs focal (localization-related), with examples; put them in a table.
  3. Explain the three phases — pre-ictal (aura/warning), ictal (the seizure), post-ictal (afterwards).
  4. Cover the four domains — cognitive, personality (the interictal temporal-lobe pattern), behavioral, emotional.
  5. Name the assessments used in epilepsy.
  6. End with management — anti-epileptic drugs and psychotherapeutic methods.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition Epilepsy is "an occasional, an excessive and a disorderly discharge of nervous tissue," brought on by any process that pathologically raises the chance of depolarisation (nerve cells switching on) and synchronised firing of groups of neurons. [John Hughlings Jackson, 1889]

Detailed notes

1. Definition and causes

Using Hughlings Jackson's classic 1889 words above, the core idea is an electrical over-firing in the brain. Many things can cause it: metabolic disorders of nerve cells, or virtually anything that damages cortical tissue — trauma, haemorrhage, ischemia (lost blood supply), anoxia (lost oxygen), infection, hyperthermia (very high body heat), or scar tissue from an earlier injury.

2. Types of seizure

Table 6.1 — Broad types of seizure
TypeWhat happens in the brainExamples / features
Primary generalizedThe seizure involves all of the cerebral cortex at onceTonic-clonic ("grand mal") (stiffening then rhythmic jerking, with loss of consciousness); absence ("petit mal") (brief blank staring spells, common in children)
Focal onset (localization-related)A localized cluster of neurons fires abnormally (it may then spread)Symptoms match the area involved; automatisms (automatic actions like lip-smacking or chewing) are common in temporal-lobe seizures

Note that while most seizures show motor signs (movement), some show mainly inhibitory signs — the blank, staring absence seizure is the classic example.

3. The three phases of a seizure

  • Pre-ictal / aura (the warning). Aura (an early warning sensation before the main seizure) can be sensory or thought-related — déjà vu (feeling you have lived this moment before), jamais vu (a familiar place feeling strange), odd smells, sounds or tastes, blurred vision, racing thoughts, stomach feelings or tingling — and emotional, most typically fear/panic. Physical warnings can include chewing movements, drooling, eyelid fluttering and eyes rolling up.
  • Ictal (the seizure itself). Ictal (during the seizure) symptoms include blacking out, confusion, loss of consciousness, "spacing out", out-of-body feelings, electric-shock sensations, deafness or odd sounds, and visual loss.
  • Post-ictal (afterwards). The recovery phase: memory loss and writing difficulty; confusion, depression and sadness, fear, frustration and shame; tiredness and sleep, headache, nausea, pain, thirst and weakness; and, during and after, physical signs such as lip-smacking, staring, stiffening, swallowing, sweating, teeth-grinding, tongue-biting, tremors and incontinence.

4. The four disturbance domains in epilepsy

  • Cognitive. Thinking differs across phases — poor during and after a seizure (ictal/post-ictal), and sometimes mildly affected between seizures (interictal (the periods between seizures)), touching memory and attention. Anti-epileptic drugs can also dull thinking, which must be balanced against seizure control.
  • Personality. In some people with long-standing temporal-lobe epilepsy, writers describe an interictal personality pattern: hypergraphia (an urge to write a great deal), hyperreligiosity (intense religious or philosophical concern), viscosity (sticky, hard-to-end conversation and social contact), circumstantiality (over-detailed, roundabout talk), and altered sexuality. This should be presented carefully as something reported in the literature in a minority, not as a rule for all patients. [Bear & Fedio, 1977; Blumer]
  • Behavioral. Automatisms (automatic movements such as lip-smacking, chewing, hand-waving) and other behaviour change around seizures.
  • Emotional. Depression and anxiety are common; a fear/panic aura can be frightening; psychosis can occur; and the unpredictability of seizures lowers quality of life.

5. Assessments used for epilepsy

  • EEG (electroencephalograph — records the brain's electrical waves; the key epilepsy test) and ECG (electrocardiograph — records the heart's electrical activity).
  • Blood testing; PET (positron emission tomography — a brain-activity scan); and spinal tap (lumbar puncture — taking a sample of CSF).
  • Bender Visual-Motor Gestalt Test (copying shapes to screen for brain dysfunction).
  • Luria-Nebraska Neuropsychological Battery (a full set of tests of brain functions).
  • WADA test (briefly putting one half of the brain to sleep to find which side holds language and memory before surgery).
  • Serum prolactin (a blood hormone that often rises just after a true seizure, helping confirm it).

6. Management and rehabilitation

  • Anti-epileptic drugs (AEDs) — psychopharmacological treatment. The commonly used medicines include carbamazepine (Tegretol), clorazepate (Tranxene), clonazepam (Klonopin), ethosuximide (Zarontin), felbamate (Felbatol), fosphenytoin (Cerebyx), gabapentin (Neurontin), lacosamide (Vimpat), lamotrigine (Lamictal), levetiracetam (Keppra), oxcarbazepine (Trileptal), phenobarbital (Luminal), phenytoin (Dilantin), pregabalin (Lyrica), primidone (Mysoline), tiagabine (Gabitril), topiramate (Topamax), valproate semisodium (Depakote), valproic acid (Depakene) and zonisamide (Zonegran).
  • Psychotherapeutic approaches (chosen and modified to the person's need):
    • Relaxation therapy — calming the mind by releasing muscle tension, to cut the stress and anxiety that can trigger seizures.
    • Behaviour modification — working with a therapist to live a full life without ignoring, or being ruled by, the epilepsy.
    • Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) — effective for the depression and anxiety that often accompany epilepsy, by changing thought patterns and behaviour.
    • Biofeedback — galvanic skin response (GSR) biofeedback (feedback from the skin's sweat response) has reduced seizure frequency; EEG biofeedback improved cognitive and motor function with the greatest seizure reduction.
    • Educational interventions — improve knowledge of epilepsy, coping, medication adherence and social skills.
    • Family counselling — a useful add-on treatment.

7 Infectious disorder: AIDS dementia complex / HAND

Some infections attack the brain directly. In advanced HIV/AIDS, the virus can reach the central nervous system and cause a slow decline in thinking, movement and behaviour called AIDS dementia complex (ADC), now more broadly termed HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder (HAND). It follows a subcortical pattern — slowed thinking and movement, apathy and low mood — and it carries the extra weight of stigma, which the care plan must tackle head-on.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Spell out the acronyms — HIV, AIDS, ADC, HAND, CNS — and explain each in a line.
  2. Say how the virus reaches and harms the CNS — direct infection and inflammation; a subcortical dementia pattern.
  3. Give the four domains — cognitive, personality, behavioral, emotional.
  4. Describe the staging — from no symptoms, through mild disorder, to HIV-associated dementia.
  5. End with assessment and management — screening, demystifying the infection, stigma education, antiretroviral context, cognitive rehab and psychosocial support.

Detailed notes

1. The acronyms and how HIV harms the brain

First, the names spelled out:

  • HIV — Human Immunodeficiency Virus (the virus that gradually weakens the body's immune defences).
  • AIDS — Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (the advanced stage of HIV infection, when the immune system is badly weakened).
  • ADC — AIDS Dementia Complex (the dementia caused by HIV in the brain), the older name for what is now grouped under HAND — HIV-Associated Neurocognitive Disorder (the spectrum of thinking problems caused by HIV in the CNS).
  • CNS — central nervous system (brain and spinal cord).

HIV can cross into the CNS and damage it by direct infection and by the neuro-inflammation (brain inflammation from the immune response) it provokes, rather than by forming a focal tumour. The result is a subcortical dementia — the pattern of slowed thinking and movement, apathy and low mood seen when deep brain structures are affected, in contrast to the memory-and-language loss of a cortical dementia like Alzheimer's. Because it is a progressive infection of brain tissue, ADC sits among the neurodegenerative/infectious disorders that share the common profile from Topic 4 (cognitive decline, mood, psychosis, sleep problems, fatigue and neuro-behavioural change).

2. The four disturbance domains

  • Cognitive. Psychomotor slowing (both thinking and movement become slow), poor attention and concentration, memory problems and executive difficulty, which can progress to a full dementia.
  • Personality. Apathy (loss of drive and interest) and social withdrawal — the person pulls back from people and activities.
  • Behavioral. Reduced initiative, general motor slowing and gait (walking) changes, so everyday activity falls off.
  • Emotional. Depression and anxiety, and a specific, heavy load of stigma-related distress because HIV/AIDS is so widely and wrongly shamed.

3. Staging / severity

HAND is usually described along a spectrum of severity:

  1. Asymptomatic — test-detectable thinking changes but no everyday impact.
  2. Mild neurocognitive disorder — mild but real problems with memory, attention and everyday tasks.
  3. HIV-associated dementia — the severe end, with marked memory, motor and behaviour problems that disrupt daily life.

4. Assessment and management

  • Assessment. Neuropsychological screening and testing, the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination), and functional / ADL (activities-of-daily-living) assessment to judge real-world impact.
  • Demystifying the infection. The therapist openly explains how HIV is and is not transmitted, clearing up the many misconceptions, and discusses routes of spread and prevention in detail. This reduces fear in the patient, family and care team.
  • Education about stigma. Because HIV/AIDS is heavily stigmatised worldwide, the therapist educates family members and health workers about the real problems patients face, so the person is supported rather than shamed.
  • Antiretroviral context. Treatment of the underlying HIV infection is the medical backbone that slows or improves the brain effects; psychological care works alongside it.
  • Cognitive rehabilitation and psychosocial support. The remedial-vs-compensatory cognitive rehab from Topic 4, plus supportive therapy, family work and group support to protect quality of life.

  • Four domains: cognitive (psychomotor slowing, attention, memory, executive), personality (apathy, withdrawal), behavioral (low initiative, gait), emotional (depression, anxiety, stigma distress).
  • Staging: asymptomatic → mild neurocognitive disorder → HIV-associated dementia.
  • Manage: screening + ADL, demystifying infection, stigma education, antiretroviral context, cognitive rehab, psychosocial support.

8 Assessment & neuropsychological batteries

Before we can help a patient, we must measure what has changed. Neuropsychological assessment (carefully measuring thinking and behaviour to map brain function) uses structured tests and batteries to find which abilities are intact and which are damaged. Good assessment moves in order: first a medical and physical work-up, then a quick screen, then a fuller battery, then a check of everyday functioning. The results guide diagnosis, rehabilitation goals and how progress is tracked.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Say why we assess — to map strengths and deficits, plan rehab and measure change.
  2. Describe the process — history and physical/lab work-up first, then screening, then a full battery, then functional/ADL assessment.
  3. List and explain the named batteries and tests in a table, each with its purpose.
  4. End with screening vs comprehensive and functional assessment.

Detailed notes

1. Why we assess, and the process

We assess to find exactly which abilities are hurt and which are spared, to set realistic rehabilitation goals, and to measure change over time. The process is a sequence:

  • History and physical/lab work-up first. As seen with stroke, the clinician takes a history of earlier episodes and does a physical and laboratory examination (urine, blood, ECG), plus imaging (CT, MRI) and tests such as angiography, CSF examination and EEG. This medical picture must come before neuropsychological testing, because it shapes diagnosis and prognosis. A neurologist's examination also checks the cranial nerves, motor and sensory systems. [teaching material; Kolb & Whishaw on the neurological examination]
  • Screening with a short test to flag problems quickly.
  • A comprehensive battery to profile each ability in depth.
  • Functional / ADL assessment to see how the deficits affect real daily life.

Honest note on the PGI-BBD

The PGI Battery of Brain Dysfunction (Pershad & Verma) is the recognised Indian neuropsychological battery for detecting brain dysfunction. It is described here at the level of its purpose and standing; exact subtest names and cut-off scores are not invented here, so that nothing is misreported.

3. Screening vs comprehensive, and functional assessment

  • Screening tests (MMSE, Clock Drawing, Bender) are short and catch problems quickly, but they can miss subtle or mild deficits.
  • Comprehensive batteries (PGI-BBD, Luria-Nebraska, Halstead-Reitan) take longer but give a detailed profile of each ability, useful for diagnosis and planning.
  • Functional / ADL assessment (Barthel Index, Blessed Functional Assessment Scale) asks the practical question: can the person manage daily life? This is what rehabilitation ultimately aims to improve.
Supplement: "fixed battery" versus "flexible battery" — two ways to test

Comprehensive neuropsychological testing comes in two styles, and it helps to know the difference:

  • Fixed (standard) battery — the same full set of tests is given to everyone, in the same way. The Halstead-Reitan Battery (a classic fixed battery) is the typical example. Strength: results are standardised and easy to compare across people and over time. Cost: it is long and may test things a particular patient does not need.
  • Flexible (process) battery — the clinician picks and adds tests as they go, following the patient's actual problems; the Luria-Nebraska (built on Luria's qualitative approach) tradition leans this way. Strength: efficient and tailored, and it looks at how the person fails, not just the score. Cost: it depends more on the examiner's skill and is harder to compare between people.

In practice many clinicians blend the two: a short screen first (such as the MMSE (Mini-Mental State Examination)), then a core set of tests, then a few extra tests chosen for that person's picture — with a functional / ADL check at the end so the report says what the person can actually do day to day.

9 Psychological intervention & rehabilitation

This final topic pulls together the methods of treatment used across all the diseases above. Rehabilitation (helping a person regain abilities or learn to live well despite a lasting disability) rests on three pillars: the brain's own spontaneous recovery, medical and therapeutic rehabilitation, and learning to live with the handicap. The work is multidisciplinary (many professions together), should start early, and treats the whole person — thinking, behaviour, mood, family and social life.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Give the principles — spontaneous recovery + medical rehab + living with the handicap; start early; multidisciplinary team.
  2. Cognitive rehabilitation — remedial/restorative vs compensatory, with the named techniques.
  3. Behavioural methods — behaviour modification/ABA, antecedent vs consequence management, positive behaviour support.
  4. Psychotherapy — CBT and supportive therapy for adjustment and depression; interpersonal therapy; relaxation/biofeedback.
  5. Family, group and psychosocial rehab — caregivers, education and family groups, vocational and ADL rehab, therapies for comorbid anxiety and depression.

Detailed notes

1. Principles of neuro-rehabilitation

  • Recovery has three strands: the nervous system's own spontaneous recovery in the weeks after injury; medical and therapeutic rehabilitation that trains lost skills; and helping the person learn to live with any lasting handicap.
  • Start early. As with stroke, rehabilitation should begin as soon as the person is stable and may continue at home.
  • Multidisciplinary team. Psychologists, physicians, nurses, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech-language therapists and social workers work together, because the problems cut across movement, thinking, mood, communication and social life.

2. Cognitive rehabilitation

Cognitive rehabilitation (a programme to restore thinking skills or work around the losses) uses individualised skills training plus metacognitive strategies (teaching the person to watch and correct their own thinking), in two approaches:

  • Remedial (restorative). Tries to rebuild the weakened ability through drill and practice, verbal and visual mnemonics, the link method (joining items into a chain of linked images) and the visual story method (turning a list into a vivid story).
  • Compensatory. Works around the deficit by changing the environment (home, school, workplace) and using external devices — alarm watches, agenda books, diaries, voice recorders and reminders — to support memory and routine.

3. Behavioural methods

  • Behaviour modification / Applied Behaviour Analysis (ABA). Using proven techniques to increase helpful behaviours and reduce unhelpful ones through reinforcement (rewarding wanted behaviour), extinction (removing the reward that kept a behaviour going), and related methods. Modern ABA includes Positive Behaviour Support (PBS) (shaping the environment and teaching skills so problem behaviour is less likely).
  • Antecedent vs consequence management. As in TBI (Topic 5), antecedent strategies set things up before (scripts, routines, an escape plan for rising anger), while consequence strategies respond after (reinforcing good behaviour). The trend favours antecedent methods, since punishment can backfire in people with impaired reasoning and control.

4. Psychotherapy, family and psychosocial rehabilitation

  • CBT for adjustment and depression. Cognitive behaviour therapy helps the person identify and change the thoughts that feed anxiety and depression, combined with behavioural techniques to gradually face and tolerate difficult situations; it is highly effective for the comorbid anxiety and depression so common in these patients.
  • Supportive therapy. The therapist offers a warm, encouraging relationship that reinforces the patient's healthy, adaptive patterns and strengthens coping, especially in relationships; trust between patient and clinician improves the treatment effect.
  • Interpersonal and relaxation methods. Interpersonal therapy (IPT) (a therapy focused on relationships and roles) for mood, and relaxation and biofeedback for tension, anxiety and pain.
  • Family and caregivers. Encourage the family to share feelings about the illness; the team provides explanation, understanding and support, and educates the family about the nature and prognosis of the illness — this reduces family turmoil.
  • Group therapy. Education groups and family groups let patients and families share experiences, reduce isolation and stigma, and learn from one another with a special sense of empathy.
  • Psychosocial, vocational and ADL rehabilitation. Because the person must live and function within society, psychosocial rehabilitation rebuilds daily-living skills and, where possible, work roles, alongside the specific neurological rehabilitation.
  • Therapies for comorbid anxiety and depression. CBT, behavioural techniques (relaxation, deep breathing) and cognitive therapy are used together to manage the anxiety and depression that frequently accompany CNS disease.
Topic 9 at a glance

  • Cognitive rehab = remedial (drill, mnemonics, link method, visual story) vs compensatory (environment + devices).
  • Behavioural = ABA / PBS, antecedent vs consequence management.
  • Psychological = CBT, supportive therapy, IPT, relaxation/biofeedback, plus family, group and psychosocial rehabilitation for comorbid anxiety and depression.
