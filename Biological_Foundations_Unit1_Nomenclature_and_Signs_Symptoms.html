Nomenclature and the Classificatory Systems in Current Use

Before any symptom can be named, psychiatry needs an agreed vocabulary and a shared catalogue of disorders, so this topic sets out what classification is, traces the parallel histories of the DSM and the ICD, and shows how the two systems resemble and differ from each other.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of nomenclature & classification → Background / why we classify → DSM history and ICD history → What each is & how they differ → Interview schedules (SCID/CIDI/SCAN) → One-line summary
  1. Nomenclature the agreed set of names for disorders is the shared vocabulary of psychiatry, while classification is the orderly grouping of those named disorders into categories, so that clinicians everywhere can mean the same thing by a diagnosis.
  2. Classification exists chiefly for communication, letting clinicians and researchers talk precisely, pool data on cause and outcome, and decide treatment, so a good system is judged by how useful and reliable it proves.
  3. The two systems in current use are the DSM the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of the American Psychiatric Association and the ICD the International Classification of Diseases of the World Health Organization, and both are built around clinical syndromes.
  4. The DSM lineage runs from DSM-I in 1952 and DSM-II in 1968, which were brief and psychoanalytic, to the landmark DSM-III in 1980, which introduced explicit operational criteria and set the template every later edition has followed.
  5. DSM-IV appeared in 1994 and the current DSM-5 in 2013, a revision that notably dropped the five-axis system the old habit of coding illness, personality, medical, social and functioning problems on separate lines used since DSM-III.
  6. The ICD lineage reaches modern psychiatry with ICD-7 in 1955, then ICD-8 in 1965 and ICD-9 in 1978, each slowly widening and sharpening the mental-disorders section as the WHO sought a single language for health statistics worldwide.
  7. ICD-10 in 1992 gave mental and behavioural disorders their own detailed chapter with clinical descriptions and diagnostic guidelines, and the current ICD-11, adopted in 2022, places them in Chapter 6 with a fully updated, dimensional-leaning structure.
  8. A key difference is purpose: the DSM is an operationalised research manual for a largely specialist readership, whereas the ICD is a broader descriptive system for public-health and general clinical use worldwide.
  9. They also differ in scope, since the ICD is a whole-body disease classification with mental disorders as just one chapter, while the DSM deals solely with mental disorders and so carries more detailed criteria for each.
  10. In style the DSM tends to favour tight operational criteria with counts and thresholds, while ICD-10 offered looser clinical descriptions and guidelines, a difference that can make the same patient meet one system's threshold but not the other's.
  11. Despite these contrasts both systems are syndrome-based they group symptoms that cluster together rather than grouping by proven cause, because psychiatry still lacks the biological markers that would let it classify most disorders by underlying pathology.
  12. The two bodies now work for harmonisation, so that DSM-5 and ICD-11 categories and codes line up far better than earlier editions did, reducing the confusion of a patient being labelled differently on either side of an ocean.
  13. To apply any system reliably clinicians use structured interview schedules, chief among them the SCID the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM, run by a clinician, which walks through DSM criteria disorder by disorder to yield a standardised diagnosis.
  14. The CIDI the Composite International Diagnostic Interview is a fully structured schedule designed for trained lay interviewers and large epidemiological surveys, giving ICD and DSM diagnoses without needing clinical judgement at each question.
  15. The SCAN the Schedules for Clinical Assessment in Neuropsychiatry, built around the Present State Examination is a semi-structured instrument that relies on the clinician's trained judgement to rate the presence and severity of each symptom.
  16. These schedules matter because they raise reliability the chance that two clinicians reach the same diagnosis, turning a classification on paper into something that can be applied consistently in research and audited in everyday clinical work.
  17. In one line, nomenclature gives psychiatry its shared names and the DSM and ICD its two great syndrome-based catalogues, one research-led and specialist, the other descriptive and global, applied reliably through structured interviews.
Sourced characterisation — Classification in psychiatry
A classification in psychiatry is a system that groups mental disorders into categories on the basis of shared clinical features (a syndrome), its chief purposes being reliable communication between clinicians, the gathering of comparable statistics, and the guidance of prognosis and treatment, as set out in the opening chapters of Fish's Clinical Psychopathology.
Source: Paraphrased from Casey, P. & Kelly, B., Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed. (introductory chapter on classification and nomenclature).

Limitations of Classification

No classification is a perfect mirror of nature, so this topic examines the philosophical and practical limits of diagnosis, from Jaspers' caution and the realism debate, through validity, reliability and the clinical-significance criterion, to the stubborn problem of comorbidity.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of the problem → Philosophical status (realism debate) → Validity vs reliability → Criterion & threshold problems → Comorbidity & category limits → One-line summary
  1. Classification is useful but provisional, because psychiatric categories are working tools rather than proven natural kinds, so every system carries limits that a thoughtful clinician must weigh rather than treating a diagnosis as a fixed fact.
  2. Karl Jaspers warned that diagnostic categories are ideal types useful mental models that real patients only ever approximate, so no actual person fits a textbook label exactly, and forcing a messy case into a neat box can distort the clinical picture.
  3. Kendler's 2016 analysis frames three philosophical stances: realism disorders are real natural entities waiting to be discovered, constructivism categories are invented by committees, and pragmatism, the middle view that categories are useful tools judged by what they achieve.
  4. A core limit is the gap between validity whether a category marks a genuinely distinct, real disorder and reliability whether clinicians agree on applying it, because a label can be highly reliable yet still not carve nature at a true joint.
  5. Operational criteria greatly improved reliability after DSM-III, but critics note this can buy agreement at the cost of validity, pinning down a definition precisely without proving that the thing defined is a real and separate illness.
  6. The clinical-significance criterion the rule that symptoms must cause distress or impairment to count as a disorder tries to keep normal reactions out, yet Allen Frances called it the seeds of tautology because it can be circular.
  7. That circularity arises because distress or impairment is used both to define the disorder and to prove it matters, so the criterion risks simply restating that a problem is a problem rather than adding genuinely new information.
  8. The systems even word this threshold differently: the DSM typically requires distress OR impairment, whereas ICD-10 often leant toward requiring BOTH, so the same person can cross one manual's line into caseness while staying below the other's.
  9. Categories may be monothetic every listed feature is required or polythetic any sufficient subset of features will do, and modern manuals are largely polythetic, which means two people with the same diagnosis can share very few symptoms.
  10. This polythetic design produces great within-category heterogeneity, so a single label like depression covers patients whose presentations overlap only partly, weakening the assumption that one diagnosis implies one cause, course or best treatment.
  11. A major limitation is comorbidity two or more disorders diagnosed in the same person at once, which is so common that Tyrer's 2017 work argues it often reflects artificial splitting of what is really one broad underlying problem.
  12. Tyrer distinguishes true co-occurrence from mere consanguinity categories that look separate but are really close relatives carved from the same territory, so high comorbidity rates may be an artefact of how the boundaries were drawn.
  13. Field data expose the fragility of boundaries, with reported ICD-10 versus DSM-IV concordance the rate at which the two systems agree on a case ranging widely, from about 33% to 87% depending on the disorder studied.
  14. Such variable agreement shows that for some conditions the two manuals are nearly interchangeable while for others they disagree sharply, undermining any claim that either system has simply found the one correct way to divide mental illness.
  15. Classification can also create stigma and self-fulfilling expectations, since a label shapes how others treat a person and how the person sees themselves, a social cost that sits alongside the scientific limits of validity and reliability.
  16. Despite all this, the pragmatic defence holds that an imperfect, agreed classification is better than none, because without shared categories research could not accumulate and clinicians could not communicate, so the goal is steady improvement, not abandonment.
  17. In one line, classification is limited by shaky validity, circular thresholds, heterogeneous polythetic categories and pervasive comorbidity, so diagnoses are best treated as useful, revisable tools rather than discovered natural facts.

Disorders of Consciousness

Consciousness is the stage on which all other mental functions appear, so this topic defines awareness of self and environment, sets out the states in which it is clouded, lowered, narrowed or dissociated, and explains why such change is usually a red flag for organic illness.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of consciousness → Background (a continuum of arousal) → Types of disordered consciousness → Clinical examples (delirium, fugue) → Significance (organic until proven) → One-line summary
  1. Consciousness is best defined as awareness of the self and of the environment, a background state of alertness that makes ordinary experience possible, so when it is disturbed every other mental function is inevitably affected too.
  2. Consciousness is not all-or-none but a continuum running from full alertness, through drowsiness and clouding, down to stupor and finally coma, so clinicians describe where on this gradient a patient sits rather than using a single on-off label.
  3. Clouding of consciousness a mild dulling where awareness and attention are slightly reduced is the gentlest change, leaving the patient muddled, slow and poorly focused, and is the earliest warning that the brain's overall function is failing.
  4. A dream-like (oneiroid) state blends vivid inner imagery with the outer world, and is the quality typical of delirium an acute, fluctuating confusional state, in which the patient is disoriented, frightened and often troubled by vivid hallucinations.
  5. A lowering of consciousness (torpor) reduced wakefulness with slow, sluggish thinking leaves the patient drowsy, easily losing the thread, drifting toward sleep, and able to give only poor and muddled answers to questions.
  6. A restriction or narrowing of consciousness (twilight state) keeps a small band of awareness intact while the rest is shut out, so the person may carry out semi-purposeful acts yet have no proper memory of them afterwards.
  7. A fugue a sudden wandering away from home with loss of personal memory is a dissociative narrowing in which a person may travel far, sometimes taking on a new identity, and later cannot recall the episode at all.
  8. These states are distinguished partly by mood and content: the twilight state often carries a tense, irritable mood, while the oneiroid state is dominated by dream-like imagery, helping the clinician tell one narrowing from another.
  9. Delirium deserves special emphasis as the commonest disorder of consciousness in general hospitals, marked by a fluctuating course, worse confusion at night, altered sleep-wake cycle and frequent visual hallucinations and misperceptions.
  10. A cardinal teaching point is that disturbed consciousness is prima facie organic on the face of it caused by physical brain disease, so any clouding should trigger a search for infection, drugs, metabolic upset or other bodily causes.
  11. This organic rule matters because missing a delirium can be fatal, so a new confusional state is treated as a medical emergency until a physical cause is found or firmly excluded, not dismissed as a purely psychological reaction.
  12. By contrast, the dissociative narrowings such as fugue can occur with a clear sensorium and are psychogenic, which is why distinguishing an organic clouding from a dissociative narrowing is one of the first judgements the clinician must make.
  13. Assessment relies on simple bedside tests of orientation, attention and the ability to sustain a conversation, because a patient who cannot hold attention or stay oriented is showing, by definition, a disorder of consciousness.
  14. The level of consciousness typically fluctuates in organic states, so repeated short examinations across the day are more revealing than a single assessment, and night-time worsening is a classic clue to an underlying delirium.
  15. Severe lowering ends in stupor, a state of near-unresponsiveness with preserved posture and some reaction to strong stimuli, and then coma, the complete and unrousable loss of awareness at the far end of the continuum.
  16. Recovery usually leaves a patchy or absent memory for the period of altered consciousness, and this amnesic gap is itself diagnostic evidence that consciousness, not merely mood or thought, was the function disturbed.
  17. In one line, disorders of consciousness are disturbances of awareness ranging from clouding to coma and including dream-like, lowered, narrowed and fugue states, and they should be read as organic until proven otherwise.
Sourced characterisation — Consciousness

Disorders of Attention and Concentration

Attention is the spotlight that selects what we process, so this topic separates active from passive attention, surveys how fatigue, anxiety, mood, psychosis and organic disease each disturb it, and shows why attentional change is both common and diagnostically informative.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of attention & concentration → Active vs passive attention → Causes of distractibility → Clinical patterns by disorder → Significance for assessment → One-line summary
  1. Attention is the active direction of awareness onto a chosen object, while concentration is the effort of holding that focus over time, so together they form the gateway through which information must pass to be remembered.
  2. A useful split is between active attention deliberately choosing what to attend to and passive attention being automatically drawn by a sudden or striking stimulus, and many disorders tip the balance between these two kinds.
  3. Distractibility the tendency for attention to be pulled away by any passing stimulus is the most frequent attentional symptom, appearing whenever active attention weakens and passive attention takes over the direction of the mind.
  4. Ordinary fatigue tiredness and boredom reduce attention in everyone, so a tired or under-stimulated person attends poorly, which means the clinician must gauge whether poor attention is situational or a genuine sign of illness.
  5. In anxiety the mind is repeatedly dragged back to the feared theme, so attention narrows onto threat and concentration on neutral tasks suffers, a pattern patients often describe as being unable to keep their mind on anything.
  6. In depression attention and concentration are slowed and effortful, so patients complain they cannot read a page or follow a conversation, and this subjective poor concentration is one of the recognised diagnostic features of the illness.
  7. In mania attention becomes highly distractible, flitting from one external stimulus to the next, so the patient cannot sustain any single task and their talk jumps in step with whatever happens to catch their eye.
  8. In schizophrenia attention is often disorganised, with patients unable to filter out irrelevant stimuli, so they feel flooded by input and their concentration fragments, mirroring the broader disturbance of thought and perception.
  9. In organic states such as delirium and dementia, impaired attention is a core feature, and indeed the inability to sustain and shift attention is one of the defining signs that separates delirium from a purely functional illness.
  10. A special pattern is the amnestic patient's rigid set, where severe memory loss leaves the person clinging to a narrow fixed focus, unable to redirect attention because they cannot update what they hold in mind.
  11. Attention and consciousness are intimately linked, because a patient whose consciousness is clouded cannot by definition attend well, so attentional testing doubles as a sensitive screen for early clouding of consciousness.
  12. Bedside tests of attention include serial sevens subtracting seven repeatedly from one hundred, reciting the months of the year backwards, or the digit span, each demanding sustained active attention that organic and severe functional illness disrupts.
  13. It is important to separate subjective poor concentration, which the patient reports, from objective inattention that the examiner can demonstrate on testing, because the two do not always agree and each carries different weight.
  14. Attentional symptoms are highly non-specific, occurring across almost every psychiatric and many physical illnesses, so poor attention alone never fixes a diagnosis and must always be read in the context of the whole clinical picture.
  15. Because attention underpins memory registration, a patient who cannot attend will appear to have a memory problem when the true fault lies one step earlier, a distinction the clinician must keep clearly in mind when testing.
  16. Culturally and educationally fair testing matters, since tasks like serial sevens assume numeracy, so the examiner chooses an attention test suited to the patient to avoid mistaking lack of schooling for genuine inattention.
  17. In one line, disorders of attention range from everyday distractibility in fatigue and anxiety to the gross inattention of delirium, and because they are common but non-specific they guide rather than make a diagnosis.

Disorders of Orientation

Orientation anchors a person in time, place and identity, so this topic defines it, explains the characteristic order in which it is lost, distinguishes disorientation from confusion, and shows why disordered orientation is one of psychiatry's clearest pointers to organic disease.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of orientation → The three spheres (time, place, person) → Order of loss → Clinical causes (organic) → Confusion vs disorientation → One-line summary
  1. Orientation is a person's awareness of their situation in time, place and person, so an oriented patient knows the date, knows where they are, and knows who they and the people around them are, grounding them in reality.
  2. Orientation depends on intact consciousness, attention and memory, so it fails when any of these underlying functions fails, which is why testing orientation is such an efficient screen for a disturbed brain.
  3. Orientation to time is usually the first sphere to be lost, so a patient who becomes vague about the day, date, month or even the year is showing the earliest and most sensitive sign of developing disorientation.
  4. Orientation to place is lost next, so the patient may mistake the hospital for home or be unable to name their town or ward, a sign of more advanced disturbance than time errors alone.
  5. Orientation to person is the last to go and the most robust, so losing awareness of one's own identity is rare and usually indicates either very severe organic disease or a dissociative, psychogenic disturbance rather than ordinary confusion.
  6. This stepwise order, time lost first and person lost last, is a reliable clinical ruler, letting the examiner gauge the depth of disturbance from which spheres remain intact and which have given way.
  7. Disorientation not knowing the time, place or who people are arising acutely is regarded as prima facie organic, so like clouding of consciousness it should prompt a search for a physical cause before any functional explanation is accepted.
  8. Disorientation is a hallmark of delirium and dementia, so its presence strongly tilts the diagnosis toward an organic disorder and away from conditions such as anxiety or depression, where orientation is characteristically preserved.
  9. Confusion muddled, unclear thinking often accompanies disorientation but is not identical to it, because confusion describes disordered thinking in general whereas disorientation specifically means losing the bearings of time, place or person.
  10. A patient can be transiently confused under stress yet remain fully oriented, so the clinician separates a muddled train of thought from true disorientation, since only the latter carries the strong organic warning.
  11. Orientation is tested with simple direct questions about the day, date, season, place and the identity of people present, a quick examination that nonetheless yields some of the most diagnostically weighty information at the bedside.
  12. Errors should be judged sensibly, because being a day out on the date in a long hospital stay is trivial, whereas naming the wrong year or decade is a serious sign of a significant disturbance of orientation.
  13. In acute organic states orientation typically fluctuates, being clearer in the morning and worse at night, so repeated testing across the day reveals the instability that is itself characteristic of delirium.
  14. A false or confabulated orientation can occur, where an amnesic patient confidently but wrongly states the date or place, so apparent certainty does not guarantee that the answer is correct or that orientation is intact.
  15. Because orientation integrates several functions, it serves as a compact index of global brain function, which is why it features in almost every cognitive screening test used in psychiatry and general medicine.
  16. Disorientation to person in a patient otherwise alert and oriented to time and place is a red flag for a dissociative state, since this reversed pattern breaks the usual organic order and suggests a psychogenic cause.
  17. In one line, disorders of orientation are losses of awareness of time, place and person, usually in that order, and their acute appearance is one of psychiatry's clearest signs of organic cerebral disturbance.

Disorders of Motor Behavior

Movement can itself become a symptom, so this topic classifies disordered motor behaviour from the patient's and observer's view, details the classic catatonic signs, and distinguishes illness-driven movements from those produced by the drugs used to treat it.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of motor disorder → Subjective vs observed; classes of movement → Catatonic phenomena → Stupor and excitement → Drug-induced movement disorders → One-line summary
  1. Disorders of motor behaviour are abnormalities of movement, posture and activity that arise from mental illness rather than from simple weakness or paralysis, so they are read as outward signs of an inner psychological or organic disturbance.
  2. Movements can be described from the subjective side, how the patient experiences them, and the objective side, what the observer sees, and the two together build the full clinical picture of a motor disorder.
  3. A key subjective experience is passivity the feeling that one's movements are made by an outside force, where the patient believes their limbs are being moved for them, a phenomenon strongly associated with schizophrenia.
  4. Movements can be grouped as adaptive purposeful, goal-directed acts, non-adaptive purposeless movements serving no goal, and induced movements that appear only in response to the examiner, and this grouping organises the whole examination.
  5. Catatonia a syndrome of marked motor disturbance is the richest source of these signs, classically occurring in severe psychosis and mood disorder and, importantly, also arising from organic brain disease, so it always warrants a medical work-up.
  6. Echopraxia automatically copying the examiner's movements and echolalia automatically repeating the examiner's words are catatonic signs in which the patient imitates without being asked and seemingly without being able to stop.
  7. Automatic obedience is the exaggerated, uncritical carrying out of every instruction, so the patient complies with even trivial or odd commands, a loss of the normal filtering of what one chooses to do.
  8. Negativism motiveless resistance to all instructions, often doing the opposite is the mirror image of automatic obedience, where the patient actively opposes or ignores what is asked without any apparent reason.
  9. Ambitendency starting a movement then checking it, caught between doing and not doing shows as a hesitant, to-and-fro motion, as when a patient half-extends a hand to shake and then withdraws it repeatedly.
  10. Waxy flexibility (cerea flexibilitas) limbs that can be moulded like wax and held in position lets the examiner place the patient's arm in an awkward posture that is then maintained for long periods without apparent discomfort.
  11. Stupor is a marked reduction or absence of movement and speech with the patient still conscious, so unlike coma the person remains aware and may later recall events, and it can be organic, schizophrenic or depressive.
  12. At the opposite pole, excitement is a state of excessive, often purposeless overactivity, ranging from the goal-driven overactivity of mania to the chaotic, bizarre excitement seen in catatonic and organic states.
  13. Mannerisms odd but goal-directed movements, like an elaborate way of saluting are distinguished from stereotypies repetitive, uniform movements with no goal, such as constant rocking, a distinction that helps localise the kind of disturbance.
  14. Tics sudden, brief, repetitive involuntary movements or sounds and compulsive acts are further motor phenomena, the former usually neurological and the latter driven by an inner urge the patient feels compelled to obey.
  15. Many motor signs today are drug-induced, because antipsychotic medicines block dopamine and so produce movement disorders that the clinician must not mistake for the illness itself when assessing the patient.
  16. The most important of these is tardive dyskinesia late-appearing, often irreversible involuntary movements of the face, tongue and limbs, alongside acute dystonia, parkinsonism and the restless legs of akathisia caused by antipsychotic treatment.
  17. In one line, disorders of motor behaviour span passivity, the catatonic signs, stupor and excitement, and the movement disorders induced by antipsychotic drugs, each requiring the clinician to separate illness from treatment and functional from organic causes.

Disorders of the Experience of Self

The sense of being a single, continuous, self-directed person with firm boundaries is normally taken for granted, so this topic uses Jaspers' four aspects of the self to map how that sense breaks down in depersonalisation, passivity phenomena and related states.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of the experience of self → Jaspers' four aspects → Depersonalisation & derealisation → Passivity phenomena → Significance (self vs world) → One-line summary
  1. The experience of self (ego) is the immediate awareness that it is *I* who think, feel and act, a certainty so basic it is never noticed until illness makes it falter and the patient feels strangely unreal.
  2. Karl Jaspers analysed this awareness into four formal aspects, a framework that lets the clinician say exactly which part of the sense of self has broken down rather than describing the disturbance only in vague terms.
  3. The first aspect is the awareness of activity and existence the sense that I am the one doing my actions and that I exist, which is weakened in depersonalisation when patients feel detached, automatic or as if they are not really there.
  4. The second aspect is the awareness of unity the sense of being one single person at any moment, whose disturbance can produce the rare experience of a double self, as though two people inhabited the same body at once.
  5. The third aspect is the awareness of continuity the sense of being the same person over time, which can be disrupted after a psychotic break so that patients feel they are no longer the person they were before the illness.
  6. The fourth aspect is the awareness of the boundaries of the self a clear line between me and the outside world, whose breakdown is central to the passivity phenomena of schizophrenia, where the border between self and world dissolves.
  7. Depersonalisation a distressing feeling of being unreal, detached or watching oneself from outside is the commonest disorder of the self, and crucially the patient keeps insight, knowing the feeling is strange and not literally true.
  8. Derealisation a feeling that the surrounding world is unreal, flat or dreamlike usually accompanies depersonalisation, so the patient reports that both themselves and their environment seem artificial, distant or lacking in their usual vividness.
  9. Depersonalisation and derealisation are non-specific, arising in anxiety, depression, tiredness, epilepsy and normal people under stress, so they point to the quality of the experience rather than to any single diagnosis.
  10. Passivity phenomena are disorders of the self-boundary in which the patient experiences their own thoughts, feelings, impulses or movements as made by an external force, so the normal ownership of inner life is lost.
  11. These include made actions, made feelings and made impulses, where patients believe an outside agency is controlling what they do, feel or want, and such experiences were classically treated as first-rank symptoms of schizophrenia.
  12. Thought alienation is a related loss of boundary, covering thought insertion thoughts put into the mind by others, withdrawal thoughts taken out and broadcasting one's thoughts escaping to others, all breaching the privacy of the self.
  13. A useful contrast is that in depersonalisation insight is retained, whereas in passivity phenomena insight is lost and the experience is held with delusional conviction, which places the two on very different levels of severity.
  14. Disturbances of the self also bear on theory of mind the ability to understand that others have their own separate thoughts and feelings, which is impaired in schizophrenia and autism and helps explain some difficulties in reading other people.
  15. A clinical warning is that depersonalisation is often under-reported, because patients find the feeling so hard to put into words and fear sounding mad, so the clinician may need to ask about it directly and sympathetically.
  16. The self-disorders form a rough hierarchy of severity, from transient depersonalisation with full insight, through persistent depersonalisation, to the insight-less passivity phenomena that signal severe psychotic illness.
  17. In one line, disorders of the experience of self are breakdowns in Jaspers' four aspects of activity, unity, continuity and boundaries, ranging from insightful depersonalisation to the boundary-dissolving passivity phenomena of schizophrenia.
Sourced characterisation — Depersonalisation
Depersonalisation is described as an unpleasant state in which the patient feels changed, unreal or detached from their own experience, as if observing themselves from outside, while retaining insight that the change is subjective and not an actual alteration of the self.
Source: Paraphrased from Casey, P. & Kelly, B., Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed. (chapter on disorders of the experience of self), after Jaspers.

Disorders of Speech

Speech is the chief window onto thought, so this topic separates disorders of the mechanics of talking from disorders of its content, covering stammering and mutism, the odd productions of psychosis, and the organic language failures known as the aphasias.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of speech disorder → Rhythm & output problems → Mutism (its types) → Psychotic speech productions → Aphasias (receptive vs expressive) → One-line summary
  1. Disorders of speech are abnormalities of the spoken output itself, and although speech and thought are closely tied, disordered speech can arise either from disturbed thinking or from a fault in the language machinery of the brain.
  2. Stammering (stuttering) the halting repetition or blocking of sounds and syllables is a disorder of the rhythm of speech, often worsened by anxiety, in which the flow of words is broken even though the person knows exactly what they wish to say.
  3. Mutism a complete absence of speech with the organs of speech intact is a striking sign that can arise in several very different conditions, so its type must be identified before its cause can be understood.
  4. Elective (selective) mutism describes a person, often a child, who speaks normally in some settings but refuses to speak in others, so the silence is situational rather than a loss of the capacity for speech.
  5. Catatonic mutism occurs as part of the catatonic syndrome, where the patient does not speak despite being conscious, while akinetic mutism silent immobility from damage near the brain's midline is an organic state of near-total lack of speech and movement.
  6. Vorbeireden, or talking past the point giving answers that are obviously wrong but clearly related to the question, is seen in the Ganser state, as when a patient asked the colour of grass replies that it is blue.
  7. Neologisms brand-new words invented by the patient with a private meaning are a sign of severe disturbance, typically schizophrenia, where the patient creates words that carry no shared meaning for the listener.
  8. Word salad (schizophasia) a jumble of real words and phrases strung together without grammar or sense is an extreme disorganisation of speech in which the connections between words are so lost that no meaning can be followed.
  9. Perseveration of speech, the inappropriate repetition of a previous word or phrase, and echolalia, the automatic echoing of the examiner's words, are further signs that may point to either organic disease or severe psychosis.
  10. A separate major group is the aphasias losses of language caused by damage to the brain's language areas, which are organic disorders of comprehension or production rather than disturbances of the content of thought.
  11. Receptive (Wernicke's) aphasia follows damage to the temporal language area, producing fluent speech that flows easily but is empty of meaning, together with a severe failure to understand what others say.
  12. Expressive (Broca's) aphasia follows damage to the frontal language area, producing non-fluent, effortful, telegraphic speech while comprehension is relatively preserved, so the patient understands but cannot fluently produce words.
  13. Distinguishing an aphasia from a thought disorder is a key clinical skill, because fluent empty Wernicke's speech can superficially resemble the word salad of schizophrenia yet points to a focal brain lesion needing neurological investigation.
  14. Dysarthria slurred speech from poor control of the speech muscles must also be separated from aphasia, because dysarthria disturbs the clarity of articulation while leaving the choice and meaning of words intact.
  15. The tempo of speech is itself informative, being fast and hard to interrupt as pressure of speech in mania, and slow, sparse and delayed as poverty of speech in depression and in the negative symptoms of schizophrenia.
  16. Careful observation of speech therefore lets the examiner infer the underlying process, since the form of the output, fluent or halting, pressured or impoverished, novel or echoed, often reveals more than the literal content of what is said.
  17. In one line, disorders of speech range from stammering and the several mutisms, through the neologisms and word salad of psychosis, to the receptive and expressive aphasias of focal brain damage.

Disorders of Thought

Thought is examined along four separate lines, so this topic sets out disorders of the stream, the possession, the content and the form of thinking, keeping delusions, obsessions, flight of ideas and formal thought disorder clearly apart.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition & the four lines → Disorders of stream (tempo) → Disorders of possession → Disorders of content (delusions) → Disorders of form (FTD) → One-line summary
  1. Disorders of thought are best organised under four separate headings, the stream, the possession, the content and the form of thinking, because a patient may have a disorder of one of these while the others remain entirely normal.
  2. Disorders of the stream concern the tempo and flow of thought, that is how fast thoughts come and how smoothly one leads to the next, independent of whether the thoughts themselves are true or false.
  3. Flight of ideas thoughts racing so fast that speech jumps between loosely linked topics is the classic stream disorder of mania, where the connections, though rapid, can usually still be traced through rhymes, puns and chance associations.
  4. Inhibition or retardation of thinking is the opposite, a slowing of the stream seen in depression, where thoughts come sparsely and effortfully and the patient describes their mind as empty, heavy or stuck.
  5. Circumstantiality reaching the point only after many unnecessary details and digressions keeps the goal of thought in view but approaches it by a long, winding route, eventually arriving where the question intended.
  6. Perseveration senseless repetition of a thought or word beyond the point of relevance and thought blocking a sudden halt where the thread of thought vanishes entirely are further stream disorders, the latter strongly suggestive of schizophrenia.
  7. Disorders of the possession of thought concern whether thinking feels owned and controlled by the self, and they cover both the obsessional phenomena and the alienation of thought seen in psychosis.
  8. Obsessions recurrent unwanted thoughts the person recognises as their own and resists and compulsions repetitive acts done to relieve the anxiety of an obsession are possession disorders in which control, not ownership, is lost.
  9. By contrast, thought alienation is a loss of ownership, covering insertion, withdrawal and broadcasting, where the patient feels thoughts are not their own, a far more severe disturbance typical of schizophrenia.
  10. Disorders of the content of thought concern what is believed, and chief among them is the delusion a fixed false belief held firmly despite clear contrary evidence and out of keeping with the person's culture.
  11. A primary (autochthonous) delusion arises fully formed out of nowhere, not understandable from the patient's mood or situation, whereas a secondary delusion grows understandably from a preceding mood state, perception or other experience.
  12. Delusions are further grouped by theme, including persecutory, grandiose, of reference, of guilt, nihilistic, hypochondriacal and of jealousy or love, and the dominant theme often reflects the underlying illness.
  13. Overvalued ideas strongly held, understandable beliefs pursued beyond reason but short of delusional conviction lie between normal beliefs and delusions, and distinguishing them matters because they imply a different diagnosis and prognosis.
  14. Disorders of the form of thought, or formal thought disorder a breakdown in the logical connections between thoughts, show in disorganised speech and are inferred because thought itself cannot be observed directly.
  15. Signs of formal thought disorder include loosening of associations ideas that drift apart with no clear link and the knight's move (derailment) sudden jumps from one topic to an unrelated one, both pointing toward schizophrenia.
  16. Keeping these four lines apart is a core exam skill, since flight of ideas is a stream disorder of mania whereas loosening of associations is a form disorder of schizophrenia, and confusing them points to the wrong diagnosis.
  17. In one line, disorders of thought divide into the stream, the possession, the content and the form, housing respectively flight of ideas, obsessions and alienation, delusions, and formal thought disorder.
Sourced characterisation — Delusion
A delusion is a false, unshakeable belief that is held with absolute conviction, is not amenable to reason or contrary evidence, and is out of keeping with the patient's social, cultural and educational background; it may be primary (arising inexplicably) or secondary (arising understandably from another experience).
Source: Paraphrased from Casey, P. & Kelly, B., Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed. (chapter on disorders of thought and belief), after Jaspers.

Disorders of Perception

Perception can go wrong in graded ways, so this topic moves from sensory distortions of real objects, through illusions, to hallucinations proper, and sorts them by modality while distinguishing true hallucinations from pseudohallucinations.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition of perceptual disorder → Sensory distortions → Illusions → Hallucinations (by modality) → Pseudohallucinations & special types → One-line summary
  1. Disorders of perception are faults in how the mind builds its picture of the world from the senses, graded from distortions of a real object, through misinterpretations, to perceptions with no object at all.
  2. Sensory distortions are changes in how a real object is perceived, altering its intensity, quality or spatial form, as in hyperacusis sounds seeming abnormally loud or micropsia objects seeming smaller than they are.
  3. Distortions of intensity make stimuli seem stronger or weaker, distortions of quality change colour or tone, and spatial distortions change apparent size, shape or distance, each pointing to specific drug or organic causes.
  4. An illusion a real external object that is misperceived or misinterpreted differs from a distortion because the whole object is read wrongly, as when a shadow is taken for an intruder, and illusions are commoner when attention is low or affect is strong.
  5. Illusions are usually normal phenomena, arising in poor light, fatigue, fear or inattention, so they carry far less diagnostic weight than hallucinations and often resolve as soon as attention is properly directed to the object.
  6. A hallucination is the key perceptual disorder, classically defined as a perception without an object, arising in external space with the full force of a true perception yet corresponding to nothing actually present.
  7. Auditory hallucinations are the commonest in psychiatry, ranging from simple noises to voices, and in schizophrenia the voices may give a running commentary, speak in the third person, or repeat the patient's own thoughts aloud.
  8. Visual hallucinations are more suggestive of an organic or drug-induced state such as delirium tremens, so their presence should prompt a search for a physical cause rather than being assumed to indicate a primary psychiatric illness.
  9. Olfactory and gustatory hallucinations of smell and taste, often unpleasant, point toward temporal-lobe epilepsy or organic disease, while tactile (haptic) hallucinations include the sensation of insects crawling on or under the skin.
  10. Hallucinations are further classed by form: an elementary hallucination is a simple flash or bang, whereas a complex one is a fully formed voice, figure or scene, the latter carrying more specific diagnostic meaning.
  11. Pseudohallucinations vivid images experienced in inner subjective space and recognised as not real differ from true hallucinations because they lack the quality of an external percept and the patient retains insight into their unreal nature.
  12. Several special hallucinations are defined by their trigger or timing, such as functional hallucinations a voice heard only while a real sound is present and the hypnagogic and hypnopompic hallucinations that occur while falling asleep or waking.
  13. Charles Bonnet syndrome is the occurrence of complex visual hallucinations in people with serious eye disease but a clear mind and full insight, a benign condition that must not be mistaken for a psychotic illness.
  14. Autoscopy seeing a double of oneself and extracampine hallucinations a perception located outside the possible sensory field, such as hearing a voice from hundreds of miles away are further special forms worth recognising.
  15. Disorders of body image belong here too, including the phantom limb feeling a limb that has been amputated and distortions in which parts of the body feel changed in size, shape or position.
  16. Distinguishing an illusion from a hallucination is a core skill, since an illusion needs a real object to misinterpret whereas a hallucination needs none, and only the latter, especially when complex and insight-less, strongly signals serious illness.
  17. In one line, disorders of perception run from distortions of real objects, through illusions, to true hallucinations sorted by modality, with pseudohallucinations and special types such as Charles Bonnet rounding out the picture.
Sourced characterisation — Hallucination
A hallucination is a perception without an object — a percept experienced in external space, with the full force and clarity of a true perception, but arising in the absence of any actual external stimulus and not subject to conscious manipulation by the perceiver.
Source: Paraphrased from Casey, P. & Kelly, B., Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed. (chapter on disorders of perception); the 'perception without an object' formulation is attributed there to Esquirol / Jaspers.

Disorders of Emotion

Emotional life has several layers and many ways of going wrong, so this topic first separates feeling, emotion, mood and affect, then grades disturbance across five levels from normal reactions to the morbid disorders of depression, mania and anxiety.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definitions (feeling/emotion/mood/affect) → The five levels of disturbance → Abnormal reactions & expressions → Morbid disorders (depression/mania) → Anxiety and ecstasy → One-line summary
  1. Clear terms matter: a feeling is a brief positive or negative state, an emotion a stirred-up feeling with bodily change, mood the prevailing background over time, and affect the emotion shown in the moment.
  2. A helpful image is that mood is the climate while affect is the weather, so the examiner records both the sustained background the patient reports and the moment-to-moment emotional expression that is observed during the interview.
  3. Fish grades emotional disturbance across five levels, running from normal emotions, through abnormal reactions and abnormal expressions, to morbid expressions and finally the morbid disorders of emotion, giving a clear ladder of increasing severity.
  4. Level one is simply normal emotion, appropriate joy, fear, sadness or anger that fits its trigger in kind, strength and duration, providing the baseline against which every abnormality is judged.
  5. Level two, abnormal emotional reactions, are responses that are understandable in kind but too intense or too prolonged for their trigger, such as grief that is far deeper or far longer than the loss would ordinarily warrant.
  6. Level three, abnormal expressions of emotion, includes incongruity of affect emotion that clashes with the situation, such as laughing at sad news, emotional lability rapid shifts of emotion, and perplexity, a bewildered emotional state.
  7. Level four, morbid expressions, covers apathy a loss of feeling and interest and blunting or flattening of affect a marked reduction in the range and intensity of emotional expression, both prominent in the negative symptoms of schizophrenia.
  8. Level five, the morbid disorders of emotion, are the sustained pathological mood states of depression, mania, pathological anxiety and ecstasy, which form the core of the affective disorders seen in psychiatry.
  9. In depression the mood is persistently low with anhedonia loss of the ability to feel pleasure, hopelessness, guilt and often diurnal variation, so the whole emotional field is pulled down and fails to lift with good events.
  10. In mania the mood is abnormally elevated, expansive or irritable, with elation that is often infectious but brittle, tipping quickly into irritability when the patient's grandiose plans are thwarted.
  11. Pathological anxiety is fear or dread out of proportion to any real threat, with bodily symptoms such as palpitations, sweating and trembling, and it becomes a disorder when it is persistent, distressing and impairs the person's functioning.
  12. Ecstasy an intense feeling of rapture, bliss or oneness with the world is a rarer morbid emotional state that can occur in mania, in some epilepsies and in certain religious or drug-induced experiences, and is often held with conviction.
  13. A distinction worth stressing is between endogenous-type mood change, seeming to arise from within with biological features, and reactive mood change clearly triggered by events, a contrast that historically guided treatment choices.
  14. The clinician assesses emotion through subjective report, what the patient says they feel, and objective observation of facial expression, posture, tears and movement, because these two sources can diverge, as in the smiling, hidden depression.
  15. Emotional symptoms must be judged against cultural norms for expressing feeling, since what looks like blunting or over-expression in one culture may be entirely normal in another, so context guards against over-diagnosis.
  16. Because emotional change colours thought, perception and behaviour, a secondary delusion or an illusion may grow understandably out of a morbid mood, which is why mood is always assessed alongside the other domains rather than in isolation.
  17. In one line, disorders of emotion are graded across five levels from abnormal reactions to morbid disorders, the latter comprising depression, mania, pathological anxiety and ecstasy, each judged against normal, culturally appropriate feeling.

Disorders of Memory

Memory is a chain of linked stages, so this topic defines registration, retention, retrieval and recall, then surveys the amnesias, the distinction between organic and psychogenic memory loss, and the paramnesias in which memory is falsified rather than lost.

Answer flow · write your answer in this order
Definition & the memory stages → The amnesias → Organic vs psychogenic loss → Ribot's law → Paramnesias (falsified memory) → One-line summary
  1. Memory is the capacity to store and later use information, and it is usefully broken into four linked stages, registration, retention, retrieval and recall, since a fault at any one stage will present as a memory complaint.
  2. Registration taking information in depends on intact attention and consciousness, so a patient who cannot attend never truly registers material, and what was never registered cannot later be remembered however hard they try.
  3. Retention holding information in store over time and retrieval finding stored information again are the middle stages, and disorders can strike either the storing of new memories or the reaching back for old ones.
  4. Recall bringing stored information back to mind is the final, testable stage, and because it is what the examiner directly observes, a failure of recall must be traced back to see which earlier stage actually broke down.
  5. Amnesia loss of memory is the central disorder, and a key split is between anterograde amnesia failure to lay down new memories after an event and retrograde amnesia loss of memories formed before the event.
  6. In the classic amnesic (Korsakoff) syndrome, severe anterograde amnesia from thiamine deficiency leaves the patient unable to form new memories while older memories and immediate attention are relatively spared, often with confabulation.
  7. A crucial clinical distinction is between organic memory loss, which follows the pattern of brain pathology, and psychogenic memory loss, which is driven by psychological factors and often spares the kind of information organic amnesia would lose.
  8. Psychogenic amnesia characteristically targets personal, emotionally charged memories, as in dissociative amnesia and fugue, so a patient may forget their own identity yet retain general knowledge, a pattern organic disease rarely produces.
  9. By contrast, organic amnesia typically spares deep personal identity until very late while heavily damaging the laying down of new facts, so the two patterns are in many ways mirror images of each other.
  10. Ribot's law states that in organic memory loss the most recent memories are lost first and the oldest, best-rehearsed memories survive longest, giving a predictable gradient that helps confirm an organic cause.
  11. This recency gradient explains why a patient with dementia may vividly recount their childhood while being unable to recall the morning's conversation, a pattern that is diagnostically reassuring of an organic rather than a feigned loss.
  12. A separate group are the paramnesias distortions or falsifications of memory, in which memory is not simply lost but altered, so the patient remembers things that did not happen or misremembers things that did.
  13. Confabulation filling gaps in memory with invented but sincerely believed accounts is the classic paramnesia of the amnesic syndrome, where the patient unknowingly produces false memories to bridge what they cannot recall.
  14. Deja vu the false sense that a new situation has been experienced before and its opposite jamais vu a familiar situation feeling strange and new are paramnesias that occur normally but may be prominent in temporal-lobe epilepsy.
  15. The Capgras delusion, in which a familiar person is believed to be an identical impostor, is a disorder at the border of memory and recognition, reflecting a split between knowing a face and feeling its familiarity.
  16. At the opposite extreme are hyperamnesia abnormally intense, detailed memory and the vivid flashbulb memory of an emotionally charged event, showing that memory can be pathologically heightened as well as lost or falsified.
  17. In one line, disorders of memory are failures or falsifications along the chain of registration, retention, retrieval and recall, divided into organic and psychogenic amnesias, Ribot's recency gradient, and the paramnesias such as confabulation.
