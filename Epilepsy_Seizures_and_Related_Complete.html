1. Basic concepts — definitions & comparison

  • Seizure (epileptic seizure): a transient occurrence of signs and/or symptoms due to abnormal excessive or hypersynchronous neuronal activity in the brain (ILAE). The clinical form depends on where the discharge starts and how it spreads.
  • Epilepsy: a disease of the brain defined (ILAE operational definition) by any one of: (1) at least two unprovoked (or reflex) seizures >24 hours apart; (2) one unprovoked seizure with a probability of further seizures similar to the general recurrence risk (about ≥60%) over 10 years; or (3) diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome.
  • Acute symptomatic (provoked) seizure: a seizure occurring in close temporal relationship to an acute insult (e.g. metabolic derangement, intoxication/withdrawal, acute stroke, CNS infection, head injury). This is not epilepsy — treat the cause.
  • Unprovoked seizure: a seizure with no identifiable acute precipitant (or due to a pre-existing static/remote cause). Two of these, or one with high recurrence risk, define epilepsy.
  • Reflex seizure: consistently triggered by a specific stimulus (e.g. flashing lights — photosensitivity).
  • Seizure disorder: a lay/clinical umbrella term often used interchangeably with epilepsy; less precise.
  • Status epilepticus: a prolonged seizure or repeated seizures without recovery between them — a neurological emergency (see §18).
  • Nonepileptic event: a paroxysmal episode resembling a seizure but not caused by abnormal epileptic neuronal discharge (e.g. syncope, PNES, parasomnia, movement disorder).
  • Psychogenic nonepileptic seizure (PNES): a paroxysmal episode resembling an epileptic seizure but arising from psychological/functional mechanisms, without the ictal EEG discharge of epilepsy.
  • Dissociative seizure: the ICD/functional-neurological framing of the same events, emphasising dissociation (a disruption of the normal integration of awareness, memory, movement) as the mechanism.

2. Terminology rules — “pseudoseizure”, PNES and dissociative seizure

Why “pseudoseizure” is discouraged

The word “pseudoseizure” is discouraged in current practice. It implies the event is “fake” or that the person is deliberately pretending — which is incorrect and stigmatising. The preferred terms are:

  • Psychogenic nonepileptic seizure (PNES) — the widely used neurological/neuropsychiatric term.
  • Dissociative seizure / dissociative (convulsive) neurological symptom disorder — the ICD-11 / functional-neurological framing, emphasising the dissociative mechanism.
  • In DSM-5-TR these events fall under Functional Neurological Symptom Disorder (conversion disorder), with attacks or seizures.

The essential clinical truth

A PNES / dissociative seizure is a real, involuntary clinical event that causes genuine distress and disability — but it is NOT caused by the abnormal epileptic neuronal activity that defines an epileptic seizure. The person is not intentionally faking. It is an involuntary response arising from functional/dissociative brain processes.

Distinguish clearly from intentional simulation. Factitious disorder = symptoms intentionally produced/feigned, motivated internally (to adopt the sick role). Malingering = intentional feigning for an obvious external gain (money, avoiding duty/prosecution) — not a mental disorder. PNES / dissociative seizures are neither — the events are not under voluntary control. Confusing these is a serious clinical and ethical error.

3. Normal brain activity vs seizure activity

Normal neuronal activity

Neurons fire in a coordinated but desynchronized way, with a tight balance between excitation (chiefly the neurotransmitter glutamate, acting on AMPA/NMDA receptors) and inhibition (chiefly GABA, acting on GABA-A/GABA-B receptors). Ion channels for sodium (Na+), calcium (Ca2+) and potassium (K+) shape each action potential and its recovery. This balance keeps networks stable.

Seizure = hypersynchronous activity

A seizure occurs when a population of neurons fires excessively and in synchrony. This arises from a shift toward too much excitation and/or too little inhibition — e.g. excess glutamate, reduced GABA, or channel dysfunction (a “channelopathy”). The abnormal discharge can stay focal or propagate along cortical and thalamocortical networks to involve both hemispheres.

  • Glutamate ↑ / GABA ↓ → hyperexcitability.
  • Sodium-channel dysfunction → sustained depolarisation (many antiseizure drugs block Na+ channels).
  • T-type calcium channels in the thalamocortical network underlie the 3-Hz spike-wave of absence seizures (ethosuximide acts here).
  • Potassium channels normally repolarise/terminate firing; dysfunction impairs seizure termination.
  • Neuronal synchronization across cortical networks converts local hyperexcitability into a clinical seizure; the site of onset and pattern of spread determine the semiology.

4. Pathophysiology of seizures

The cascade. Neuronal hyperexcitability → hypersynchronous discharge → network propagation → clinical seizure. Whether the event stays focal or generalizes depends on the balance of local excitation/inhibition and the connectivity of the involved networks.

  • Focal cortical onset: a discrete group of neurons generates a paroxysmal depolarizing shift and fires in synchrony.
  • Local spread: surrounding inhibition (the “inhibitory surround”) normally contains it; if it fails, activity spreads to adjacent cortex (the classic Jacksonian march of a focal motor seizure).
  • Bilateral propagation: spread via commissural and thalamocortical pathways can recruit both hemispheres → focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure.
  • Thalamocortical networks: generalized onset seizures (e.g. absence) engage these circuits from the start, producing bilaterally synchronous EEG discharges.

Clinical manifestations by network involved:

  • Motor (primary motor cortex) → clonic jerking, tonic stiffening, automatisms.
  • Sensory (sensory cortex) → tingling, numbness, visual/auditory phenomena.
  • Autonomic (insula, mesial temporal) → epigastric rising sensation, palpitations, flushing, piloerection.
  • Cognitive → déjà vu/jamais vu, dysphasia, confusion.
  • Emotional (amygdala/limbic) → sudden ictal fear, rarely pleasure or laughter (gelastic seizures).

6. The ILAE epilepsy classification framework (three levels + etiology)

The 2017 ILAE framework classifies at three sequential levels, with etiology and comorbidities considered at every step. These are not interchangeable concepts.

Seizure type → Epilepsy type → Epilepsy syndrome (with etiology throughout).

  • Level 1 — Seizure type: classify the individual seizure as focal onset, generalized onset, or unknown onset (see §7).
  • Level 2 — Epilepsy type: classify the person's epilepsy as focal, generalized, combined generalized & focal, or unknown.
  • Level 3 — Epilepsy syndrome: where features cluster into a recognised syndrome (e.g. juvenile myoclonic epilepsy) with characteristic age, seizure types, EEG, and prognosis.
  • Etiology (cross-cutting): structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, unknown — determined as early as possible because it drives treatment.

Why the distinction matters

A person can have a single seizure type but no epilepsy (a provoked seizure); can have an epilepsy type without a named syndrome; and can have a syndrome that predicts prognosis and drug choice (e.g. avoiding sodium-channel blockers in some generalized epilepsies). Conflating “seizure type” with “epilepsy syndrome” leads to mis-treatment.

7. Seizure classification (ILAE 2017)

The first question is always where does the seizure begin? — focal onset (one hemisphere/network), generalized onset (bilateral networks from the start), or unknown onset. Then classify by awareness (for focal) and by the first prominent feature (motor vs non-motor).

· Focal onset seizures

Begin in networks limited to one hemisphere. Classified first by awareness:

  • Focal aware seizure (old term: simple partial) — awareness is fully retained throughout; the person can recall the event. Often an “aura” (which is itself a focal aware seizure).
  • Focal impaired awareness seizure (old term: complex partial) — awareness is impaired at any point; the person is typically amnesic for part or all of the event.
  • Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure (old term: secondarily generalized) — begins focally then spreads to both hemispheres as a convulsion.

Then classified by the first prominent feature: motor onset or non-motor onset.

Experiential phenomena & the temporal lobe

Déjà vu, jamais vu, the epigastric rising aura, ictal fear, and complex hallucinatory/experiential phenomena are strongly associated with mesial temporal-lobe networks (amygdala/hippocampus). They are genuine focal aware seizures — the person is awake and can often describe them — and they may herald a focal impaired-awareness seizure or a focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizure.

· Generalized onset seizures

Engage bilateral networks from the start; awareness is usually (though not always) impaired. Divided into motor and non-motor (absence).

  • Motor: tonic-clonic, clonic, tonic, myoclonic, myoclonic-tonic-clonic, myoclonic-atonic, atonic, epileptic spasms.
  • Non-motor (absence): typical, atypical, myoclonic absence, absence with eyelid myoclonia.

· Generalized tonic-clonic seizure (GTCS) — in detail

The full sequence

  • Preictal / prodrome: hours before, some report irritability or a vague sense that a seizure is coming (not the same as an aura).
  • Ictal — tonic phase (~10–20 s): abrupt loss of consciousness, generalized tonic stiffening, often a vocalization/“epileptic cry” (air forced through a closed glottis), the person falls, apnoea and cyanosis may occur, jaw clenches (risk of lateral tongue biting).
  • Ictal — clonic phase (~30–60 s): rhythmic, symmetric jerking of the limbs that slows and stops; autonomic changes (tachycardia, hypertension, sweating, salivation/frothing); urinary (or faecal) incontinence may occur.
  • Postictal: confusion, deep sleep, fatigue, headache, muscle aches (myalgia), amnesia for the event, and sometimes transient focal weakness (Todd's paresis if focal onset).
Supportive but NOT individually diagnostic. Lateral tongue biting, urinary incontinence, marked postictal confusion, cyanosis and elevated post-event lactate/creatine kinase raise the probability of an epileptic GTCS — but no single one proves it (e.g. incontinence can occur in syncope; tongue biting can occur in PNES, though tip-biting is more typical there). Use the whole picture.

Remember

GTCS = Cry → stiffen (tonic) → jerk (clonic) → postictal confusion/sleep, with LOC throughout, lateral tongue bite and incontinence common but not proof.

· Absence seizures

Typical vs atypical

  • Typical absence: abrupt onset and offset of a brief (usually <10 s) behavioural arrest and staring, with impaired awareness; no aura, no postictal confusion; may have subtle automatisms (lip movements) or eyelid flutter. Often provoked by hyperventilation. EEG: generalized 3-Hz spike-and-wave. Seen in childhood/juvenile absence epilepsy.
  • Atypical absence: less abrupt onset/offset, longer, with more tone change (head drop) and often occurring in developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (e.g. Lennox-Gastaut). EEG: slower (<2.5 Hz) spike-wave.
  • Absence with eyelid myoclonia and myoclonic absence are recognised variants.
Differential diagnosis (crucial — absence is often missed or over-diagnosed)

From daydreaming/inattentiveness (can be interrupted by touch/calling; no true impairment or EEG discharge); from focal impaired awareness seizures (longer, with aura and postictal confusion, and often automatisms — absence has abrupt on/off and no postictal phase); from dissociation and PNES (context, no EEG discharge); from ADHD inattention.

Remember

Absence = brief, abrupt on/off staring, no aura, no postictal confusion, 3-Hz spike-wave, provoked by hyperventilation. Contrast with focal impaired-awareness (aura + automatisms + postictal confusion).

· Myoclonic seizures

  • Positive myoclonus: sudden brief muscle contraction (shock-like jerk); negative myoclonus: brief loss of tone (a lapse, e.g. dropping an object).
  • Consciousness is usually preserved during isolated jerks.
  • Timing: classically on awakening / in the morning; provoked by sleep deprivation, fatigue, alcohol and flashing lights.
  • Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (JME): the archetype — morning myoclonic jerks (often spilling drinks/dropping toothbrush), plus GTCS and sometimes absences; onset in adolescence; typically lifelong but drug-responsive.

Remember

“Morning jerks, spills the coffee, worse after a late night” — think myoclonic seizures / JME.

· Atonic seizures (“drop attacks”)

  • Sudden loss of muscle tone → head drop or collapse/fall, usually very brief, with high injury risk (facial/head trauma — protective helmets sometimes used).
  • Associated syndromes: Lennox-Gastaut syndrome and other developmental & epileptic encephalopathies.
Differential diagnosis

From syncope (prodrome, pallor, cardiovascular trigger), cataplexy (emotion-triggered tone loss with preserved awareness, in narcolepsy), and atonic PNES.

· Epileptic spasms

  • Clinical appearance: brief (~1–2 s) flexor, extensor, or mixed contractions of the trunk and limbs, characteristically occurring in clusters (often on waking).
  • Age: typically infancy (peak 3–12 months), but can occur later.
  • Developmental implications: often associated with developmental regression/stagnation.
  • Syndrome: Infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS) — formerly West syndrome: the classic triad is epileptic spasms + developmental regression + a chaotic high-voltage EEG called hypsarrhythmia. Early recognition and treatment matter for outcome.

Remember

West syndrome triad: spasms (in clusters) + developmental regression + hypsarrhythmia. Now called infantile epileptic spasms syndrome (IESS).

· Unknown-onset seizures

  • Why onset may be unknown: the event was unwitnessed, occurred in sleep, or the beginning was not observed/recalled.
  • Limitations of eyewitness observation: lay witnesses often miss or misdescribe the onset; the most reliable data come from video and EEG.
  • Reclassification: a seizure classified as “unknown onset” can be reclassified later (as focal or generalized) once more information — a witnessed onset, EEG, or imaging — becomes available.

9. Localization — what semiology suggests (and its limits)

Semiology localizes imperfectly. Seizure symptoms reflect the networks recruited, not only the site of onset; seizures spread rapidly, so the first clinical sign may arise from propagated (not onset) cortex. Localization from semiology is probabilistic and must be integrated with EEG and imaging — never treat it as exact.

· Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE)

Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy (the commonest focal epilepsy in adults)

  • Often associated with hippocampal (mesial temporal) sclerosis, sometimes with a history of childhood febrile seizures.
  • Aura: classic epigastric rising sensation, ictal fear, déjà vu, olfactory/gustatory hallucinations, experiential phenomena.
  • Ictal: arrest and stare, oroalimentary automatisms (lip-smacking, chewing) and manual automatisms (fumbling), impaired awareness, contralateral dystonic posturing.
  • Postictal: prominent confusion; postictal dysphasia if the dominant (usually left) temporal lobe is involved.

Lateral (neocortical) temporal features

Auditory hallucinations, complex experiential/perceptual phenomena, vertiginous sensations.

Psychiatric & memory implications

TLE has the strongest association with psychiatric comorbidity (depression, anxiety, interictal and postictal psychosis) and with memory impairment (verbal memory with left/dominant temporal involvement; visuospatial memory with right). Neuropsychological assessment is important, especially before epilepsy surgery.

Differential diagnosis

From panic disorder (ictal fear/autonomic aura can mimic panic — but ictal fear is briefer, stereotyped, and may progress to automatisms/impaired awareness); from PNES; from psychotic disorders (for the psychiatric features).

· Frontal lobe epilepsy (FLE)

  • Brief events (often <30 s), frequent, often in clusters and nocturnal.
  • Hypermotor/bizarre movements: thrashing, pedalling/bicycling legs, pelvic thrusting, agitated complex behaviour — can look strange and dramatic.
  • Bilateral tonic posturing (e.g. “fencing” posture), versive head/eye turning, vocalization.
  • Awareness may be preserved even during dramatic motor activity; rapid postictal recovery (little confusion).
A major source of diagnostic confusion. Because FLE events are bizarre, brief, nocturnal, with preserved awareness and normal scalp EEG (deep frontal foci are hard to capture), they are frequently misdiagnosed as PNES, parasomnias, or a movement disorder — and vice versa. Video-EEG and expert semiology review are often needed.

Differential diagnosis

From PNES, NREM parasomnias (sleepwalking/night terrors) and REM sleep behaviour disorder, and paroxysmal movement disorders.

· Occipital, parietal & insular seizures

  • Occipital: elementary visual hallucinations (flashing lights, colours, simple shapes, usually contralateral), ictal blindness/visual field loss, eye deviation, eyelid flutter. Differential: migraine with visual aura (migraine aura is slower-evolving, longer, with characteristic scintillating scotoma).
  • Parietal: contralateral somatosensory phenomena (tingling, numbness, pain), distorted body image, vertigo. Differential: TIA, migraine aura.
  • Insular: laryngeal/throat constriction, chest tightness, dysautonomia, viscerosensory sensations — can mimic temporal or frontal seizures and panic attacks; often under-recognised.

9B. Types of epilepsy — the complete picture (five ways to classify)

Why “temporal lobe epilepsy” was not a top-level heading before — and now is. Modern (ILAE 2017) classification does not use anatomical lobe names as its main axis. Its top level is onset (focal / generalized / combined / unknown), and lobe-based names like “temporal lobe epilepsy” are a descriptive sub-label of focal epilepsy (they were the backbone of the older 1989 scheme). This handbook covered them under §9 (localization) — this section now gathers all the “types of epilepsy”, including the full lobe-by-lobe account (temporal, frontal, parietal, occipital, insular), in one place, and shows how each classification axis relates to the others.

“Type of epilepsy” can mean five different things depending on the axis used. All are valid and used together:

  1. By onset (ILAE Level 2 — the primary modern axis): focal, generalized, combined generalized & focal, unknown.
  2. By anatomy / lobe (localization-related focal epilepsies): temporal, frontal, parietal, occipital, insular.
  3. By etiology: structural, genetic, infectious, metabolic, immune, unknown.
  4. By age of onset: neonatal, infantile, childhood, adolescent/adult.
  5. By syndrome (Level 3): named clusters (e.g. JME, CAE, LGS) — see §10.

Axis 2 — By anatomy / lobe (the focal / localization-related epilepsies, in full detail)

These are all subtypes of focal epilepsy, named by where in the cortex the seizures begin. The clinical picture reflects the function of that region. (Localization from semiology is probabilistic — see §9.)

(a) Temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) — the commonest focal epilepsy in adults

  • Two sub-forms: mesial TLE (from the hippocampus/amygdala/parahippocampus — by far the most common, often with hippocampal/mesial temporal sclerosis and a history of childhood febrile seizures) and lateral (neocortical) TLE (from the lateral temporal neocortex).
  • Aura (mesial): classic epigastric rising sensation, ictal fear, déjà vu / jamais vu, olfactory or gustatory hallucinations, dreamy/experiential states.
  • Aura (lateral): auditory hallucinations (buzzing, voices, music), complex perceptual/experiential phenomena, vertigo.
  • Ictal: arrest and stare; oroalimentary automatisms (lip-smacking, chewing, swallowing) and manual automatisms (fumbling, picking); impaired awareness; contralateral dystonic limb posturing; may evolve to a bilateral convulsion.
  • Postictal: prominent confusion; postictal dysphasia if the dominant (usually left) temporal lobe is involved (a lateralizing sign).
  • Course & treatment: often becomes drug-resistant; mesial TLE with hippocampal sclerosis is the most surgically treatable epilepsy (anterior temporal lobectomy can be curative).
  • Neuropsychiatry: the strongest association with depression, anxiety, and postictal/interictal psychosis, and with memory impairment (verbal memory — left/dominant; visuospatial memory — right).
Differential diagnosis

From panic disorder (ictal fear/autonomic aura mimic panic — but seizures are briefer, stereotyped, and progress to automatisms/impaired awareness); from PNES; from primary psychotic/mood disorders (for the psychiatric features).

(b) Frontal lobe epilepsy (FLE) — the second commonest focal epilepsy

  • Character: brief (often <30 s), very frequent, often in clusters and characteristically nocturnal (sleep-related hypermotor epilepsy).
  • Motor features: hyperkinetic/bizarre movements (thrashing, pedalling/bicycling legs, pelvic thrusting, rocking), bilateral tonic posturing (e.g. the “fencing” / figure-of-four posture), versive head/eye turning, and vocalization.
  • Awareness may be preserved even during dramatic movements; rapid postictal recovery (little or no confusion).
  • Sub-regions: supplementary motor area (asymmetric tonic posturing), dorsolateral (versive/contralateral clonic), orbitofrontal/cingulate (complex behavioural/hypermotor, autonomic), frontopolar.
  • Diagnostic difficulty: because events are bizarre, brief, nocturnal, with preserved awareness and often a normal scalp EEG (deep foci), FLE is frequently mislabelled as PNES, parasomnias, or a movement disorder — video-EEG and expert semiology review often needed.
Differential diagnosis

From PNES, NREM parasomnias (sleepwalking, night terrors), REM sleep behaviour disorder, and paroxysmal movement disorders.

(c) Parietal lobe epilepsy

  • Sensory-dominant: contralateral somatosensory symptoms — tingling, numbness, a crawling or electric sensation, and (uncommonly) pain.
  • Distorted body image (a limb feeling absent, enlarged or moved), vertigo, and disturbances of spatial perception.
  • Often spreads quickly to adjacent lobes, so the first obvious clinical features may be temporal- or frontal-type — making it hard to localize (a “silent” onset zone).
Differential diagnosis

From TIA and migraine aura (both slower-evolving); from functional sensory symptoms.

(d) Occipital lobe epilepsy

  • Elementary visual hallucinations — flashing lights, colours, simple geometric shapes, usually in the contralateral visual field and often brief and coloured/circular (contrast with migraine).
  • Ictal amaurosis (transient blindness), visual field loss, eye/eyelid deviation, forced eyelid flutter, and eye-movement sensations.
  • May include occipital headache and spread to temporal or parietal cortex.
Differential diagnosis

From migraine with visual aura — migraine aura is slower-evolving (minutes), longer-lasting, with a characteristic scintillating (zig-zag) scotoma that marches across the field, whereas occipital-seizure hallucinations are brief, coloured and often circular. Also from occipital stroke/TIA.

(e) Insular lobe epilepsy — the “great mimic”

  • Viscerosensory/autonomic: throat or chest constriction, laryngeal discomfort, dyspnoea, palpitations, an unpleasant rising sensation, dysautonomia; unpleasant somatosensory (electric/warm) sensations over large body areas.
  • Often followed by dysarthria and focal motor/dystonic features as it spreads.
  • The insula sits deep between the frontal, temporal and parietal opercula, so insular seizures mimic temporal, frontal, and even panic attacks, and are under-recognised; scalp EEG frequently misses them (may need depth electrodes).
Differential diagnosis

From temporal and frontal lobe epilepsy, and from panic disorder and cardiac/respiratory causes of the somatic sensations.

Any epilepsy type also has an etiological label that drives treatment: structural (e.g. hippocampal sclerosis, tumour, cortical dysplasia, stroke, trauma), genetic (e.g. the genetic generalized epilepsies, channelopathies), infectious (e.g. neurocysticercosis, encephalitis), metabolic, immune (autoimmune encephalitis), or unknown. Etiologies co-exist (e.g. a genetic focal epilepsy with a structural lesion).

11. Developmental and epileptic encephalopathies (DEE)

  • Epileptic encephalopathy: the epileptic activity itself (frequent seizures and/or abundant interictal discharges) contributes to cognitive and behavioural impairment beyond what the underlying cause alone would produce.
  • Developmental encephalopathy: developmental impairment due to the underlying cause, independent of epileptic activity.
  • Developmental AND epileptic encephalopathy (DEE): both mechanisms operate together (e.g. Lennox-Gastaut, Dravet, IESS).
  • Consequences: intellectual disability, autism-spectrum features, behavioural disturbance, regression or stagnation of development.
  • Distinction from “epilepsy alone”: in ordinary epilepsy, cognition is largely preserved between seizures; in a DEE, ongoing epileptic activity actively degrades development — which is why controlling the epileptic activity can improve the developmental trajectory.

12. The postictal state

The postictal state is the period of transient brain dysfunction after a seizure ends, before the person returns to their baseline. It reflects the brain's recovery from the seizure, not ongoing seizure activity.

Why it happens

  • Neuronal recovery: exhausted, metabolically depleted neurons need time to restore ion gradients and energy.
  • Active cortical inhibition: seizure-terminating inhibitory mechanisms (partly GABA-mediated, with neuromodulators like adenosine) that stop the seizure also transiently suppress cortical function — producing confusion, weakness (Todd's), and slowing.
  • Metabolic recovery: transient changes in local blood flow, glucose use and lactate.
  • Transient neurological dysfunction: the involved network is temporarily “offline”, so its normal function fails (e.g. dominant temporal → aphasia; motor cortex → Todd's paresis).

Postictal aggression is rarely goal-directed. It is typically reactive/resistive, provoked by attempts to restrain or examine a confused person, and non-directed. · Postictal aphasia

  • Dominant-hemisphere involvement: transient language disturbance (word-finding difficulty, impaired comprehension or expression) after a seizure involving the dominant (usually left) temporal/frontal language areas. It is a useful lateralizing sign (points to the dominant hemisphere).
  • Transient: resolves over minutes to hours as the network recovers.
  • Differential from stroke: as with Todd's paresis — new aphasia can signal an acute stroke; a preceding witnessed seizure and gradual resolution favour a postictal cause, but imaging is required if there is doubt.
  • When urgent: first presentation, no clear preceding seizure, persistent or worsening deficit → investigate as possible stroke / structural lesion.

· Postictal confusion

  • Why it happens: transient global cortical suppression after the seizure; commonest and longest after GTCS and focal impaired-awareness (temporal) seizures; essentially absent after absence and myoclonic seizures.
  • Course: gradual clearing over minutes to a few hours; the person is disoriented, slow, may repeat questions, and is amnesic for this period.
  • Factors associated with longer confusion: prolonged/severe seizure, older age, underlying brain lesion, higher medication burden, and ongoing nonconvulsive status epilepticus (a key consideration if confusion is unusually prolonged).
  • Differential diagnosis: nonconvulsive status epilepticus (needs EEG), delirium from another cause, intoxication, hypoglycaemia, and postictal psychosis (if psychotic features emerge, often after a lucid interval).

· Postictal headache

  • Occurrence: very common after GTCS and focal seizures.
  • Mechanisms: transient changes in cerebral blood flow, neuronal/metabolic stress, and muscle strain; overlap with migraine mechanisms (epilepsy and migraine are comorbid).
  • Pattern: often a diffuse or migraine-like throbbing headache lasting hours; usually self-limiting.
  • Red flags / differential: thunderclap or worst-ever headache, headache with fever/neck stiffness, or focal deficit → exclude subarachnoid haemorrhage, meningitis/encephalitis, or a structural lesion. A first seizure with a severe new headache always needs urgent imaging.

14. Postictal psychosis (PIP)

DefinitionA psychotic episode emerging after a seizure (typically a cluster), classically following a lucid interval of relative normality
EpidemiologyAccounts for a substantial share of psychoses in epilepsy; more common in long-standing, drug-resistant focal (esp. temporal-lobe) epilepsy
Time courseOnset usually within hours to a few days (often after a ~1–6 day lucid interval); episode lasts days to weeks, then typically resolves
OutcomeUsually self-limiting, but recurrent; a minority evolve toward a chronic interictal psychosis; carries suicide risk during episodes

The classic clinical pattern

Seizure cluster → postictal recovery / lucid interval (typically 1–6 days of relative normality) → onset of psychosis. The psychosis then persists for days to weeks and resolves. Not every case follows this exact sequence — the lucid interval may be short or absent, and presentations vary.

Psychotic symptoms

  • Delusions (often persecutory or grandiose; sometimes religious/mystical — mood-congruent affective colouring is common).
  • Hallucinations (auditory or visual).
  • Thought disorder and behavioural disturbance; prominent affective features (elation or depression) are characteristic.
  • Relatively preserved insight early in some, fluctuating course, and often a degree of clouding.

Risk factors

Long duration of epilepsy (often >10 years), focal impaired-awareness / temporal-lobe epilepsy, bilateral or diffuse interictal EEG abnormalities, secondarily generalized seizures, seizure clusters, and a family history of psychiatric disorder.

15. Ictal psychiatric phenomena

These are psychiatric symptoms that are themselves the seizure (i.e. produced directly by the ictal discharge), most often arising from limbic/temporal networks.

  • Ictal fear: a sudden, intense, unprovoked fear/terror — brief, stereotyped, often with autonomic features and progressing to automatisms or impaired awareness (amygdala/mesial temporal). Can be mistaken for a panic attack.
  • Ictal anxiety: abrupt anxiety as part of the ictal semiology.
  • Ictal hallucinations: olfactory, gustatory, auditory, visual or experiential, arising during the discharge.
  • Ictal psychosis-like phenomena: occur mainly in focal (nonconvulsive) status epilepticus — the psychotic state is the ongoing seizure and resolves when it is terminated.
  • Ictal behavioural changes / automatisms: semi-purposeful behaviours during impaired awareness.
  • Ictal aggression: genuinely ictal aggression is rare and typically brief, non-directed and stereotyped (unlike planned violence).

How ictal differs from postictal and interictal

Ictal phenomena are brief, stereotyped, and coincide with the EEG discharge (resolve when it stops). Postictal phenomena follow the seizure, often after a lucid interval, and last longer. Interictal phenomena occur between seizures, unrelated to any single seizure's timing.

16. Interictal psychosis

  • Timing: occurs between seizures, independent of the timing of individual seizures — a chronic or recurrent psychosis in a person with epilepsy.
  • Relationship to epilepsy: associated with long-standing temporal-lobe epilepsy; risk rises with duration of epilepsy.
  • Phenomenology: a schizophrenia-like psychosis (delusions, hallucinations) but classically with better-preserved affect and personality/social relatedness and fewer negative symptoms than schizophrenia (this contrast is a classic teaching point, though not absolute).
  • “Forced normalization” / alternative psychosis: a described phenomenon in which psychosis emerges as seizures come under control (and the EEG “normalizes”) — an inverse relationship between seizures and psychiatric symptoms.
  • Differential diagnosis: from schizophrenia (no epilepsy link), postictal psychosis (episodic, seizure-linked, self-limiting), and drug-induced psychosis.
  • Treatment considerations: antipsychotics are used but many lower the seizure threshold (see §40) — choose agents with lower seizure liability and optimise seizure control.

17. Interictal behavioural & psychiatric features of epilepsy

Psychiatric comorbidity is common in epilepsy

  • Mood symptoms: depression is the commonest psychiatric comorbidity in epilepsy and is often under-recognised; an interictal dysphoric pattern is described.
  • Anxiety: common, including fear of seizures and situational avoidance.
  • Psychosis: interictal and postictal (above).
  • Personality/behavioural changes: historically a “Geschwind syndrome” was described in some temporal-lobe epilepsy (hyperreligiosity, hypergraphia, altered sexuality, “viscosity”/circumstantiality) — this is controversial and not universal, and should be stated with caution.
  • Cognitive difficulties: attention, memory (especially with temporal-lobe involvement) and processing speed — contributed to by seizures, the underlying lesion, and medication.
  • Suicidality: suicide risk is increased in epilepsy (multifactorial — depression, stigma, and some antiseizure drugs carry a labelled mood/suicidality caution). Screen actively.
Clinical caution. These are associations; not every person with epilepsy has psychiatric symptoms, and attributing all behaviour to the epilepsy risks diagnostic overshadowing. Assess each symptom on its merits and consider medication effects.

18. Status epilepticus (SE)

Neurological emergency. Status epilepticus carries significant morbidity and mortality; prolonged seizures cause neuronal injury. Recognition and prompt clinician-led treatment are critical. The information here is educational, not a treatment protocol.

  • Definition (operational, ILAE): a condition resulting from failure of the mechanisms that terminate a seizure, leading to abnormally prolonged seizures. Two operational time points are used: t1 = the time beyond which the seizure is likely to be prolonged (for convulsive SE, ~5 minutes) — the point to start treatment; t2 = the time beyond which there is risk of long-term consequences (for convulsive SE, ~30 minutes).
  • Convulsive status epilepticus (CSE): continuous or repeated tonic-clonic seizures without recovery of consciousness — the classic emergency.
  • Nonconvulsive status epilepticus (NCSE): ongoing seizure activity without prominent convulsion — presents as altered awareness/behaviour; needs EEG to diagnose (see §20).
  • Refractory SE: SE that continues despite adequate first- and second-line treatment.
  • Super-refractory SE: SE that continues (or recurs) ≥24 hours after starting anaesthesia (or recurs on weaning it).

Complications

Hypoxia, aspiration, hyperthermia, rhabdomyolysis and renal failure, cardiac arrhythmia, metabolic derangement, cerebral oedema, and long-term neuronal injury / cognitive impairment.

19. Status epilepticus — general management algorithm (educational)

Clinician-led and individualised. The following is a guideline-based educational outline of the general sequence, not prescribing advice. Actual drug/dose decisions are made by the treating team.

  1. Recognition & timing — note seizure start; treat convulsive SE at ~5 minutes.
  2. ABC — airway, breathing, circulation; oxygen; position safely; IV access; monitoring.
  3. Check glucose (and treat hypoglycaemia); send bloods (electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, renal/hepatic, drug levels, toxicology); consider thiamine before glucose in at-risk patients.
  4. Immediate stabilization — correct reversible causes (glucose, sodium, etc.).
  5. First-line: a benzodiazepine (e.g. IV lorazepam, or IM midazolam / rectal or buccal midazolam if no IV access).
  6. Second-line: an IV antiseizure medication (e.g. levetiracetam, valproate, or phenytoin/fosphenytoin) if seizures continue.
  7. Refractory: anaesthetic agents (e.g. midazolam/propofol/thiopental infusion) with intubation and continuous EEG monitoring in ICU.
  8. Super-refractory / ongoing: ICU management, investigate/treat the underlying cause (including autoimmune encephalitis), continuous EEG.

20. Nonconvulsive status epilepticus (NCSE)

  • Presentation: altered consciousness or behaviour without a prominent convulsion — ranging from subtle confusion, a fluctuating “fog”, staring, and mutism to bizarre behaviour, aphasia, or a prolonged “twilight” state; sometimes subtle motor signs (eyelid or facial twitching, automatisms).
  • Why it is easily missed: it can masquerade as a psychiatric disorder (acute confusional/psychotic state, catatonia, dissociation), delirium, or prolonged postictal confusion.
  • EEG is essential: the diagnosis rests on capturing ongoing ictal activity on EEG; clinical improvement with a trial of IV benzodiazepine (with EEG) supports it.
  • Differential from psychiatric disorders: consider NCSE in any unexplained, prolonged or fluctuating altered mental state, especially in someone with epilepsy, the elderly, or the critically ill — get an EEG.

Remember

“Unexplained prolonged confusion / behaviour change → think NCSE → get an EEG.” It can mimic psychiatric illness and is treatable.

21. Psychogenic nonepileptic seizures (PNES) / dissociative seizures

What they are

PNES / dissociative seizures are paroxysmal episodes that resemble epileptic seizures — altered movement, sensation, awareness or responsiveness — but are not caused by abnormal epileptic neuronal discharge. They arise from functional / dissociative brain processes. They are a common reason for referral to epilepsy services and a leading cause of “drug-resistant seizures” that are actually not epileptic.

The person is NOT faking. PNES / dissociative seizures are real, involuntary events causing genuine distress and disability. They are not deliberate simulation. Explaining this compassionately (and avoiding the word “pseudoseizure”) is itself part of treatment — a stigmatising or dismissive explanation worsens outcomes.

How they are framed across systems

  • Neurology/neuropsychiatry: psychogenic nonepileptic seizure (PNES).
  • ICD-11 / functional-neurology: dissociative neurological symptom disorder, with (non-epileptic) seizures — a dissociative seizure.
  • DSM-5-TR: Functional neurological symptom disorder (conversion disorder), with attacks or seizures.
Not factitious disorder or malingering. In factitious disorder and malingering the symptoms are intentionally produced (for the sick role, or for external gain respectively). In PNES / dissociative seizures the events are not under voluntary control. These are fundamentally different and must not be conflated.

22. Mechanism of PNES / dissociative seizures

Current (integrative) models

PNES is best understood through a biopsychosocial, functional-neurological model rather than a single cause. Contributing processes include:

  • Dissociation — a disruption of the normal integration of awareness, memory, emotion and movement.
  • Altered attention and self-monitoring, and abnormal top-down cognitive control over movement/awareness.
  • Emotion-regulation difficulties and heightened autonomic arousal; the event may function as an involuntary “shutdown” in response to overwhelming affect or perceived threat.
  • Threat processing and learned/automatic behavioural responses that become triggered involuntarily.
  • Predisposing, precipitating and perpetuating factors (including, in some, adverse experiences or trauma).
Trauma is NOT necessary or universal. Although trauma and adverse life experiences are more common in PNES populations, PNES does not always result from trauma, and a trauma history is neither required for the diagnosis nor present in every case. Avoid assuming or implying a trauma cause in an individual.

23. Psychiatric associations of PNES

Commonly associated (association, not proof of cause)

  • Anxiety disorders (including panic), depressive disorders.
  • Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) and other trauma-related presentations.
  • Dissociative disorders.
  • Personality difficulties/disorders in a subgroup.
  • Trauma exposure and chronic stress in a proportion of patients.
  • High rates of other functional/somatic symptoms.
Association ≠ causation. These conditions are more frequent in people with PNES than in the general population, but their presence does not prove they caused the PNES, and their absence does not exclude PNES. Each is assessed and treated on its own merits.

Coexistence with epilepsy. A minority of people have both epilepsy and PNES — this makes diagnosis harder and is a key reason to characterise each event type (ideally with video-EEG) rather than assume all events share one mechanism.

25. Individual differentiating signs — value and limitations

For each sign: what it suggests → why → its limitation. None is an absolute rule.

  • Eyes closed vs open: closed/forced-shut eyes suggest PNES (epileptic convulsions usually have eyes open) → because active eye closure implies retained motor control → limitation: not universal; some epileptic seizures have closed eyes.
  • Resistance to eye opening: suggests PNES → active resistance implies preserved volitional tone → limitation: can be inconsistent.
  • Asynchronous / out-of-phase limb movements: suggest PNES → epileptic clonus is rhythmic and synchronous → limitation: frontal seizures can be asynchronous/bizarre.
  • Side-to-side head movement & pelvic thrusting: more common in PNES → not typical of the evolving tonic-clonic pattern → limitation: both occur in frontal-lobe epilepsy.
  • Prolonged, fluctuating, waxing-and-waning course: suggests PNES → true GTCS is brief and self-terminating → limitation: status epilepticus is also prolonged (but looks and behaves differently).
  • Retained awareness/recall during a bilateral “convulsion”: suggests PNES → bilateral motor activity in epilepsy usually impairs awareness → limitation: focal motor seizures can spare awareness.
  • Emotional expression / ictal crying / speaking mid-event: suggests PNES → implies retained higher function → limitation: not universal.
  • Sudden onset/offset: abrupt on/off favours epilepsy; gradual build-up/wind-down favours PNES → limitation: overlaps.
  • Duration: >5–10 min of continuous “convulsion” without evolving favours PNES → true convulsive SE evolves and is a medical emergency → limitation: must not miss true status.
  • Postictal confusion: marked, prolonged confusion favours epilepsy; rapid orientation favours PNES → limitation: unreliable alone.
  • Tongue biting: lateral biting favours epilepsy; tip/lip biting favours PNES → jaw clenching pattern differs → limitation: absence of biting proves nothing.
  • Urinary incontinence & injury: can occur in both → limitation: not discriminating.
  • Vocalization: ictal cry at onset favours epilepsy; emotional words mid-event favour PNES → timing/content differ → limitation: overlaps.
  • Responsiveness / avoidance: purposeful avoidance of a noxious stimulus or a hand-drop-avoiding-face test favours PNES → implies preserved processing → limitation: soft sign, not definitive.
State it explicitly: “No single bedside sign reliably establishes PNES versus epilepsy in every patient.” Integrate multiple features with a detailed history, witness account/home video, and — where available — video-EEG.

26. Video-EEG

  • Gold standard (in appropriate settings): simultaneous video + EEG that captures a typical event allows correlation of the clinical semiology with the electrical activity — the most reliable way to separate epileptic seizures from PNES.
  • Ictal EEG: shows the discharge during an epileptic seizure; its absence during a captured typical bilateral convulsion strongly supports PNES.
  • Interictal EEG: between seizures — may show epileptiform discharges (supportive of epilepsy) but is often normal.
  • Limitations: requires capturing a habitual event; deep (e.g. frontal, mesial) foci may not show on scalp EEG; movement artefact can obscure the record; a single normal study does not exclude epilepsy.

Two rules to memorise

“Normal EEG ≠ no epilepsy” — a normal routine (interictal) EEG does not exclude epilepsy (routine EEG has limited sensitivity; repeat/sleep-deprived/prolonged studies increase yield).
“Abnormal interictal EEG ≠ every event is epileptic” — interictal epileptiform abnormalities do not prove that a particular observed event was an epileptic seizure; the event must be characterised on its own merits (ideally captured).

27. Provocation / induction of events (diagnostic)

  • In specialist settings, event capture during video-EEG may be aided by standard activation methods (e.g. hyperventilation, photic stimulation) or, in some centres, by suggestion-based induction to record a habitual event for diagnosis.
  • Ethical & clinical issues: induction/suggestion protocols raise concerns about consent and deception; practice varies, and they must be used only under specialist supervision with appropriate consent and safeguards.
  • Limitations: a provoked event may not be representative; a negative test does not exclude epilepsy or PNES.
No do-it-yourself induction. Seizure/event induction is a specialist, supervised diagnostic procedure. This handbook gives no instructions for inducing seizures outside a controlled clinical setting.

28. Syncope vs epileptic seizure

Syncope is transient loss of consciousness from global cerebral hypoperfusion (cardiovascular/reflex), not abnormal neuronal discharge. It is a very common seizure mimic.

30. Dissociative episode vs epileptic seizure

  • Altered awareness: in a dissociative episode the person may seem “absent”, detached or unresponsive, with depersonalization (feeling detached from oneself) and/or derealization (surroundings feel unreal).
  • Amnesia: patchy or complete amnesia for the episode can occur (dissociative amnesia), overlapping with the amnesia of impaired-awareness seizures.
  • Emotional triggers: dissociative episodes are frequently precipitated by stress, trauma reminders, or strong affect.
  • Behavioural manifestations: staring, unresponsiveness, or complex behaviour (dissociative fugue) — but longer, more variable, and context-linked than a stereotyped focal seizure, with no ictal EEG discharge.
  • Overlap with PNES: dissociative seizures sit within this spectrum; the differentiation from epilepsy uses the same principles as §24–26.

32. Movement disorders vs seizures

  • Tics: brief, stereotyped, suppressible, preceded by an urge and followed by relief; awareness retained; wax and wane — unlike seizures.
  • Dystonia: sustained twisting postures; can be paroxysmal; no impaired awareness; consider paroxysmal dyskinesias (kinesigenic/non-kinesigenic).
  • Dyskinesia / chorea: continuous flowing involuntary movements (e.g. Huntington's, drug-induced); not paroxysmal “attacks”.
  • Tremor: rhythmic oscillation; postural/action or resting; no LOC.
  • Stereotypies: rhythmic, repetitive, often soothing; distinguishable from focal motor seizures.
  • Functional (psychogenic) movement disorders: variable, distractible, entrainable, incongruent with recognised patterns — the movement-disorder analogue of PNES.

33. Metabolic and toxic mimics (and true provoked seizures)

These can produce seizure-like events (altered consciousness, collapse, jerking) and/or genuine acute symptomatic seizures — the distinction matters because the treatment is to correct the derangement, not to start long-term antiseizure drugs.

  • Hypoglycaemia: confusion, sweating, tremor, focal signs or seizures — always check glucose in any collapse/confusion.
  • Electrolyte disturbance: hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, hyperglycaemia — can cause acute symptomatic seizures.
  • Syncope (see §28) and hypoxia — global hypoperfusion/oxygen lack → collapse ± convulsive movements.
  • Intoxication: stimulants (cocaine, amphetamines), some drugs in overdose.
  • Withdrawal: alcohol (classic withdrawal seizures 6–48 h after stopping; risk of delirium tremens) and benzodiazepine/barbiturate withdrawal.
  • Organ failure: uraemia (renal), hepatic encephalopathy.

Remember

“Check glucose, sodium, calcium, magnesium; ask about alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal and drugs” in any first seizure — reversible causes first.

34. Raised intracranial pressure (ICP) & structural causes

Structural lesions both cause seizures and raise intracranial pressure — and raised ICP is itself a medical emergency. The skull is a fixed box containing brain, blood and cerebrospinal fluid (the Monro-Kellie doctrine): if one component expands (a mass, swelling, or excess CSF), pressure rises once compensation is exhausted.

Features of raised ICP

  • Headache — classically worse in the morning, on lying down, and with coughing/straining/bending.
  • Nausea and vomiting (may be projectile).
  • Papilloedema (swollen optic discs on fundoscopy).
  • Visual disturbance, diplopia (a false-localizing sixth-nerve palsy).
  • Reduced consciousness, drowsiness.
  • Cushing's triad (late, ominous): hypertension + bradycardia + irregular breathing — signals impending herniation.
  • Seizures and focal neurological deficits from the underlying lesion.

A first adult-onset focal seizure, or a seizure with focal deficit / raised-ICP features, requires urgent neuroimaging to exclude a structural cause. In suspected raised ICP with a mass lesion, a lumbar puncture can precipitate fatal herniation (“coning”) and is contraindicated until imaging excludes a mass. Autoimmune encephalitis is an important treatable cause of new seizures + psychiatric symptoms — consider it, especially with subacute onset, movement disorder and cognitive change.

35. Diagnostic work-up of a first seizure

The diagnosis of a seizure/epilepsy is primarily clinical — built on a meticulous history — supported by investigations. The core question after a first event: was it an epileptic seizure, and if so why, and what is the recurrence risk?

History (the most important step)

  • Eyewitness account / home video — the single most valuable data source (onset, movements, colour, duration, recovery).
  • Detailed semiology (the 12-point framework, §8): aura, awareness, motor/sensory/autonomic/cognitive/emotional features, duration, postictal state.
  • Precipitants: sleep deprivation, alcohol, flashing lights, missed medication, acute illness.
  • Medication history and substance use (intoxication/withdrawal; drugs lowering seizure threshold).
  • Past neurological disease, head trauma, CNS infection, febrile seizures, birth/developmental history.
  • Metabolic symptoms; family history of epilepsy/sudden death/psychiatric illness.

Examination & investigations

  • General + neurological examination; cardiovascular exam (for syncope); fundoscopy (papilloedema).
  • Bloods: glucose, sodium, calcium, magnesium, renal/hepatic function, full blood count; toxicology; (prolactin/lactate/CK are only supportive and time-limited).
  • ECG — essential to exclude a cardiac (arrhythmic) cause of collapse; consider prolonged QT.
  • EEG — supports classification and recurrence-risk estimate (see §36); a normal EEG does not exclude epilepsy.
  • Neuroimaging: MRI is preferred (detects hippocampal sclerosis, cortical dysplasia, tumour); CT is used acutely/urgently (trauma, suspected bleed/mass, in the emergency setting).
  • Lumbar puncture only if CNS infection/subarachnoid haemorrhage suspected — after imaging if raised ICP possible.
  • Consider autoimmune/paraneoplastic antibodies if subacute onset with psychiatric/cognitive/movement features.
When each is most useful. ECG for every transient LOC (rule out cardiac). MRI for focal-onset seizures, focal signs, or refractory epilepsy. CT for the acute/emergency picture. EEG to support (not to prove or disprove) the diagnosis and to classify. Video-EEG when the diagnosis is uncertain or PNES is suspected.

  • Interictal epileptiform discharges (spikes, sharp waves, spike-wave) support a diagnosis of epilepsy and help classify (focal vs generalized).
  • Ictal patterns: the electrographic seizure itself; the definitive evidence when captured.
  • Focal abnormalities (e.g. temporal spikes) suggest focal epilepsy; generalized spike-wave suggests genetic generalized epilepsy (e.g. 3-Hz in absence).

37. Neuropsychological assessment in epilepsy

  • Purpose: to characterise cognitive strengths/weaknesses, monitor change, contribute to localization/lateralization, support pre-surgical evaluation (predicting post-operative memory risk), and inform rehabilitation and psychological support.
  • Domains assessed: intelligence, memory (verbal vs visuospatial — sensitive to temporal-lobe function/laterality), attention/processing speed, executive function, language, and mood/quality of life.
  • Localization value: verbal memory deficits point to dominant (usually left) temporal dysfunction; visuospatial memory deficits to the non-dominant (right) temporal lobe.
  • Contributors to cognitive difficulty: the underlying lesion, seizure frequency/type, ongoing epileptic activity (esp. in DEE), antiseizure-medication effects, mood, and psychosocial factors — disentangling these is a core role of the assessment.
  • Role in PNES: neuropsychological and psychological assessment helps understand the person's difficulties and guides therapy; performance-validity considerations apply, interpreted carefully and non-judgementally.

38. Antiseizure medications (ASMs) — overview

Educational overview only — not prescribing advice. Drug choice depends on seizure/epilepsy type, age, sex, comorbidity, interactions and pregnancy plans, and is individualised by the prescriber.

  • Broad mechanisms: sodium-channel blockade (carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, oxcarbazepine, lacosamide); calcium-channel effects (ethosuximide for absence; gabapentin/pregabalin); GABA enhancement (benzodiazepines, phenobarbital, tiagabine, vigabatrin); SV2A binding (levetiracetam, brivaracetam); multiple mechanisms (valproate, topiramate, zonisamide).
  • Selection principles: match the drug to the seizure/epilepsy type. Sodium-channel blockers can worsen some genetic generalized epilepsies (myoclonic/absence) and Dravet syndrome; broad-spectrum agents (valproate, lamotrigine, levetiracetam, topiramate) suit generalized epilepsies. Ethosuximide is specific for absence.

Adverse effects, toxicity, withdrawal, interactions, adherence, pregnancy

  • Dose-related toxicity: sedation, ataxia, diplopia, nystagmus, tremor, cognitive slowing.
  • Idiosyncratic: rash (including serious Stevens-Johnson syndrome / DRESS, notably with lamotrigine if titrated too fast, and with carbamazepine), hepatotoxicity, blood dyscrasias, hyponatraemia (carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine).
  • Withdrawal: abrupt discontinuation can precipitate seizures or status epilepticus — ASMs are tapered, not stopped suddenly; non-adherence is a leading cause of breakthrough seizures.
  • Drug interactions: enzyme inducers (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital) reduce levels of many drugs including oral contraceptives and some psychotropics; valproate inhibits metabolism and raises lamotrigine levels (rash risk).
  • Adherence: central to control; missed doses are a common seizure trigger; simplify regimens and address side effects and stigma.
  • Pregnancy: valproate is highly teratogenic (neural-tube defects and neurodevelopmental harm) and is avoided in people who can become pregnant wherever possible; folic acid, pre-conception counselling and specialist planning are essential; lamotrigine/levetiracetam are generally considered lower-risk (specialist decision).

39. Psychiatric & cognitive effects of antiseizure medications

Psychiatric adverse effects

  • Levetiracetam: irritability, agitation, anxiety, depression, aggression (“levetiracetam rage”) — common and important.
  • Topiramate, zonisamide: depression, and cognitive slowing / word-finding difficulty (topiramate — “dull-iramate”), rarely psychosis.
  • Phenobarbital: sedation, depression, hyperactivity in children.
  • Vigabatrin: depression/psychosis (and irreversible visual-field loss).
  • Class caution: ASMs carry a labelled warning of a small increased risk of suicidal ideation/behaviour — screen for mood and suicidality.

Mood-stabilising / beneficial effects

  • Valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine have mood-stabilising properties (used in bipolar disorder); lamotrigine is antidepressant-leaning.
  • Good seizure control often improves mood and cognition.

Cognitive effects

Most ASMs can cause some sedation, slowed processing, attention and memory difficulty, dose-dependently; topiramate and phenobarbital are among the more cognitively burdensome, whereas lamotrigine and levetiracetam tend to be more cognitively benign. Polytherapy and high doses worsen cognitive load. Neuropsychological assessment helps separate medication effects from disease effects.

Forced normalization revisited. Occasionally, as seizures come under tight control on an ASM, a person may develop psychiatric symptoms (psychosis/dysphoria) — the “alternative psychosis” / forced normalization phenomenon (§16). Recognise it rather than assuming a primary psychiatric relapse.

Remember — highest-risk psychotropics

“Bupropion, clomipramine/TCAs, clozapine, tramadol” are the classic seizure-threshold-lowering psychotropics to be cautious with in epilepsy.
