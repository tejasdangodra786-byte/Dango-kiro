Part 1 — What is Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention?

4. Definition of MBRP

4.1 Formal definition (source-based)

ESTABLISHED Chawla et al. (2010, p. 388) describe MBRP as "a recently developed after-care treatment for adults with substance use disorders" that "integrates mindfulness practice and aspects of relapse prevention" (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985; Daley & Marlatt, 2006), a cognitive-behavioural treatment aiming to prevent relapse. Witkiewitz, Marlatt & Walker (2005) first proposed it as a "new" cognitive-behavioural intervention whose goal is to develop awareness and acceptance of thoughts, feelings and sensations through mindfulness practice and to use these skills as a coping strategy in high-risk situations. The clinician's guide is Bowen, Chawla & Marlatt (2011); 2nd edition Bowen, Chawla, Grow & Marlatt (2021).

Source, author, year: Witkiewitz, Marlatt & Walker, 2005 (conceptual paper); Bowen, Chawla & Marlatt, 2011 (manual); Bowen et al., 2009 (first pilot RCT); Bowen et al., 2021 (2nd ed.).

4.2 Simplified definition

MBRP teaches people in recovery to notice what is happening inside them (sensations, thoughts, feelings, urges) before they act, so that they can pause and choose a response that serves recovery instead of reacting on "automatic pilot".

4.3 Explaining the definition

  • Aftercare treatment: originally delivered after inpatient or intensive outpatient stabilisation (Bowen et al., 2009). It assumes some stability is already in place.
  • Integrates mindfulness and relapse prevention: the cognitive-behavioural half (identify triggers/high-risk situations, coping, lapse management) and the contemplative half (formal meditation, inquiry, informal practice) are woven into every session.
  • Interrupting habitual reactions: the central aim is to "interrupt the habitual reactive behaviors associated with high-risk situations, triggers, and cravings" (Chawla et al., 2010).
  • Format (research protocol): eight weekly 2-hour group sessions, two facilitators, 6–10 participants, closed cohort, daily home practice with audio recordings (Chawla et al., 2010; Witkiewitz & Bowen, 2010).

4.4 Central therapeutic purpose; population; problems addressed

QuestionAnswer
PurposeIncrease awareness of triggers and reactions; change the relationship to discomfort and craving; create a pause between trigger and action; support a sustainable recovery lifestyle.

5. What exactly does MBRP do?

MBRP aims to help a person to:

  1. Recognise internal and external triggers (people, places, moods, body states) earlier.
  2. Become aware of automatic reactions — the chain trigger → sensation/thought/emotion → urge → action.
  3. See craving as a changing experience that rises, peaks and falls (urge surfing).
  4. Notice thoughts without automatically believing or acting ("thoughts as thoughts").
  5. Observe emotions and body sensations with curiosity.
  6. Tolerate discomfort and reduce experiential avoidance.
  7. Develop alternative responses (SOBER breathing space, calling support, leaving the situation).
  8. Make more deliberate decisions in high-risk situations.
  9. Build a sustainable recovery lifestyle (balance, self-compassion, social support, continuing practice).
Eliminating craving vs. responding differently. The programme does not promise craving will disappear. The target is the response to craving. In the Session 2 inquiry (Bowen et al., 2021 excerpt) facilitators emphasise that mindfulness "is not necessarily about changing one's experience, but about creating a different relationship to it". Suppression is discouraged because trying not to think about something tends to make the thought harder to unhook (Chen, Jindani & Turner; Witkiewitz et al., 2005 on suppression measures).
Fictional example — alcohol. Rahul (38) finishes work after an argument with his manager. Previously: argument → tight chest, "I deserve a drink" → walks to the bar without noticing. With MBRP skills: he notices tight chest and the thought, names it ("anger, urge to drink, 6/10"), takes a SOBER breathing space on the pavement, and phones his sponsor while walking home by a different road. Craving did not vanish; the sequence changed.
Fictional example — opioid (smack). Imran (29), on opioid agonist treatment, passes the lane where he used to buy. Heart races, tingling, thought: "Just to feel normal." He pauses, notices the surge peaking around 2 minutes, walks to a tea stall, texts his counsellor, and continues his medication plan. Mindfulness is used alongside his prescribed treatment.

Part 2 — History and Development of MBRP

6. Historical background

6.A Roots of mindfulness

Contemporary mindfulness derives from Buddhist contemplative traditions dating back some 2,500 years (Chen et al., Appendix 2). The Pali word sati is commonly rendered "mindfulness" but also carries senses of remembering, bearing in mind and sustained presence; no single English word captures it, and translations differ across schools. Traditional practice sat within an ethical and soteriological path (enlightenment) whereas clinical programmes are secular, time-limited, symptom- and function-oriented, and delivered by health professionals (Kabat-Zinn, 2003, in Chawla et al., 2010). The shared core is attention directed on purpose with an attitude of openness and non-judgement (Bishop et al., 2004).

Clinical caution: some clients worry mindfulness is religious. Chen et al. recommend psychoeducation that meditation appears across traditions and that clinical mindfulness teaches technique, not belief; invite clients to integrate practice with their own faith if they wish.

6.B Modern clinical mindfulness

  • Jon Kabat-Zinn developed Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center (first paper 1982; Full Catastrophe Living, 1990): eight weekly group sessions (2–2.5 h), body scan, sitting meditation, hatha yoga, plus a retreat day.
  • Segal, Williams & Teasdale built Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT; 2002, 2013) to prevent depressive relapse, drawing on Teasdale's work on decentring and "attentional control" (Teasdale, Segal & Williams, 1995). In a trial, patients with three or more prior episodes roughly halved relapse (Teasdale et al., 2000).
  • Research themes: attention regulation, awareness of mental events, reduced rumination, emotion regulation (reviews: Baer, 2003; Goyal et al., 2014 — moderate evidence for anxiety/depression/pain, with caveats about study quality).

6.C Relapse prevention (Marlatt)

Marlatt & Gordon (1985) proposed a cognitive-behavioural model: high-risk situations, coping responses, self-efficacy, outcome expectancies, lapse, abstinence violation effect (AVE), and lifestyle balance (e.g., "positive addictions" such as exercise or meditation; Glasser, 1976). Meta-analytic support: Irvin et al. (1999) — 26 studies, N=9,504, overall r=.14 with variation by substance and caution that mechanisms were poorly understood (summarised in Witkiewitz et al., 2005).

6.D Development of MBRP

Marlatt had studied meditation with drinkers from the 1970s (Marlatt & Marques, 1977), run randomised comparisons of transcendental meditation, relaxation and reading in heavy-drinking students (Marlatt et al., 1984) and of meditation/exercise (Murphy, Pagano & Marlatt, 1986), and in 1980s–90s argued for Buddhist "skillful means" in addiction treatment (Marlatt, 2002). The University of Washington Addictive Behaviors Research Center also studied Vipassana in an incarcerated sample (Bowen et al., 2006). Witkiewitz, Marlatt & Walker (2005) then integrated these strands with MBSR/MBCT structure. Sarah Bowen, Neharika (Neha) Chawla and Marlatt developed and tested the eight-session manual; Katie Witkiewitz led mechanism work; Joel Grow joined the 2nd-edition team; Marlatt (d. 2011) was principal investigator of the NIDA-funded pilot (R21 DA010562).

Timeline

Gap addressed: relapse prevention was effective but the field lacked a way to change people's reaction to craving/negative affect (Witkiewitz et al., 2005); mutual-help and CBT coping-skills alone showed unclear mechanisms (Morgenstern & Longabaugh, 2000).

Part 3 — Theoretical Foundations

7. Relapse prevention theory (Marlatt)

The cognitive-behavioural relapse model (Figure 1 in Witkiewitz et al., 2005, redrawn below in original form) is a branching sequence that begins at a high-risk situation.

Component-by-component

ComponentExplanation
High-risk situationAny internal/external situation that threatens perceived control: negative emotion, interpersonal conflict, social pressure, cues, availability, stress (Larimer et al., 1999).
Coping responseCognitive or behavioural strategy (Shiffman, 1984: stress vs temptation coping; Moos, 1993: approach vs avoidance). Effective coping → arrow up; ineffective → arrow down.
Self-efficacySituation-specific confidence in resisting; the "Achilles' heel" idea — outcome depends on the lowest context-specific rating (Hagga, 1990).
Outcome expectanciesBeliefs about substance effects (positive reinforcement: euphoria; negative reinforcement: relief from distress). Strengthened after poor coping.
LapseThe first use after commitment.
Abstinence violation effectAttributing the lapse to internal, uncontrollable failure → guilt/shame → "I've already blown it" → continued use. A lapse seen as a mistake/learning opportunity leads to a "prolapse" (return to goal).
RelapseReturn to a pattern of use.
Stress, social pressure, cues, negative affectFrequent high-risk triggers; negative affect strongly cues craving in lab and clinical studies (Witkiewitz & Bowen, 2010).
Craving/impulsivityCraving predicts reinstatement for major drugs (Witkiewitz & Bowen, 2010); MBRP targets the reactive link rather than craving alone.
Fictional case — Sunita (stimulant). Friend's text: "party tonight, plenty". Ineffective coping (goes "just to watch") → confidence drops, "I can handle one line" → lapse → "I've ruined 40 days" (AVE) → binge. Alternative: SOBER pause, decline, tell counsellor, plan evening; lapse (if it occurs) treated as information: what happened, what next?

Part 5 — Understanding Addiction through MBRP

11. Substance-use behaviour and automaticity

  • Habit formation / cue-reactivity: repeated pairing of cues (places, people, moods, time of day) with substance effects makes cues trigger craving and action sequences, sometimes outside awareness (Ludwig & Wikler, 1974; Tiffany, 1990 — automatic vs non-automatic processes).
  • Reinforcement: positive (euphoria, social enhancement) and negative (relief of distress/withdrawal) — Baker et al. (2004) affective-processing model; operant analysis in Witkiewitz et al. (2005).
  • Emotional and stress-related use: negative affect is a prominent craving cue (Witkiewitz & Bowen, 2010).
  • Impulsivity, attentional bias, automatic sequences: plausible contributors; evidence is substance- and person-specific.
  • Experiential avoidance: acting to escape internal states; central target of MBRP (Chen et al.: "the avoiding just doesn't work… it works for a brief period" — participant quote in Bowen et al., 2021 excerpt).
SubstanceTypical processes (not universal)
Opioids / smackWithdrawal-related discomfort and negative reinforcement; strong environmental/social cues (places, peers, paraphernalia); pain (physical/emotional) as trigger; medically supervised treatment context essential.
StimulantsCue-induced craving; "party/available" contexts; impulsivity and affect swings; sleep disruption; craving predicts use (Hartz et al., 2001, for methamphetamine, via Witkiewitz & Bowen).
AlcoholSocial and habitual contexts (time/place), negative mood, "just one" expectancies.
TobaccoHighly habitual cues (after tea/meals, breaks), stress; frequent short craving episodes suit brief practices.
Caution: do not assume identical triggers across substances; build an individual functional analysis (trigger → thought/sensation/emotion → urge → behaviour → consequence).

DimensionDescription

Craving as a cognitive response with stimulus properties (Tiffany; Witkiewitz et al., 2005) means it is "amenable to treatment intervention"; MBRP accepts rather than restructures craving experience. Craving self-report has limits (Drummond et al., 2000).

Clinical assessment of craving

  • Daily 0–10 rating (frequency, peak, duration) for the past week — compare the Penn Alcohol Craving Scale (PACS; Flannery et al., 1999) used with alcohol and adapted for other drugs in Bowen et al. (2009); α=.87 in that sample.
  • Trigger-specific mapping: where, when, with whom, mood, sensations, thoughts, action, outcome.
  • For opioids/tobacco use substance-appropriate validated scales after verifying language versions and norms in India.
Fictional case — opioid craving (Imran, 29). Evening, rooftop, text from old friend. Craving 7/10: chest tight, mouth dry, shoulders tense. Thought: "One small dose won't hurt." Emotion: loneliness. Urge: leave the house. Using urge surfing at "4/10 practice level" in session earlier, he rehearsed naming: "tight chest — thought — urge to call him". At home he did a 3-minute SOBER, called brother. Craving peaked ~15 minutes, then eased; he recorded it on the craving wave sheet. His clinic dose was unchanged; the counsellor updated the plan.

13. The lapse and relapse process

  • Lapse — single episode after a commitment; relapse — return to prior pattern. Definitions vary widely in trials (e.g., any use vs heavy drinking days vs days of use); always specify.
  • AVE: internal, stable, uncontrollable attribution of a lapse → shame → loss of control (Marlatt, 1985, via Witkiewitz et al., 2005).
  • All-or-nothing thinking ("I've failed") raises relapse probability.
  • Self-efficacy falls after poor coping; early help-seeking halts escalation.
A lapse is not proof that treatment failed. Treat it as data: What was the trigger? What did I notice? What would I try next time? Who will I tell today?

Part 7 — Standard Eight-Session MBRP Programme

Format of the research protocol (Chawla et al., 2010; Witkiewitz & Bowen, 2010): eight weekly 2-hour group sessions; two co-facilitators; 6–10 participants; closed cohort; each session typically opens with a 20–30 (up to 30–45) minute guided meditation followed by inquiry, experiential exercises and relapse-prevention discussion; daily home practice with audio recordings (about 45 minutes/day was assigned in the trial). Fidelity measured with the MBRP-AC (Chawla et al., 2010; adherence to ~90% of components in the trial). Timetables below are illustrative unless stated.

Part 9 — MBRP for Different Substances

Mindfulness is an adjunct. It does not replace medically indicated withdrawal management, opioid agonist treatment, psychiatric care or other evidence-based services. Evidence for MBRP derives mostly from mixed alcohol/cocaine/methamphetamine samples (Bowen et al., 2009); evidence for specific substances such as opioids or tobacco is more limited and should be stated as such. PRELIMINARY

9.1 Opioids and heroin/smack

  • Formulation example: Imran (29), smack for 6 years, now on opioid agonist treatment. Triggers: lane near old den, loneliness at night, body aches, friend's call. Craving 6–8/10 with chest tightness, restlessness; thought "just to feel normal". Previous lapses followed shame and isolation.
  • Techniques: SOBER with a phone-a-supporter step; urge surfing at 3–4/10 first; body scan modified for pain (eyes open); kindness practice for shame; mountain for grounding; trigger-route planning.
  • Adaptations: shorter, structured practices; align sessions with medication timing; reinforce adherence to prescribed treatment; avoid practices that increase withdrawal distress when unmedicated.
  • Safety: overdose risk after abstinence (reduced tolerance) — provide naloxone information via the treating team; withdrawal needs medical management; screen for pain disorders, trauma, suicidality.
  • Limitations: limited trials with opioid-dependent samples; do not extrapolate from alcohol/stimulant data.

9.2 Stimulants

  • Formulation: Sunita (26), methamphetamine; cue-driven craving at parties and after conflict; impulsivity; poor sleep.
  • Techniques: SOBER, urge surfing, mindful walking, thoughts-as-thoughts for "just once" thoughts, sleep and routine planning.
  • Adaptations: short high-energy practices, movement; avoid prolonged lying body scan if agitated.
  • Safety: psychosis risk, agitation, suicidality in crash. Limitation: trial samples included cocaine/methamphetamine but findings are mixed and preliminary.

9.3 Alcohol

  • Formulation: Rahul (38), social and stress drinking; triggers: work conflict, evening routine, bar route.
  • Techniques: full programme; HALT; lapse and AVE education; social support planning.
  • Safety: do not abruptly stop heavy dependence without medical advice (withdrawal seizures).

9.4 Tobacco / nicotine

  • Formulation: Meera (42), automatic smoking after tea and at stress; many daily cues.
  • Techniques: brief 3-minute SOBER, urge surfing (3 min), informal mindful routines, replacing cue sequences. Acceptance-based smoking cessation has some supportive evidence (Gifford et al., 2004, cited in Witkiewitz & Bowen) but is not MBRP-specific.
  • Limitation: nicotine pharmacotherapy remains first-line adjunct.

Part 12 — Facilitator Skills and Therapist Training

20. Role of the MBRP facilitator

  • Stance: embody curiosity, presence and non-judgement; ideally own regular practice (Chen et al.; Chawla et al., 2010 notes therapists had CBT background, some with years of personal practice, a 2-day workshop, weeks of training and weekly supervision).
  • Group management: keep the session focused, work as a team (MBRP-AC rating), manage dominant speakers (thank, redirect to direct experience), support quiet members (invite, never force), handle silence (tolerate it), do not moralise about substance use, respond to shame warmly, encourage home practice without blame.
  • Avoid excessive interpretation, advice-giving and psychotherapy-style analysis during inquiry.

21. Inquiry after practice

Inquiry explores direct experience of a practice, not analysis. MBRP-AC "key questions" (Chawla et al., 2010): (1) highlight raw experience ("What did you experience? What body sensations?") and distinguish thoughts, feelings and sensations; (2) distinguish from the usual way of experiencing; (3) relate to relapse ("How does it relate to relapse?").

24. Individual MBRP delivery

  • Advantages: personalisation, flexible pacing, trauma-sensitive adaptation, easier safety monitoring.
  • Limitations: loss of group learning, normalisation and support; no evidence of equivalence with the group protocol.
  • Modifications: shorter practices, more explicit inquiry/psychoeducation, direct customised relapse plan, session recordings for practice.

29. Evidence for Brief MBRP

The repository's B-MBRP (6 individual inpatient sessions, Hindi) has no outcome data yet. It is the intervention of a planned M.Phil pilot (quasi-experimental, waitlist control by admission cohort, planned N=40, T1/T2/T3; hypotheses H1–H6). Therefore:
  • Direct evidence: none. Do not claim it works or that it is equivalent to the 8-session group programme.
  • Indirect evidence: (1) eight-session MBRP RCTs (Bowen et al., 2009; Bowen et al., 2014 ⚠); (2) general findings that brief mindfulness practice may change attention/cognition (Zeidan et al., 2010; Moore et al., 2012 — both cited in Chen et al.) but not always (Leverty, 2012); (3) dose–response (Carmody & Baer, 2008); (4) attenuation of MBRP effects without ongoing support (Witkiewitz & Bowen, 2010).
  • Claims in the proposal that need checking before you cite them: (a) the proposal lists "Riya, Kumar & Chandel (2025), Indian J Psychiatry" as an MBRP + usual care study, while its Research Gap section says the two Indian studies identified used MBSR rather than MBRP — resolve this inconsistency after reading the original papers; (b) effect sizes d=0.61 (craving), 0.48 (impulsivity), 0.72 (mindfulness) are stated to be consistently reported in MBRP trials — I could not verify these figures; (c) the "31% fewer days of substance use" statement for Bowen et al. (2014); (d) "Brief MBI — OUD inpatient (2023, PMC)" lacks full citation details; (e) statements in the Notes file that craving usually peaks in 5–10 minutes and subsides in 20–30 minutes are teaching heuristics, not established parameters; (f) neurobiological statements (prefrontal thickness, amygdala reactivity) should be written as hypotheses.
  • Needed: pilot data, a fidelity measure, pre-registration, and comparison with a longer or group format.
