A. DEPRESSION SCALES

1. Beck Depression Inventory - Second Edition (BDI-II)

Depression
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Severity rating of depressive symptoms
  • Screening for depression in clinical and research settings
  • Monitoring treatment response over time
  • NOT a diagnostic tool — supports clinical judgment
Structure
  • Items: 21 items
  • Response format: 4-point Likert scale (0–3) for each item
  • Subscales: Cognitive-Affective (items 1–14) & Somatic-Performance (items 15–21)
  • Content areas: Sadness, pessimism, past failure, loss of pleasure, guilty feelings, punishment feelings, self-dislike, self-criticalness, suicidal thoughts, crying, agitation, loss of interest, indecisiveness, worthlessness, loss of energy, sleep changes, irritability, appetite changes, concentration difficulty, fatigue, loss of interest in sex
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Score each item 0–3 based on severity endorsed
  • Step 2: Sum all 21 items for total score
  • Score range: 0–63
  • Special rule: If patient selects two responses for one item, score the HIGHER value
  • Item 16 (Sleep) & Item 18 (Appetite): Have both increase and decrease options — score highest endorsed
Score RangeSeverity
0–13Minimal Depression
14–19Mild Depression
20–28Moderate Depression
29–63Severe Depression
Clinical Interpretation
  • High scores (29+): Significant depressive episode; evaluate for suicidality
  • Low scores (0–13): Minimal symptoms; may indicate remission or subclinical presentation
  • Red flag: Item 9 (Suicidal ideation) — any score ≥1 requires immediate clinical follow-up
Administration Details
  • Type: Self-report
  • Time: 5–10 minutes
  • Age: 13 years and above
  • Time frame assessed: Past 2 weeks
Advantages
  • Quick, easy to administer
  • Excellent psychometric properties (high internal consistency α = 0.92)
  • Aligned with DSM-IV/DSM-5 criteria
  • Widely validated across cultures including Indian populations
  • Sensitive to change — useful for treatment monitoring
Limitations
  • Self-report bias (under/over-reporting)
  • Somatic items may inflate scores in medical populations
  • Copyrighted (Pearson) — requires purchase
  • Not suitable for diagnosis alone
  • Cultural interpretation of items may vary
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • PHQ-9: 9-item, free, directly maps DSM-5 criteria; better for screening in primary care
  • HAM-D: Clinician-rated; better for research & severe depression assessment
  • Zung SDS: Self-report, 20 items; older, less sensitive to change
  • CES-D: 20-item epidemiological tool; focus on community screening

2. Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D / HDRS)

Depression
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Clinician-rated severity assessment of depression
  • Gold standard for clinical trials & research
  • Monitoring treatment response (pharmacotherapy & psychotherapy)
  • Evaluating depressive episodes in already-diagnosed patients
Structure
  • Items: 17 items (HAM-D-17; original), also available as 21-item & 24-item versions
  • Response format: Clinician-rated; mix of 0–4 and 0–2 scales
  • 9 items: Scored 0–4 (absent to severe)
  • 8 items: Scored 0–2 (absent, slight/doubtful, clearly present)
  • Key domains: Depressed mood, guilt, suicide, insomnia (early/middle/late), work/activities, retardation, agitation, anxiety (psychic/somatic), somatic symptoms, genital symptoms, hypochondriasis, weight loss, insight
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item based on clinical interview & observation
  • Step 2: Sum all 17 items
  • Score range: 0–52 (17-item version)
  • Note: Items 18–21 (in 21-item version) assess diurnal variation, depersonalization, paranoid symptoms, obsessional symptoms — NOT included in total score
Score Range (17-item)Severity
0–7Normal / Remission
8–13Mild Depression
14–18Moderate Depression
19–22Severe Depression
≥23Very Severe Depression

Response criteria: ≥50% reduction = Response; Score ≤7 = Remission

Clinical Interpretation
  • High scores (≥23): Very severe depressive episode; high functional impairment
  • Low scores (≤7): Clinical remission
  • Red flag: Item 3 (Suicide) — score of 3 or 4 requires immediate risk assessment
  • Emphasis on somatic/vegetative symptoms — may overestimate severity in medically ill
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-rated (semi-structured interview)
  • Time: 15–20 minutes
  • Age: Adults (18+)
  • Training required: Yes, for reliable administration
Advantages
  • Gold standard in depression research
  • Clinician-rated — less subject to response bias
  • Well-established psychometric properties
  • Widely used in pharmacological trials
  • Free to use
Limitations
  • Requires trained clinician — inter-rater reliability concerns
  • Heavy emphasis on somatic/insomnia symptoms
  • Less sensitive to atypical depression features
  • Time-consuming compared to self-report measures
  • Uneven item scoring (mix of 3-point and 5-point items)
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • MADRS: 10-item clinician-rated; more sensitive to change; less somatic emphasis
  • BDI-II: Self-report; quicker; patient perspective
  • PHQ-9: Brief self-report; free; maps to DSM-5 criteria directly
  • QIDS: 16-item; equal weighting of DSM criterion domains
B. ANXIETY SCALES

3. Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)

Anxiety
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Measures severity of anxiety symptoms
  • Discriminates anxiety from depression (focuses on somatic/physiological symptoms)
  • Monitoring treatment response
  • Screening in clinical settings
Structure
  • Items: 21 items
  • Response format: 4-point Likert (0–3): Not at all, Mildly, Moderately, Severely
  • Content: Predominantly somatic/physiological anxiety symptoms (numbness, hot flashes, wobbliness, heart pounding, dizziness, trembling, etc.)
  • Time frame: Past 1 week
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item 0–3
  • Step 2: Sum all 21 items
  • Score range: 0–63
  • No reverse scoring
Score RangeSeverity
0–7Minimal Anxiety
8–15Mild Anxiety
16–25Moderate Anxiety
26–63Severe Anxiety
Clinical Interpretation
  • High scores (26+): Significant somatic anxiety; rule out panic disorder, medical causes
  • Low scores (0–7): Minimal anxiety; possible remission
  • Note: May underestimate cognitive/worry-based anxiety (GAD-type)
  • Red flag: Very high scores with sudden onset — consider panic disorder or medical emergency
Administration Details
  • Type: Self-report
  • Time: 5–10 minutes
  • Age: 17 years and above
Advantages
  • Good discriminant validity (anxiety vs. depression)
  • Quick and easy
  • High internal consistency (α = 0.92)
  • Useful in panic disorder assessment
Limitations
  • Overemphasis on somatic symptoms — may miss cognitive anxiety (worry, apprehension)
  • May inflate scores in medical/elderly populations
  • Copyrighted (Pearson)
  • Less useful for GAD assessment
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • GAD-7: Free, 7-item; better for cognitive/worry symptoms of GAD
  • HAM-A: Clinician-rated; covers both somatic & psychic anxiety
  • STAI: State-Trait; differentiates current vs. trait anxiety
  • DASS-21 (Anxiety subscale): Measures anxiety alongside depression & stress

4. Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAM-A)

Anxiety
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Clinician-rated severity assessment of anxiety
  • Monitoring treatment efficacy in anxiety disorders
  • Research tool for pharmacological trials
  • Assesses both psychic and somatic anxiety
Structure
  • Items: 14 items
  • Response format: Clinician-rated, 5-point scale (0–4) per item
  • Two clusters:
  • Psychic Anxiety (7 items): Anxious mood, tension, fears, insomnia, intellectual/cognitive, depressed mood, behavior at interview
  • Somatic Anxiety (7 items): Muscular, sensory, cardiovascular, respiratory, GI, genitourinary, autonomic symptoms
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item 0 (not present) to 4 (very severe/incapacitating)
  • Step 2: Sum all 14 items
  • Score range: 0–56
  • Subscale scores: Psychic anxiety (items 1–6, 14) + Somatic anxiety (items 7–13)
Score RangeSeverity
<17Mild Anxiety
18–24Mild to Moderate Anxiety
25–30Moderate to Severe Anxiety
≥31Very Severe Anxiety

Response criteria: ≥50% reduction in score = Treatment response

Clinical Interpretation
  • High scores (≥31): Severe, incapacitating anxiety; significant functional impairment
  • Low scores (<17): Mild anxiety; may not require intensive intervention
  • Psychic > Somatic: Suggests generalized anxiety/worry pattern
  • Somatic > Psychic: Suggests prominent physical anxiety; rule out medical causes
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-rated (semi-structured interview)
  • Time: 10–15 minutes
  • Age: Adults (18+)
Advantages
  • Comprehensive coverage of both psychic & somatic anxiety
  • Gold standard for clinical trials
  • Free to use
  • Good sensitivity to treatment change
Limitations
  • Overlap with depression symptoms (Item 6 = depressed mood)
  • Requires trained clinician
  • Poor discriminant validity between anxiety & depression
  • Developed for GAD; less specific for panic/social anxiety/OCD
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • BAI: Self-report; better discriminant validity from depression; somatic focus
  • GAD-7: Brief, free, self-report; specific to GAD
  • STAI: Differentiates state vs. trait anxiety
  • Liebowitz Social Anxiety Scale: Specific for social phobia
C. PSYCHOSIS SCALES

5. Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)

Psychosis / General Psychopathology
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Assesses severity of general psychiatric symptoms
  • Monitoring treatment response in psychotic disorders
  • Quick overall psychopathology assessment
  • Widely used in inpatient and research settings
Structure
  • Items: 18 items (expanded version: 24 items)
  • Response format: Clinician-rated, 7-point scale (1–7) per item
  • 1 = Not present, 2 = Very mild, 3 = Mild, 4 = Moderate, 5 = Moderately severe, 6 = Severe, 7 = Extremely severe
  • Domains covered: Somatic concern, anxiety, emotional withdrawal, conceptual disorganization, guilt, tension, mannerisms, grandiosity, depressed mood, hostility, suspiciousness, hallucinatory behavior, motor retardation, uncooperativeness, unusual thought content, blunted affect, excitement, disorientation
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item 1–7 based on interview + observation
  • Step 2: Sum all 18 items for total score
  • Score range: 18–126 (18-item version)
  • Note: Minimum score is 18 (not 0), since lowest rating per item = 1
Score RangeSeverity
18–31Minimal / Not Ill
32–40Mildly Ill
41–52Moderately Ill
53–65Markedly Ill
≥66Severely Ill
Clinical Interpretation
  • High scores (≥53): Significant psychopathology; likely needs inpatient care
  • Low scores (≤31): Minimal symptoms; possible remission
  • Pattern analysis: Look at individual item clusters for positive symptoms vs. negative symptoms vs. affective symptoms
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-rated
  • Time: 15–20 minutes
  • Age: Adults (18+)
  • Based on: Clinical interview + behavioral observation
Advantages
  • Brief and comprehensive
  • Covers wide range of psychopathology (not limited to psychosis)
  • Good inter-rater reliability with training
  • Free to use
  • Useful in acute settings
Limitations
  • Does not differentiate positive from negative symptoms as well as PANSS
  • Requires trained rater
  • Limited assessment of negative symptoms
  • Less specific than PANSS for schizophrenia research
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • PANSS: 30-item; separate positive, negative & general subscales; more detailed for schizophrenia
  • CGI: 1-item global severity; ultra-brief clinical impression
  • SAPS/SANS: Separate scales for positive & negative symptoms; more detailed item-level assessment

6. Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS)

Schizophrenia / Psychosis
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Comprehensive assessment of schizophrenia symptom dimensions
  • Differentiates positive, negative, and general psychopathology
  • Gold standard for schizophrenia research & drug trials
  • Monitoring treatment response
Structure
  • Items: 30 items total
  • 3 Subscales:
  • Positive Scale (P): 7 items — Delusions, conceptual disorganization, hallucinatory behavior, excitement, grandiosity, suspiciousness, hostility
  • Negative Scale (N): 7 items — Blunted affect, emotional withdrawal, poor rapport, passive/apathetic social withdrawal, difficulty in abstract thinking, lack of spontaneity, stereotyped thinking
  • General Psychopathology (G): 16 items — Somatic concern, anxiety, guilt, tension, mannerisms, depression, motor retardation, uncooperativeness, unusual thought content, disorientation, poor attention, lack of judgment, disturbance of volition, poor impulse control, preoccupation, active social avoidance
  • Response format: 7-point scale (1–7) per item
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item 1–7 based on 30–40 min structured interview (SCI-PANSS)
  • Step 2: Calculate subscale scores:
  • Positive Scale: Sum P1–P7 (Range: 7–49)
  • Negative Scale: Sum N1–N7 (Range: 7–49)
  • General: Sum G1–G16 (Range: 16–112)
  • Total PANSS Score: P + N + G (Range: 30–210)
  • Composite Index: P minus N (indicates predominance of positive vs. negative symptoms)
SubscaleRangeInterpretation
Positive (P)7–49Higher = more positive symptoms
Negative (N)7–49Higher = more negative symptoms
General (G)16–112Higher = more general psychopathology
Total30–210Overall illness severity
Total ScoreApproximate Severity
30–58Mildly Ill
59–75Moderately Ill
76–95Markedly Ill
≥96Severely Ill

Response: ≥20% reduction in total PANSS = minimal response; ≥50% = good response

Clinical Interpretation
  • P > N: Predominant positive symptoms; better prognosis; more responsive to typical antipsychotics
  • N > P: Predominant negative symptoms; poorer prognosis; consider atypical antipsychotics
  • High G scores: Significant general psychopathology beyond core schizophrenia symptoms
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-rated (requires SCI-PANSS structured interview)
  • Time: 30–40 minutes
  • Age: Adults (18+)
  • Training: Required; certification recommended for research
Advantages
  • Most comprehensive scale for schizophrenia
  • Clear separation of symptom dimensions
  • Excellent psychometric properties
  • Standard in clinical trials worldwide
  • Composite index useful for subtype classification
Limitations
  • Time-consuming (30–40 minutes)
  • Requires trained rater & structured interview
  • Copyrighted
  • Negative scale has been criticized for including cognitive items
  • May not capture full deficit syndrome
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • BPRS: Shorter (18 items); quicker; less specific for schizophrenia
  • SAPS/SANS: Separate detailed scales for positive/negative symptoms; more item granularity
  • CGI-SCH: Brief global impression specific to schizophrenia
  • PSP: Personal and Social Performance scale; focuses on functioning rather than symptoms

7. Scale for the Assessment of Positive Symptoms (SAPS)

Psychosis – Positive Symptoms
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Detailed assessment of positive symptoms in schizophrenia
  • Complements SANS for comprehensive symptom profiling
  • Research tool; used in longitudinal monitoring
Structure
  • Items: 34 items + 4 global rating items = 34 items grouped into 4 domains
  • 4 Domains (with global ratings):
  • 1. Hallucinations (7 items + 1 global)
  • 2. Delusions (13 items + 1 global)
  • 3. Bizarre Behavior (4 items + 1 global)
  • 4. Positive Formal Thought Disorder (9 items + 1 global)
  • Response format: 6-point scale (0–5) per item
  • 0 = None, 1 = Questionable, 2 = Mild, 3 = Moderate, 4 = Marked, 5 = Severe
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item 0–5
  • Step 2: Calculate domain scores by summing items within each domain
  • Step 3: Total score = Sum of all items OR sum of 4 global ratings (Summary score)
  • Summary score (4 global ratings): Range 0–20
  • Total composite score: Range 0–170 (all individual items)
  • No formal severity cut-offs published — interpret relative to baseline & norms
DomainNo. of ItemsScore Range
Hallucinations70–35
Delusions130–65
Bizarre Behavior40–20
Positive Formal Thought Disorder90–45
Clinical Interpretation
  • Higher scores: More prominent positive symptoms; florid psychosis
  • Domain-specific analysis: Helps identify predominant symptom type (e.g., hallucination-predominant vs. delusion-predominant)
  • Change scores: Most useful for tracking treatment response over time
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-rated
  • Time: 20–30 minutes (often combined with SANS)
  • Age: Adults
  • Developer: Nancy Andreasen (1984)
Advantages
  • Very detailed item-level assessment of positive symptoms
  • Includes formal thought disorder assessment
  • Free to use
  • Good for research on specific symptom dimensions
Limitations
  • No established severity cut-offs
  • Time-consuming when combined with SANS
  • Less commonly used in clinical practice (PANSS preferred)
  • Requires clinical expertise for rating
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • PANSS Positive subscale: Shorter; part of comprehensive PANSS; established cut-offs
  • BPRS: Briefer overall psychopathology assessment
  • PSYRATS: Specific to hallucinations and delusions; dimensional assessment

8. Scale for the Assessment of Negative Symptoms (SANS)

Psychosis – Negative Symptoms
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Detailed assessment of negative symptoms in schizophrenia
  • Complements SAPS for full symptom profiling
  • Research & clinical monitoring of deficit states
  • Evaluating response to atypical antipsychotics
Structure
  • Items: 25 items grouped into 5 domains (+ 5 global ratings)
  • 5 Domains:
  • 1. Affective Flattening/Blunting (8 items + 1 global)
  • 2. Alogia (5 items + 1 global)
  • 3. Avolition/Apathy (4 items + 1 global)
  • 4. Anhedonia/Asociality (5 items + 1 global)
  • 5. Attention (3 items + 1 global)
  • Response format: 6-point scale (0–5)
  • 0 = None, 1 = Questionable, 2 = Mild, 3 = Moderate, 4 = Marked, 5 = Severe
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item 0–5
  • Step 2: Calculate domain scores (sum items within domain)
  • Step 3: Summary score = Sum of 5 global ratings (Range: 0–25)
  • Total composite score: Sum of all 25 items (Range: 0–125)
DomainItemsRange
Affective Flattening80–40
Alogia50–25
Avolition/Apathy40–20
Anhedonia/Asociality50–25
Attention30–15

Note: No universally established severity cut-offs; interpret relative to baseline and clinical context.

Clinical Interpretation
  • Higher scores: More prominent negative/deficit symptoms; poorer prognosis
  • Domain patterns: Helps plan targeted rehabilitation (e.g., high avolition → behavioral activation)
  • Distinguish: Primary negative symptoms vs. secondary (due to medication, depression, social deprivation)
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-rated (interview + observation)
  • Time: 15–20 minutes
  • Age: Adults
  • Developer: Nancy Andreasen (1984)
Advantages
  • Most detailed scale for negative symptoms
  • 5 clearly defined domains
  • Free to use
  • Good reliability with training
  • Useful for rehabilitation planning
Limitations
  • No formal severity cut-offs
  • Attention subscale may overlap with cognitive deficits
  • Does not distinguish primary vs. secondary negative symptoms
  • Less used in clinical trials now (PANSS-N preferred)
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • PANSS Negative subscale: Shorter; part of PANSS; more widely used in trials
  • BNSS (Brief Negative Symptom Scale): 13-item; newer; better construct validity
  • CAINS: Motivation/Pleasure + Expression domains; aligned with current negative symptom models
  • NSA-16: 16-item; clinician-rated; balances brevity & comprehensiveness
D. MANIA SCALE

9. Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS)

Mania / Bipolar Disorder
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Assesses severity of manic symptoms
  • Monitoring treatment response in bipolar disorder (manic/mixed episodes)
  • Standard outcome measure in mania clinical trials
Structure
  • Items: 11 items
  • Response format: Clinician-rated
  • Scoring: 7 items scored 0–4; 4 items scored 0–8 (double-weighted items)
  • Double-weighted items (0–8): Irritability, Speech, Thought Content, Disruptive/Aggressive Behavior
  • Regular items (0–4): Elevated mood, Increased motor activity, Sexual interest, Sleep, Appearance, Insight, Language-thought disorder
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item based on clinical interview + observation
  • Step 2: 7 items scored 0–4; 4 items scored 0–8
  • Step 3: Sum all items
  • Score range: 0–60
  • Important: Double-weighted items given extra weight due to clinical significance and poor cooperation potential
Score RangeSeverity
0–6Not significant / Remission
7–12Mild Mania
13–19Moderate Mania
20–25Moderate-Severe Mania
≥26Severe Mania

Response: ≥50% reduction = Treatment response; Score ≤12 = Remission (commonly used criterion)

Clinical Interpretation
  • High scores (≥26): Severe manic episode; likely requires hospitalization
  • Low scores (≤6): Remission or euthymic state
  • Red flags: High irritability + disruptive behavior scores = risk of aggression; High thought content score = psychotic mania
  • Note: Does not assess depressive symptoms — use with depression scale for mixed states
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-rated
  • Time: 15–30 minutes
  • Age: Adults (18+); adapted versions for adolescents available
  • Basis: Interview + observation + informant report (last 48 hours)
Advantages
  • Gold standard for manic episode severity
  • Quick and reliable
  • Good sensitivity to change
  • Free to use
  • Double-weighting captures disruptive mania well
Limitations
  • No assessment of depressive symptoms (problem in mixed states)
  • Requires clinician training
  • Limited psychometric data for hypomania
  • Double-weighted items may bias toward irritable mania
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • Bech-Rafaelsen Mania Scale (MAS): 11-item; equal weighting; more psychometrically balanced
  • Altman Self-Rating Mania Scale (ASRM): 5-item self-report; quick screening
  • CARS-M: Clinician-Administered Rating Scale for Mania; distinguishes classic vs. psychotic mania
  • MDQ (Mood Disorder Questionnaire): Self-report screening for bipolar disorder (not severity)
E. OCD SCALE

10. Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS)

Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Gold standard for OCD severity assessment
  • Rates severity independent of type/content of obsessions/compulsions
  • Monitoring treatment response (CBT and pharmacotherapy)
  • Research outcome measure
Structure
  • Items: 10 core items (+ optional investigation/insight items)
  • Two subscales (5 items each):
  • Obsession Subscale (items 1–5): Time occupied, interference, distress, resistance, control
  • Compulsion Subscale (items 6–10): Time occupied, interference, distress, resistance, control
  • Response format: Clinician-rated, 5-point scale (0–4) per item
  • Preceded by: Y-BOCS Symptom Checklist (64+ symptoms) to identify target symptoms
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Administer Y-BOCS Symptom Checklist to identify current obsessions & compulsions
  • Step 2: Rate items 1–5 for obsessions (0–4 each)
  • Step 3: Rate items 6–10 for compulsions (0–4 each)
  • Step 4: Calculate:
  • Obsession subtotal = Sum items 1–5 (Range: 0–20)
  • Compulsion subtotal = Sum items 6–10 (Range: 0–20)
  • Total Y-BOCS = Obsession + Compulsion subtotals (Range: 0–40)
  • Special note: If patient has only obsessions (no compulsions), multiply obsession score × 2 for total equivalent
Total ScoreSeverity
0–7Subclinical
8–15Mild OCD
16–23Moderate OCD
24–31Severe OCD
32–40Extreme OCD

Response criteria: ≥35% reduction = Responder; ≥25% = Partial response; Score ≤12 = Remission (commonly used)

Clinical Interpretation
  • High scores (24+): Severe OCD; significant functional impairment; may need combined CBT + SSRI
  • Low scores (≤7): Subclinical; obsessions/compulsions not clinically significant
  • Obsession > Compulsion score: Predominantly obsessional OCD (ruminations/mental rituals)
  • Red flag: Item 3 (distress) very high with low resistance (item 4) = poor prognosis without treatment
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-rated (semi-structured interview)
  • Time: 15–30 minutes (including symptom checklist)
  • Age: Adults; CY-BOCS for children (ages 6–17)
Advantages
  • Gold standard for OCD severity
  • Content-independent (rates severity regardless of OCD type)
  • Excellent psychometric properties
  • Widely validated across cultures
  • Free to use
Limitations
  • Requires trained clinician
  • Does not assess insight (Item 11 optional, not in total)
  • May not capture avoidance behaviors well
  • Compulsion score may be 0 for purely obsessional OCD (use multiply rule)
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • OCI-R (Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised): Self-report; 18 items; assesses OCD symptom dimensions
  • DOCS (Dimensional OCD Scale): Self-report; 20 items; 4 empirically-based dimensions
  • CY-BOCS: Child/adolescent version; same structure adapted for ages 6–17
  • BABS: Assesses degree of conviction/insight in beliefs (used alongside Y-BOCS)
F. COGNITIVE ASSESSMENT SCALES

11. Mini Mental State Examination (MMSE)

Cognitive Functioning / Dementia
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Brief screening for cognitive impairment
  • Detecting dementia and delirium
  • Monitoring cognitive decline over time
  • Most widely used bedside cognitive screen globally
Structure
  • Items: 11 items (30 points total)
  • Domains assessed:
  • Orientation to Time (5 points)
  • Orientation to Place (5 points)
  • Registration (3 points)
  • Attention & Calculation (5 points) — Serial 7s or WORLD backwards
  • Recall (3 points)
  • Language (8 points) — Naming, repetition, 3-stage command, reading, writing
  • Visuospatial (1 point) — Copy intersecting pentagons
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Score each item correct (1) or incorrect (0)
  • Step 2: Sum all points
  • Total score range: 0–30
  • Higher score = better cognition
ScoreInterpretation
27–30Normal
24–26Mild Cognitive Impairment
18–23Mild Dementia
10–17Moderate Dementia
<10Severe Dementia

Adjust for education: In Indian populations, cut-off of 24 may be too high for illiterate/low-education groups. Cut-off of 23 or lower often used. Some use 25/26 for educated populations.

Clinical Interpretation
  • Score <24: Generally indicates cognitive impairment requiring further evaluation
  • ≥3 point decline over 6 months: Clinically significant deterioration
  • Pattern analysis: Orientation loss early = Alzheimer's; Attention loss = Delirium
  • Red flag: Rapid decline (>5 points in 6 months) = consider delirium, rapidly progressive dementia
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-administered
  • Time: 5–10 minutes
  • Age: Adults (generally 60+, but usable from 18+)
Advantages
  • Quick bedside administration
  • Universally known and recognized
  • Good for serial monitoring
  • Hindi and regional language versions available for India
Limitations
  • Copyrighted (PAR Inc.) since 2001
  • Ceiling effect — misses MCI and early dementia in educated individuals
  • Floor effect in advanced dementia
  • No executive function assessment
  • Education/literacy bias (especially in Indian context)
  • Poor sensitivity for frontal/subcortical dementias
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • MoCA: Better sensitivity for MCI; includes executive function; free; 30 points
  • ACE-III: More comprehensive (100 points); covers 5 cognitive domains; better for differentiating dementia types
  • HMSE (Hindi Mental State Examination): Adapted for Indian illiterate populations
  • CDR (Clinical Dementia Rating): Stages dementia severity based on 6 domains; informant-based

12. Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)

Cognitive Functioning / MCI
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Screening for Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI)
  • More sensitive than MMSE for early/mild cognitive deficits
  • Assesses executive functions (unlike MMSE)
  • Useful across multiple neurological conditions (PD, stroke, TBI)
Structure
  • Items: Multiple tasks, total 30 points
  • Domains assessed (8 cognitive areas):

  • May fluctuate with mood state
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • Y-BOCS Item 11 (Insight): Single item; less detailed; part of Y-BOCS
  • OVIS (Overvalued Ideas Scale): 11 items; assesses overvalued ideation specifically
  • David's Insight Scale (SAI): 3 dimensions of insight; for psychosis
  • BCIS (Beck Cognitive Insight Scale): Self-report; measures cognitive insight in psychosis
K. SUICIDE ASSESSMENT SCALE

23. Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)

Suicide Risk Assessment
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Gold standard for assessing suicidal ideation and behavior
  • Prospective risk assessment and monitoring
  • Distinguishes suicidal ideation from suicidal behavior (critical distinction)
  • Used in clinical settings, emergency departments, research, and FDA-mandated drug trials
  • Available for adults and children/adolescents (age 6+)
Structure
  • Two main sections:
  • Section 1: Suicidal Ideation (5 items) — rated for severity
  • 1. Wish to be dead
  • 2. Non-specific active suicidal thoughts
  • 3. Active suicidal ideation with any methods (not plan) without intent
  • 4. Active suicidal ideation with some intent to act, without specific plan
  • 5. Active suicidal ideation with specific plan and intent
  • Intensity rating (for most severe ideation): Frequency, Duration, Controllability, Deterrents, Reasons (each 1–5)
  • Section 2: Suicidal Behavior (5 categories)
  • 1. Actual attempt
  • 2. Interrupted attempt
  • 3. Aborted attempt
  • 4. Preparatory acts/behavior
  • 5. Non-suicidal self-injurious behavior (NSSI)
  • Response format: Yes/No for presence; severity rated on ordinal categories
  • Versions: Lifetime/Recent, Since Last Visit, Baseline/Screening, Children's version (age 6–11)
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Determine highest level of suicidal ideation endorsed (1–5)
  • Step 2: Rate intensity of most severe ideation (5 subscales, each 1–5; total intensity range: 5–25)
  • Step 3: Determine suicidal behavior category (if any)
  • Step 4: Rate lethality of any attempts (0–5 scale + medical damage)
  • No single total score — interpreted categorically

Children's Version (C-SSRS Children's)
  • Age: 6–11 years (simplified language)
  • Same structure but developmentally appropriate wording
  • Key considerations: Children may not fully understand death's permanence; assess cognitive maturity
  • Collateral information: Always supplement with parent/caregiver interview
Clinical Interpretation
  • Ideation level 4–5: Imminent risk; requires immediate intervention
  • Any suicidal behavior (actual/interrupted/aborted): High-risk; safety planning mandatory
  • High intensity scores: Frequent, uncontrollable thoughts with few deterrents = highest concern
  • Red flags: Ideation level ≥4, any behavior category endorsed, high lethality, low controllability, absence of deterrents
Administration Details
  • Type: Clinician-administered (structured interview)
  • Time: 5–10 minutes
  • Age: Adults (18+); Children/Adolescents (6–17 with adapted version)
  • Training: Free online training available (cssrs.columbia.edu)
  • Setting: Clinical, emergency, research, schools, military
Advantages
  • FDA gold standard for clinical trials
  • Clear distinction between ideation and behavior
  • Free to use (public domain)
  • Validated across populations (adults, adolescents, children)
  • Multiple versions for different timeframes
  • Categorical risk assessment aids clinical decision-making
  • Available in 140+ languages including Hindi
Limitations
  • No single summary score (can be complex to interpret)
  • Relies on patient disclosure (concealment possible)
  • Young children may not comprehend abstract questions about death
  • Snapshot in time — suicidality fluctuates rapidly
  • Does not replace comprehensive suicide risk assessment
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • PHQ-9 Item 9: Single screening item for suicidal thoughts; very brief; low specificity
  • Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSS): 21-item; self-report; quantifies ideation severity
  • SAD PERSONS Scale: 10-item mnemonic-based risk assessment; clinical decision tool
  • SBQ-R (Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire-Revised): 4-item self-report; lifetime + recent
L. CRAVING & SUBSTANCE-SPECIFIC SCALES

24. Obsessive Compulsive Drug Use Scale (OCDUS)

Craving / Substance Use
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Measures intensity and intrusiveness of drug craving using OCD-like dimensions
  • Conceptualizes craving as obsessive thoughts and compulsive drug-seeking behavior
  • Monitors craving severity during treatment and recovery
  • Originally developed for heroin; adapted versions for alcohol (OCDS), cocaine, nicotine
Structure
  • Items: 12 items (OCDUS for drugs); OCDS = 14 items (for alcohol)
  • 3 Subscales:
  • 1. Thoughts & Interference: Frequency, intrusiveness, distress of drug thoughts (items 1–5)
  • 2. Desire & Control: Intensity of desire, ability to resist (items 6–9)
  • 3. Resistance: Efforts to resist drug thoughts and use (items 10–12)
  • Response format: 5-point Likert scale (0–4) per item
  • Time frame: Past 7 days
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Rate each item 0–4
  • Step 2: Sum all items for total score
  • Total score range (OCDUS-12): 0–48
  • Subscale scores: Sum relevant items for each subscale
  • Higher scores = more intense craving
Total ScoreCraving Severity
0–10Minimal craving
11–20Mild craving
21–30Moderate craving
31–40Severe craving
41–48Extreme craving

OCDS (Alcohol version): 14 items; Score range 0–56; Cut-off ≥20 suggests clinically significant craving.

Clinical Interpretation
  • High Thoughts & Interference: Intrusive drug thoughts dominating cognition; poor concentration
  • High Desire & Control: Intense urges with poor ability to resist; high relapse risk
  • High Resistance (low): Patient not actively fighting craving; motivational work needed
  • Increasing scores over time: Relapse risk increasing; intensify intervention
  • Red flag: Total ≥30 + declining resistance = imminent relapse risk
Administration Details
  • Type: Self-report or clinician-administered
  • Time: 5–10 minutes
  • Age: Adults (18+)
  • Versions: OCDUS (drugs), OCDS (alcohol), OCDCS (cocaine), OCDNS (nicotine)
Advantages
  • Captures cognitive dimension of craving (obsessive quality)
  • Multidimensional (thoughts + desire + resistance)
  • Sensitive to treatment change
  • Available for multiple substances
  • Good psychometric properties
  • Theoretically grounded (OCD model of addiction)
Limitations
  • No universally established clinical cut-offs
  • Self-report bias (denial, minimization)
  • Different versions for different substances (not one unified tool)
  • May not capture impulsive/cue-triggered craving well
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • DDQ (Desires for Drug Questionnaire): 13 items; 3 factors; broader craving model
  • ACQ (Alcohol Craving Questionnaire): 47/12 items; multidimensional craving
  • Penn Alcohol Craving Scale (PACS): 5 items; brief; frequency + intensity + duration
  • VAS (Visual Analogue Scale for Craving): Single item 0–100mm; ultra-brief; less reliable

26. Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

Depression Screening
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Brief depression screening and severity measure
  • Directly maps to DSM-5 MDD criteria (9 criteria)
  • Widely used in primary care and research
  • Free, publicly available
Structure
  • Items: 9 items (each = one DSM-5 criterion)
  • Response format: 4-point Likert (0–3): Not at all, Several days, More than half the days, Nearly every day
  • Time frame: Past 2 weeks
  • 10th item (functional impairment): Not scored but clinically informative
Scoring Method
  • Sum all 9 items: Range 0–27
  • Diagnostic algorithm: 5+ items scored ≥2 (including item 1 or 2) = probable MDD

28. Depression Anxiety Stress Scales - 21 (DASS-21)

Depression / Anxiety / Stress
Purpose / Clinical Use
  • Simultaneously measures depression, anxiety, and stress
  • Discriminates between three negative emotional states
  • Screening and severity assessment
  • Widely used in research and clinical settings in India
Structure
  • Items: 21 items (7 per subscale)
  • 3 Subscales (7 items each):
  • Depression: Dysphoria, hopelessness, devaluation of life, self-deprecation, anhedonia, lack of interest, inertia
  • Anxiety: Autonomic arousal, skeletal muscle effects, situational anxiety, subjective anxiety
  • Stress: Nervous arousal, difficulty relaxing, irritability, impatience, overreaction
  • Response format: 4-point scale (0–3): Did not apply, Applied to some degree, Applied a considerable degree, Applied very much/most of the time
  • Time frame: Past 1 week
Scoring Method
  • Step 1: Sum items for each subscale (7 items each)
  • Step 2: MULTIPLY each subscale score by 2 (to match DASS-42 norms)
  • Score range per subscale (after ×2): 0–42

Administration Details
  • Type: Self-report
  • Time: 5–10 minutes
  • Age: 17+ years
  • Free to use (public domain)
Advantages
  • Three constructs in one instrument
  • Free and publicly available
  • Good discriminant validity between depression, anxiety, and stress
  • Widely validated in Indian populations
  • Useful for non-clinical and clinical samples
Limitations
  • Must remember to multiply by 2 for DASS-21 (common error)
  • Not diagnostic; screening only
  • "Stress" construct not well-mapped to DSM categories
  • Anxiety subscale heavily somatic (like BAI)
Alternatives / Similar Scales
  • PHQ-9 + GAD-7: Separate tools for depression & anxiety; DSM-aligned
  • BDI-II + BAI: Separate Beck tools; copyrighted; more detailed
  • K-10 (Kessler Psychological Distress Scale): 10-item; general distress; widely used in epidemiology
