1History and Development of the Rorschach

The Rorschach is a performance-based (the person does a task while the examiner watches, rather than simply reporting on themselves) personality assessment. A person is shown ten standardized inkblots and asked what they might be. How the person perceives, organizes, and explains the ambiguous blots is treated as a sample of how their personality "works" when faced with an unstructured problem. To understand the viva, you must understand why there are competing "systems" and how the field arrived at Exner's Comprehensive System and, later, R-PAS.

1.1 Hermann Rorschach and the birth of the method

  • Hermann Rorschach (1884–1922) was a Swiss psychiatrist. Working with psychiatric patients, he noticed that people with different conditions responded differently to inkblots. He systematized a set of blots and a method of eliciting and recording responses.
  • He published Psychodiagnostik in 1921, introducing the ten blots and an early scoring approach that already emphasized location (where on the blot), determinants (what blot feature drove the response — form, movement, colour), and content, plus the proportion of popular/original answers.
  • Rorschach framed the test primarily as a study of perception ("how" a person sees) rather than a free-association study of unconscious content ("what" the symbols mean). This perceptual emphasis is why modern CS/R-PAS treat it as a performance task.
  • He died in 1922, aged 37, barely a year after publication, leaving the method young and unfinished. This early death is the single most important fact for the viva: it is why multiple independent systems developed.

1.2 After Rorschach's death — divergent development (1920s–1960s)

With no founder to arbitrate, several American and European workers each extended the method in their own direction. By the 1960s there were five major, mutually incompatible "systems," each with its own administration wording, coding symbols, and norms. A response scored one way in Beck's system could be scored differently in Klopfer's — the same test had become, in practice, several different tests.

1.3 John Exner and the Comprehensive System (1970s onward)

  • John E. Exner Jr. (1928–2006) conducted an empirical comparison of the five major systems (published 1969) and found they diverged so much that research results could not be pooled.
  • In 1974 he published the first edition of the Comprehensive System (CS), selecting the most empirically defensible and reliably codable components from the existing systems and welding them into one standardized procedure with uniform administration, coding, norms, and an interpretive framework.
  • The CS became the dominant worldwide approach for ~30 years. The repository's Workbook and interpretation Manual are CS documents, so Files 1 and 2 teach the CS.
The repository's interpretation Manual (Exner, CS) is organized around interpretive clusters and opens its interpretation half at Chapter 13 ("General Guidelines Regarding Interpretation"). (Manual, Ch. 13.)

1.4 R-PAS (2011) — the newest major development

  • After Exner's death, the CS was not further revised. A research group led by Gregory J. Meyer, Donald J. Viglione, Joni L. Mihura, Robert E. Erard, and Philip Erdberg built the Rorschach Performance Assessment System (R-PAS), published 2011.
  • R-PAS retained only CS variables with the strongest empirical/meta-analytic support, added international norms, converted scores to standardized (M=100, SD=15) scales, and introduced R-Optimized administration to control the number of responses. (R-PAS is covered in detail in File 2, §10, and compared to the CS.)
Three eras to recite in a viva: (1) Rorschach 1921 — the method; (2) the "five systems" era (Beck, Klopfer, Hertz, Piotrowski, Rapaport–Schafer) — fragmentation; (3) integration — Exner's CS (1974) and then R-PAS (2011).

2Major Rorschach Systems

Because the method fragmented after 1922, a viva examiner will test whether you understand that the "systems" are not interchangeable. Each has its own administration, coding symbols, and norms, so a raw code or a cutoff from one system is meaningless inside another.

Never say "the Rorschach score for X is...". Always say "in the Comprehensive System, X is coded/calculated as...". Specifying the system is the single clearest marker of competence in a viva.

2.2 The Comprehensive System (CS) — Exner

  • Originator: John Exner (1974), integrating the empirically best parts of the five systems.
  • Theory: Deliberately atheoretical/empirical — scores are retained because data support them, not because a theory predicts them.
  • Administration/inquiry: Standardized seating (side-by-side), free-association ("What might this be?") across all 10 cards, then a separate Inquiry for every response to clarify location and determinants. Verbatim recording throughout.
  • Coding: Seven categories per response — Location, Developmental Quality, Determinant(s), Form Quality, Content, Popular, Special Scores — plus the Z (organizational) score.
  • Interpretation: A Structural Summary of ratios/percentages/indices, interpreted cluster by cluster (controls, affect, processing, mediation, ideation, self-perception, interpersonal) with special indices (PTI, DEPI, CDI, S-CON, HVI, OBS).
  • Strengths: Standardized, reliable coding, large norms, broad clinical adoption.
  • Limitations: Some variables criticized for weak validity; norms questioned (over-pathologizing debate of the 2000s); not revised after 2006.

2.3 R-PAS — Rorschach Performance Assessment System

  • Originators: Meyer, Viglione, Mihura, Erard, Erdberg (2011). Grew directly out of the CS.
  • Theory: Rorschach as a behavioural performance task — "the personality in action" — interpreting what the person does with the blots.
  • Administration: R-Optimized — the examiner prompts for more responses when few are given and gently pulls back when many are given, steering the record toward an optimal range (about 18–27 responses) and largely removing the need for re-administration. (Verified: Meyer et al., 2011; see File 2 §10 for sources.)
  • Coding/variables: Keeps evidence-supported CS codes (with some renaming, e.g., Complexity, and dropped/added variables); results are expressed as standard scores with percentiles against international norms.
  • Interpretation: Organized into Page 1 (reliable, well-supported variables) and Page 2 (more tentative), each with standardized scores.
  • Strengths: Tighter evidence base, controlled R, modern norms, courtroom-defensible structure.
  • Limitations: Newer and less universally taught; the R-Optimized change itself has been debated (though meta-analyses find it does not distort projective material).

Which system is associated with Exner, and what did integrating the earlier systems achieve?

Exner created the Comprehensive System (1974). Integration gave the field one standardized administration, coding, and normative framework so that reliability improved and research findings could finally be pooled, instead of five incompatible "Rorschachs."

Why is it inappropriate to use the coding rules of one system with the interpretive rules of another?

Because each system's norms and interpretive cutoffs were derived from responses coded by that same system's rules. Mixing them breaks the chain of evidence: a value produced by CS coding has no validated meaning in a Klopfer or R-PAS interpretive table, so any conclusion drawn would be uninterpretable.

Which procedure applies when response productivity is unusually low?

This is system-specific, so do not quote a universal rule. In the CS, a protocol with fewer than 14 responses (R<14) is considered potentially unreliable and is re-administered (the person is asked to go through the cards again and give more answers); if still <14, interpretation proceeds only with great caution. In R-PAS, the problem is prevented at the source: R-Optimized administration prompts for additional responses during the test so that very short records rarely occur. The two systems solve the same problem by different means — never say "below 14, switch systems."

A frequent viva trap: claiming "below 14 responses you must use R-PAS." That is wrong. The R<14 rule is a CS re-administration rule; R-PAS is a different system with its own (R-Optimized) solution. Attribute each rule to its system.

2.5 Why systems must not be treated as interchangeable

The same inkblots are used, but administration wording, which features count as which determinant, the form-quality tables, the norms, and the interpretive cutoffs all differ. A "CF" in CS and a "CF" in another framework are not guaranteed to mean the same thing or to map onto the same norms. Treating them as one would be like comparing temperatures while mixing Celsius and Fahrenheit without converting.

3Purpose, Nature and Structure of the Test

3.1 What the Rorschach is and what it assesses

The Rorschach presents ten standardized inkblots and asks the person, "What might this be?" The task is deliberately ambiguous: there is no correct answer, so the person must impose organization on an unclear stimulus. How they do this — what they notice, how accurately they see, how they combine and explain features, and what themes emerge — is sampled as a reflection of personality processes.

It is used to assess (as supported by the CS): perceptual accuracy and reality testing, thinking/ideation (organization, flexibility, disturbance), affect (how emotion is processed and modulated), coping resources and stress tolerance, self-perception, and interpersonal perception.

3.2 Projective vs performance-based framing

Historically the Rorschach was called a "projective" test — the idea that ambiguous stimuli pull out ("project") inner needs and conflicts. The CS, and especially R-PAS, re-frame it as a performance-based task: the person performs a visual-cognitive problem-solving act, and the coded behaviours are the data. Both framings coexist, but the modern, defensible emphasis is on observable performance.

3.4 Achromatic, chromatic and mixed cards — why it matters

  • Achromatic (I, IV, V, VI, VII): only black/grey/white — any colour determinant coded here must be achromatic colour (C') or shading, never chromatic colour.
  • Mixed (II, III): black plus red — both achromatic and chromatic colour are possible.
  • Chromatic (VIII, IX, X): full colour — chromatic colour (FC/CF/C) becomes central; these cards probe emotional responsiveness.

3.5 Administration format, uses, and appropriate inferences

  • Individual administration only in clinical use (group/slide versions exist historically for research but are not CS-standard).
  • Uses: personality description, clinical formulation (e.g., contribution to questions of reality testing, coping, affect regulation), treatment planning, and research.
  • Appropriate inferences: cautious hypotheses about processes (e.g., "this person tends to rely on emotion over reflection"), always integrated with history, interview, and other tests.
  • Inappropriate inferences: a specific diagnosis from a single code; statements of certainty; "detecting lies"; culture-blind pathologizing.
Reliability/validity & culture: coding reliability is good when examiners are trained; validity is strong for some variables (e.g., thought disturbance, reality testing) and weaker for others. Norms must fit the person's cultural/linguistic/developmental background. The Rorschach is one source among several.

4Administration: Step by Step

The CS prescribes a fixed sequence so that records from different examiners are comparable. The whole test runs in two phases: Response (Free Association) for all 10 cards, then Inquiry for all 10 cards (Inquiry gets its own Section 5).

4.1 Preparation and materials

  • The 10 standard cards in order; a location sheet (a small printed copy of each blot where the examiner marks the area used); recording forms; pen; a quiet, private room.
  • Seat side-by-side (examiner slightly behind/beside the person), not face-to-face — this reduces the examiner's facial expressions influencing responses and lets the examiner see the card as the person does.

Why: standard materials and seating keep the stimulus situation constant. Record: nothing yet except setup notes. Avoid: face-to-face seating; a cluttered/interrupted room.

Why side-by-side seating?

To minimize examiner cues (expressions, gaze) that could shape responses, and so the examiner sees the blot in the same orientation as the person.

4.2 Rapport and instructions

Build brief, neutral rapport, then give the standardized instruction. The CS opening question is essentially: "What might this be?"

  • Do: keep instructions minimal and non-leading; answer "how many?" with "it's up to you"; allow the person to turn the card.
  • Avoid: explaining what others "usually see," suggesting there is a right answer, or telling them how many responses to give.
Illustrative dialogue — instructions (fictional)

Person: "What am I supposed to do?"
Examiner: "I'll show you some cards, and I'd like you to tell me what they might be."
Person: "How many things should I say?"
Examiner: "That's up to you."
Person: "Can I turn it?"
Examiner: "It's up to you."

Record: the fact that standard instructions were given; any questions the person asked. Common viva Q: "What do you reply if asked how many responses to give?" → "It's up to you" (never specify a number).

4.3 Response (Free-Association) phase — presenting the cards

  • Hand each card in the standard upright orientation, in order I→X.
  • Record every word verbatim, plus card rotations (note position symbols), pointing, and response time if the system/trainer requires.
  • Be non-directive: if the person gives only one response to Card I, you may once encourage ("take your time, people often see more than one thing"), but do not push on later cards.
  • If a person gives an enormous number of responses, you may gently move on after a reasonable number on early cards.

Why: verbatim wording is the only basis for later determinant and special-score coding. Avoid: paraphrasing, interpreting aloud, or asking "why" during this phase (clarification belongs to Inquiry).

"Examiners should never fail to make a verbatim record of the verbalizations of the subject." Verbatim wording is required because determinants (especially shading distinctions) and special scores depend on the exact words. (CS, as extracted in the repository coding guide.)

4.4 Response productivity (R)

  • R = total number of responses across all 10 cards.
  • CS rule: if R < 14, the record is treated as potentially unreliable and re-administered (see §2.4 and the caution there).
  • Very high R is not automatically "healthier"; it must be interpreted against other variables (see File 2).
Do not equate "more responses" with "better functioning." R is a context variable; many ratios are read relative to R.

4.5 Inquiry, testing-the-limits, questions, concluding

  • Inquiry follows after all cards are seen (full detail in §5).
  • Testing the limits (optional, after the formal Inquiry): the examiner may check whether the person can see a common percept they omitted (e.g., "some people see an animal here — can you?"). It is not coded into the structural data; it is purely clinical/qualitative and must never contaminate the formal record.
  • Handling questions: redirect neutrally ("there are no right answers; whatever it looks like to you"). Do not reassure in ways that imply correctness.
  • Brief/unclear/unusual responses: note them verbatim; clarify in Inquiry, never by leading in the Response phase.
  • Concluding: thank the person; record behavioural observations (cooperation, affect, latency, card-handling, comments).
Testing-the-limits data are never entered into coding or the Structural Summary. Keep them separate.

4.6 Recording behavioural observations

Note attitude (guarded, eager), emotional reactions to particular cards, response latency, frequent card turning, critical comments ("this is ugly"), and any impairment that could affect validity. These contextualize the numbers later.

5The Inquiry Phase

The Inquiry is where the examiner finds out what the person must be able to code: exactly where the percept was (location) and exactly what blot features produced it (determinants). It is not a second chance to give new responses and not an interrogation of meaning.

5.1 Purpose and timing

  • Timing: after all 10 cards have been seen in the Response phase. The examiner returns to each response in order.
  • Purpose: clarify (1) location, (2) determinants, and (3) exactly what was seen (for content and form quality) — without adding or changing the original percept.
Inquiry is not a re-administration. Its job is to clarify responses already given, not to elicit new ones. (This is a classic File 3 viva question.)

5.2 The standard non-directive technique

The examiner re-reads the person's exact words and asks open prompts:

  • "I'm going to read back what you said, and I'd like you to show me where you saw it and what made it look like that."
  • "Help me see it the way you did."
  • "What about the blot made it look like [the object]?"

5.3 What to clarify, and the cardinal rule

  • Location: have the person outline the area; mark the location sheet; compare to the D/Dd tables.
  • Determinants: establish whether form, movement, colour, achromatic colour, texture, vista, diffuse shading, dimensionality, pair, or reflection contributed — but only if the person's own words show it.
  • Form/movement/colour/shading/depth: listen for the person's spontaneous language ("fuzzy," "bright red," "floating," "far away").
No leading questions. Never ask "Was it the colour?" or "Did the shape remind you?" A leading question manufactures a determinant the person did not actually use, which corrupts the coding and everything downstream. Determinants must be articulated by the person, not supplied by the examiner.
Golden rule of coding: a determinant is coded only if the Response or Inquiry gives evidence that the blot feature contributed. When "dark"/"light" is ambiguous between achromatic colour (C') and diffuse shading (Y), the CS steps down to Y.

5.4 Recording inquiry material

Record the Inquiry verbatim too, keyed to each response, with the location marked. Note card rotations. Do not summarize ("said it was the shape") — write the person's words ("the pointy bits on top are the ears").

5.5 Ten fictional inquiry examples

Each example shows: the original response, what is unclear, a neutral inquiry, a plausible clarification, the coding-relevant information obtained, and what must not be inferred from the response alone. (All fictional, for teaching.)

Example 1 — Card V "A bat"

Unclear: which feature drove it; is movement present?
Neutral inquiry: "What made it look like a bat?"
Clarification: "Just the shape — the wings out to the sides and the little feet."
Coding-relevant: whole blot (W); only shape used → determinant F; content Animal (A); likely Popular.
Must NOT infer: do not assume movement ("flying") — it was not stated, so no FM.

Example 2 — Card II "Two bears"

Unclear: are the bears doing something (movement)? pair?
Neutral inquiry: "Help me see the two bears."
Clarification: "They're touching their paws together, like nose to nose."
Coding-relevant: animal doing an animal-natural action → FM; two identical symmetrical objects → Pair (2).
Must NOT infer: do not call it cooperative (COP) unless the interaction is clearly positive/cooperative — "touching paws" is borderline and needs the person's framing.

Example 3 — Card VI "An animal skin"

Unclear: texture vs plain form.
Neutral inquiry: "What about it looks like a skin?"
Clarification: "It looks furry and soft, the way the shading goes light and dark."
Coding-relevant: tactile quality from shading → Texture; form primary → FT; content animal skin (Ad); Popular for Card VI.
Must NOT infer: do not code texture if the person only named "a skin" without any tactile/shading language — "skin" alone is not enough for T.

Example 4 — Card II "Blood"

Unclear: any form? pure colour?
Neutral inquiry: "What made it look like blood?"
Clarification: "It's just red, like a splash — no particular shape."
Coding-relevant: colour only, no form → C (pure colour); content Blood (Bl); no form quality (formless).
Must NOT infer: do not code CF unless some vague form is present; "no particular shape" = pure C.

Example 5 — Card III "Two people"

Unclear: movement? active/passive? integrated (DQ+)?
Neutral inquiry: "Show me the two people and what they're doing."
Clarification: "They're bending over, lifting a pot between them."
Coding-relevant: human kinesthetic action → M (active, Ma); two areas meaningfully related → DQ+; Pair; Popular (two human figures).
Must NOT infer: do not assume cooperation = COP automatically; "lifting a pot between them" is a reasonable COP candidate but should rest on the person's clearly cooperative framing.

Example 6 — Card IV "Something dark and scary"

Unclear: is "dark" colour (C') or shading (Y)? form?
Neutral inquiry: "What makes it look dark and scary?"
Clarification: "It's black, like it's made of shadow, kind of shapeless."
Coding-relevant: "dark" intent ambiguous → CS steps down to diffuse shading; little form → YF (or Y if truly formless).
Must NOT infer: do not code C' ("black as a colour") unless the person clearly uses blackness as a colour property; when ambiguous, code Y.

Example 7 — Card VII "A woman's face in the mirror"

Unclear: reflection vs pair?
Neutral inquiry: "Tell me about the mirror part."
Clarification: "One side is her, the other side is her reflection in a mirror."
Coding-relevant: explicit mirror/reflection language → Reflection Fr; Pair is not coded when Reflection is coded.
Must NOT infer: do not code both reflection and pair; they are mutually exclusive.

Example 8 — Card VIII "A colourful animal climbing"

Unclear: colour role (FC vs CF)? movement?
Neutral inquiry: "What made it look like that?"
Clarification: "It's shaped exactly like a little animal, and it happens to be pink; it's climbing up the side."
Coding-relevant: definite shape first, colour secondary → FC; animal-natural action → FM (blend FMa.FC); Popular (Card VIII lateral animal).
Must NOT infer: do not code CF — form clearly dominates ("shaped exactly like"), so colour is secondary.

Example 9 — Card IX "Just a mess of colours"

Unclear: is there any form? is it an abstraction (AB)?
Neutral inquiry: "Help me see it."
Clarification: "There's no shape, the colours just feel chaotic, like confusion itself."
Coding-relevant: colour only, no form → C; abstract concept represented by colour → special score AB; content Human Experience/idiographic as appropriate.
Must NOT infer: do not read "chaotic/confusion" as evidence of clinical confusion in the person — it is a coding datum (AB), not a diagnosis.

Example 10 — Card X "A pelvis, X-ray"

Unclear: what created the X-ray look — shading or just labeling?
Neutral inquiry: "What makes it look like an X-ray?"
Clarification: "The lighter and darker bits make it look see-through, like film."
Coding-relevant: see-through quality from shading, form present → FY; content Anatomy/X-ray (An, Xy).
Must NOT infer: do not read "pelvis/X-ray" as a bodily-preoccupation diagnosis; An+Xy is tallied and interpreted only in context (File 2).

Never coach a person toward a particular answer or toward a "better" determinant. The Inquiry extracts what is already there; it does not improve the response. Ethical and valid coding depends on this.

6Complete Coding Guide

This follows the CS coding sequence used in the workbook. Every response is coded across seven categories, always in this order, plus the Z (organizational) score:

Location → Developmental Quality → Determinant(s) → Form Quality → (Pair) → Content → Popular → [Z score] → Special Scores
Every response must receive a Location, a DQ, at least one Determinant, a Form Quality (unless formless), and at least one Content. Pair, Popular, Z, and Special Scores are added only when they apply. A single omission (e.g., a missed texture) can cascade into wrong indices — review all coding before interpreting.

6A. Location & Developmental Quality

Location — where on the blot

  • Decision rules: code what was actually used, not what the person claims ("the whole thing" but describes a part → D/Dd). S is never coded alone.
  • Clinical relevance (coding only; interpret in File 2): balance of W:D:Dd reflects how the person allocates attention (global vs detail vs idiosyncratic).
Three examples

1. Card V "a whole butterfly" → W.

Common errors: coding W when a small-but-meaningful area is omitted; mis-sorting D vs Dd without the tables; forgetting the S modifier.
Distinguish from DQ: Location = where; DQ = how well organized. They are different questions about the same response.

Does using the whole blot (W) automatically mean sophisticated organization?

No. W is a location. Organization/integration is captured by DQ and the Z score. A whole-blot "bat" (W) with no integration is DQo; it is not more "organized" than a well-integrated two-area D+ response.

Developmental Quality (DQ) — how well organized

CodeNameCriterionExample
+SynthesizedTwo+ areas/parts, each with form demand, meaningfully related"Two people lifting a pot"

v/+Vague-synthesizedTwo+ areas related, but at least one has no form demand"Clouds around a mountain"

  • Form demand = the object naturally has a specific shape (a bat does; smoke/fog/blood-splatter do not).
  • Decision tree: integrated? → yes: all parts have form? +/v/+; no: single object has specific form? o/v.

Common errors: calling two separately-named objects "+" without a meaningful relationship (that is "o"); treating "two people" as "+" when no interaction is described.
Clinical relevance: more DQ+ suggests effortful integration; many DQv suggests vague, under-elaborated processing — interpret cautiously and in context (File 2).

How is location distinguished from developmental quality?

Location answers where (W/D/Dd/S). DQ answers how well the percept is organized/integrated (+, o, v/+, v). The same W response can be DQ+ (integrated scene) or DQo (single object).

6B. Determinants — the heart of coding

A determinant is the blot feature that made the response look the way it did (form, movement, colour, achromatic colour, shading, dimensionality, pair, reflection). A response can have one determinant or several (a blend, written with dots, e.g., FMa.FC).

Golden rule: code a determinant only when the Response or Inquiry gives evidence the feature contributed. Never assume.

Form (F)

Response based only on shape/contour. The moment any other feature contributes, it is no longer pure F (it becomes FC, FT, M, etc.). Pure F is never part of a blend.

  • "A map — it's shaped like a country" = F
  • "A red butterfly" = FC (not F — colour contributed)

Related concept — Lambda (L): the proportion of pure-F responses, reflecting an economical/simplifying tactic (formula in §7).

Movement — M, FM, m (never physically in the blot; always projected)

CodeWhat movesRuleExample
MHuman / human-like, OR an animal doing a human-only actKinesthetic human activity"A woman dancing"; "two bears playing chess" (human-only act → M)
FMAnimal doing a species-natural actNatural animal motion"A bird flying"; "two bears wrestling"
mInanimate object / natural force, or motion without volitionInanimate movement/tension"Smoke rising"; "an explosion"; "a leaf falling"

Each is also marked active (a) or passive (p): Ma = "fighting"; Mp = "sleeping". Written Ma, FMp, ma, etc.

The decision: human (or human-only act) → M; animal natural act → FM; inanimate/force/no volition → m.

Common errors: coding FM for an animal doing a human-only act (should be M); missing the a/p marker; coding M for a static figure with no postural tension.

Chromatic colour — FC, CF, C (the form–colour hierarchy)

CodeDominanceExample
FCForm > colour (definite shape, colour adds)"A red butterfly" (shape defines it)
CFColour > form (colour drives it, vague shape)"A colourful paint splatter"
CColour only, no form"Blood" (just the redness)
Step-down principle: when in doubt between FC and CF, code the less form-dominated one (CF). Clear evidence of form dominance is required for FC.

Achromatic colour — FC', C'F, C'

Black/grey/white used as a colour (not as shading). "A black cat" = FC'; "darkness/night, shapeless" = C'.

C' vs Y (the most common determinant error): if the intent of "light"/"dark" is unclear, code diffuse shading Y, not C'. Code C' only when blackness/whiteness is clearly used as a colour property ("black like coal").

Shading — Texture (T), Vista (V), Diffuse (Y)

FamilyImpression createdCodesSignal words
TextureHow it would feel to touchFT, TF, Tfurry, soft, rough, fuzzy, silky, wet
VistaDepth from shading (near/far)FV, VF, V"looks far away because it's darker/lighter," "a valley behind"
DiffuseGeneral light/dark, no touch/depthFY, YF, Yshadowy, smoky, foggy, X-ray, transparent
Vista (V) vs Form Dimension (FD): both create depth, but V uses shading and FD uses size/shape. "Farther because it's darker" = V; "farther because it's smaller" = FD.

Form Dimension (FD)

Depth/3-D created by size or shape differences, no shading. Always written FD (no hierarchy). "A road narrowing into the distance" = FD.

Pair (2) and Reflection (Fr, rF)

  • Pair (2): two identical objects based on the blot's symmetry. "Two butterflies" → (2).
  • Reflection (Fr/rF): one object and its mirror image ("a swan and its reflection in water"). Written Fr (form primary) or rF (reflection dominant, vague form).
If the words "reflection/mirror/reflected" are used → Reflection. Two identical objects with no reflection language → Pair. When Reflection is coded, Pair is NOT coded for that response (mutually exclusive).

Blends and blend-sequencing

When two+ determinants contribute, write them with dots in CS order: movement → chromatic colour → achromatic colour → shading (T,V,Y) → FD → reflection. Example: "a red bird flying" = FMa.FC; "a furry black bear" = FT.FC'.

Special blendContainsWhy it matters
Colour-Shading blendchromatic colour + a shading/C'Ambivalent/confusing affect (e.g., CF.FC')
Shading blendtwo+ different shadingsPainful, complex emotional experience
m + Y blendinanimate movement + diffuse shadingSituational stress marker

Three determinant examples:

Determinant practice

1. "A grey wolf, you can tell it's furry" → FT (form + texture).
2. "Bright red blood dripping down" → C.m? — careful: if "dripping" implies motion and it's pure red with no shape, code ma.C; if a shape is described, CF or Cma as the words support. Borderline — code from the exact words.
3. "A person seeing themselves in a mirror" → Fr (reflection; no pair).

6C. Form Quality (FQ)

FQ rates how well the percept fits the contours of the area used — i.e., perceptual accuracy. It is not about the content's meaning.

CodeLabelMeaning
+Superior-overelaboratedAccurate and unusually enriched form detail (rare)
oOrdinaryCommon, good fit; listed as ordinary in the FQ tables
uUnusualUncommon but the contours do support it (you can see it)
-Minus/distortedPoor fit; contours do not support it (you cannot see it)
(none)No formFormless determinants (pure C, T, V, Y, C') — FQ cannot be assigned
FQ is location-specific and determined by the CS Form Quality tables (built on large samples). You cannot judge FQ by "eyeballing" — you must consult the table for that exact area. The same content can be FQo in one area and FQ- in another.
  • Procedure: identify exact location → look it up in the FQ table → if listed as o/u/- use that; if not listed, judge whether contours support it (yes → u; no → −). FQ+ requires it to be listed and enriched.
  • Common error: over-using "−". Many unusual percepts are u (legitimate fit), not −. Always check the table.
  • Distinguish FQ from content: "a monster" can be FQo if its shape fits well; the bizarre content does not make the form "minus."
FQ examples

1. Card V whole "a bat" → FQo (ordinary in the table).
2. Card I centre "a jack-o-lantern" → FQu (uncommon but the shape supports it).
3. Card V whole "a giraffe" → FQ- (the whole-V contour does not support a giraffe).
Table-dependent: any FQ judgment that hinges on a specific area must be checked against the actual FQ table/location — do not memorize a response as universally o or −.

Can a Popular response have poor form quality?

Usually P responses are FQo, but P is assigned on location + content criteria, not on FQ. A response can, in principle, be a Popular and still not be FQo — P and FQ are coded independently.

What is the difference between a determinant and a content code?

A determinant is why the blot looked like the object (the feature used: form, colour, movement, shading). Content is what category the object belongs to. "A red butterfly flying" → determinant FMa.FC, content A.

Organizational Activity (Z)

A Z score is assigned when the person organizes blot elements into a meaningful relationship. It captures cognitive organizing effort — which is distinct from merely using an area.

TypeWhen assigned
ZWA W response with form (the whole was organized)
ZATwo+ adjacent D areas meaningfully integrated
ZDTwo+ distant D areas meaningfully integrated
ZSWhite space integrated with adjacent areas

Each card has specific Z values (from the Z table). Zf = count of Z responses; Zsum = sum of their Z values; Zest = expected Zsum for that Zf; Zd = Zsum − Zest (over- vs under-incorporation).

Prerequisites for Z: there must be organization — either a form-based W, or a meaningful relating of separate areas, or meaningful use of space. Simply using the whole blot as a single vague thing (Wv) does not earn ZW. Organization ≠ location.

What is organizational activity, what are its prerequisites, and does whole-blot use automatically establish it?

It is the cognitive work of relating blot elements into a meaningful whole, scored as Z. Prerequisites: a form-bearing W, or integration of two+ areas, or meaningful space integration. Whole-blot use does not automatically establish it — a vague Wv (e.g., "just clouds over the whole thing") has no organizational Z. Organization is distinct from location and from DQ, though it relates to both.

Common Z mistakes: giving ZW to a formless Wv; crediting Z for two areas that are merely named together without a meaningful relationship; selecting the wrong card-specific Z value. Always consult the Z table.

What makes a response qualify for a special score, and can a special score alone establish a diagnosis?

It qualifies when the person's exact verbalization meets the criterion (e.g., an impossible two-object relationship → FAB). No — a single special score never establishes a diagnosis; it contributes to a weighted pattern (e.g., WSum6, PTI) interpreted with all other data.

7Structural Summary, Calculations & Formulas

Once every response is coded, the counts are tallied and combined into the Structural Summary: a set of ratios, percentages, and indices. File 1 shows you how to calculate them; File 2 interprets them. Worked numbers below use the two fictional structural summaries carried through these files (a higher-R case, "A", R=28; and a low-R case, "B", R=14).

All cutoffs/thresholds quoted are CS values. Do not apply them to R-PAS. Never invent a cutoff; where a value depends on normative tables (FQ, Z, form-quality-based XA%), say so.

7.1 Core counts

R = total number of responses. Lambda (L) = Pure F ÷ (R − Pure F)

Lambda — meaning of symbols: Pure F = number of pure-form responses; R = total responses. Where values come from: the determinant tallies. Worked (A): if Pure F = 8 and R = 28, L = 8 ÷ (28−8) = 8÷20 = 0.40. Interpretation (plain): higher L = more economical/avoidant engagement; L > 0.99 flags an avoidant simplifying style. Common errors: dividing by R instead of (R − PureF). Viva: "What does a high Lambda mean and how is it computed?"

The CS interpretation routine reviews these "Capacity for control / Situation-related stress" variables early, and treats EB as a stylistic indicator only when EA is sufficiently large and one side exceeds the other by the required margin. (Manual, Ch. 13 cluster table & Ch. 14–15.)

Worked example (Case A)

Sum M = 2, WSumC = 10 → EB = 2:10; EA = 12.0. FM=4, m=4, C'=1, T=0, V=0, Y=1 → eb = 8:2, es = 10. Remove excess m (4→1 leaves 3 excess) and excess Y (1→1 leaves 0): Adj es = 7. With EA 12 vs es 10, D = 0; EA 12 vs Adj es 7, Adj D = +1. EBPer = 10÷2 = 5.0 (markedly pervasive extratensive).

Interpretation (plain, see File 2): strong resources (EA=12); baseline control above average (Adj D +1); currently trimmed to average by mild situational load (D 0 < Adj D); rigidly emotion-driven coping (EB right-side dominant, EBPer 5.0).

Validity gate: if EA ≤ ~3.5, EB is not a valid style indicator (too few data points). In Case B, EB = 2:0 with EA = 2.0 → the apparent "introversive" reading is invalid; with L=1.0 the style is better described as avoidant. Always check EA before interpreting EB.

Common calculation errors: forgetting the colour weights in WSumC; not subtracting excess m/Y (only the amount above 1) for Adj es; reading D/Adj D without the conversion table.
