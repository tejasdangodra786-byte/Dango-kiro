Part I — What the P-F Study Is, and the Theory Behind It

1. In one line, one paragraph, and in depth

One-sentence

The Rosenzweig Picture-Frustration Study (P-F Study) is a semi-projective, cartoon-based instrument that measures a person's characteristic reactions to everyday frustration along two dimensions — the direction of aggression and the type of aggression.

One paragraph. Developed by Saul Rosenzweig out of his experimental work on frustration and repression, the P-F Study presents 24 cartoon-like drawings, each showing two people in a mildly frustrating situation. One figure speaks words that frustrate (or describe the frustration of) the other; the second figure has an empty speech balloon. The examinee writes the first reply that comes to mind for that blank figure. Because people tend to identify with the frustrated figure and project their own habitual style, the replies are scored for the direction in which aggression is turned (outward, inward, or turned aside) and the type of reaction (dwelling on the obstacle, defending the ego, or persisting toward a solution). The manual describes it as a controlled projective technique that "stands midway in design between the word-association and the thematic apperception techniques" — it keeps the objectivity of word-association while reaching some of the personality material the TAT probes.

The 5-minute version
  • Developer: Saul Rosenzweig; first paper introducing the technique, 1945. Grew out of his theory of frustration (1934, 1938, 1944).
  • Full name lineage: "Picture-Association Study for Assessing Reactions to Frustration" → shortened to "Picture-Frustration Study" → popularly the P-F Study.
  • Nature: semi-projective / "controlled projective." Objective enough to score reliably, projective enough to reveal habitual style.
  • What it captures: not a diagnosis — a sample of a person's habitual mode of reacting to frustration.
  • Forms: Children's, Adolescent (covered by the Adult Form for ages 14+), Adult. This guide is the Adult Form, Indian adaptation.

2. History & development (kept brief)

The P-F Study emerged from Rosenzweig's laboratory studies of frustration and repression. An earlier, simpler version (1945) had a first section that was developed into the full "Picture-Association Method." The Adult Form was introduced first and later revised (1948); the Children's Form followed. Adaptations exist in French, German, Italian and Japanese. The Indian adaptation described here was published by Rupa Psychological Corporation (Varanasi, 1968); a notable change in this edition was a more consistent terminology for the types and directions of aggression (detailed in Part VI).

3. Theoretical foundation — step by step

1
Frustration arises when an obstacle blocks a need or goal. The 24 pictures each stage a common, mild frustration.
2
Frustration provokes aggression — but in the manual's usage "aggression" is the broad genus (enterprise/assertion), of which hostility is only one negative species.
3
Projection / identification: the subject is assumed to identify with the frustrated figure and to project his own bias into the reply.
4
Direction of aggression — is it turned outward (Extraggression, E-A), inward on the self (Intropression, I-A), or turned aside / glossed over (Impression, M-A)?
5
Type of aggression — does the reply dwell on the obstacle (Obstacle-Dominance, O-D), defend the ego (Ego-Defence, E-D), or persist toward a solution (Need-Persistence, N-P)?
6
Crossing 3 directions × 3 types yields the nine scoring factors (plus two variants), which are the whole quantitative engine of the test.

4. What it does — and does not — establish

Can contribute to understanding

  • Habitual direction of aggression under frustration
  • Reaction type (obstacle-focus vs ego-defence vs problem-solving)
  • Defensiveness, self-blame vs external attribution
  • Persistence / conformity / avoidance
  • Conformity to the group's modal responses (GCR)
  • Shifts across the protocol (Trends)

Cannot establish on its own

  • A psychiatric diagnosis
  • That a person is or will become violent
  • Risk of suicide or harm
  • Fixed personality "labels"
  • Deep unconscious content (the manual explicitly discourages "deep" interpretation)
Important

The P-F Study is one component of assessment. The manual is explicit that interpretation should rest on the explicit meaning of the words and the quantitative profile, not on speculative depth analysis.

Part II — When to Use It (and When Not)

5. Clinical & applied uses (from the manual)

The Adult Form is meant for adults of both sexes and can be used with adolescents; it is designed for subjects aged 14 and over. The manual lists these applications:

Clinical

Used as a probe for diagnosis and with special groups — e.g. delinquents, stutterers, sexual offenders, suicidal cases, patients, the aged, the physically handicapped, and the mentally retarded. Always alongside interview and other data.

Cross-cultural

Directions/types of aggression are available for several nationalities (Indian, Japanese, French, Italian, German), enabling national, cultural and religious comparisons.

Industrial / applied

Applied for personnel selection, job adjustment and related industrial purposes.

Research

Widely used in systematic research on projective technique and human motivation. The Adult and Children's forms are roughly comparable, with 16 parallel items, so they can be used together for special clinical or research purposes.

6. When it should not be your primary tool

Common error to avoid

Do not lean on the P-F Study where comprehension or the projective set breaks down or where a decision cannot wait. It presupposes that the subject can read the situation, write a first reply, and respond to all 24 items in sequence.

  • Acute psychiatric emergencies or gross disorganisation.
  • Severe cognitive impairment, severe language/comprehension problems (the subject cannot grasp the pictured situation).
  • Any situation needing immediate risk assessment.

P-F findings never replace a suicide/violence risk assessment, the mental status examination, the diagnostic interview, or a full psychological assessment.

Part IV — Test Structure & Ego / Superego Situations

Test material

  • 24 cartoon-like drawings, each with two figures in a mildly frustrating everyday situation.
  • The left figure speaks words that create or describe the frustration; the right figure has a blank speech balloon.
  • Facial features and other personality cues are deliberately omitted — this keeps the task ambiguous and encourages projection.
  • The subject writes the first reply the blank figure might give.

9. Ego-blocking vs. superego-blocking situations

Ego-blocking

Some obstacle — personal or impersonal — directly interrupts, disappoints, deprives, or frustrates the subject.

16 situations: 1, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 18, 20, 22, 23, 24.

Superego-blocking

Someone accuses, charges or incriminates the subject — the frustration is a reaction to something the subject himself has (or is said to have) done.

8 situations: 2, 5, 7, 10, 16, 17, 19, 21.

Clinical pearl

In superego situations there is usually an implied prior ego-blocking — the present accuser was, in effect, first frustrated by the subject. The ego/superego distinction is not absolute: a subject may read a superego situation as ego-blocking or vice-versa. When that happens, score according to the subject's own bent, not the situation's official type. This is exactly why superego items get special scoring rules (Part VI).

Part V — Administration

10. Before you begin

  • Materials: the standard P-F Study Booklet. The subject should write on the standard booklet — substitute answer sheets can change results.
  • Setting & rapport: a quiet setting; enough rapport that the subject will write freely and quickly.
  • Mode: individual or group; the whole test takes about 20 minutes. For clinical work, individual administration is preferred because it allows the Inquiry, which makes scoring and interpretation much more reliable.
  • Even in individual administration the subject writes his own responses.

11. Standard administration, step by step

1
Distribute / hand over the booklet and read the instructions from the cover page while the subject follows along in his own copy.
2
Read the standard instructions (below), orienting the subject toward a quick, objective, first-association reply.
3
Demonstrate with the first picture — show how a reply is arrived at, then ask the subject to open the booklet, look at situation 1, think of the first reply the blank figure might give, and write it.
4
Insist on sequence & completeness: make clear orally that items are answered in order (the trend analysis depends on order) and that no situation is left blank.
5
Signal on finishing: ask the subject to raise a hand (or signal) when done, so total time can be recorded and revision of first replies is discouraged; the tester then collects the booklet.
6
Record total time on the cover page.
7
Read-back & Inquiry (individual): after completion, have the subject read his written replies aloud; note voice inflections; ask non-leading questions to clarify brief or ambiguous replies.

12. The standardised instructions (paraphrased)

In each picture two people are shown talking. The words of one person are given. Imagine what the other person would answer, and write in the blank box the very first reply that comes into your mind. Work as fast as you can.

Purpose of the wording: to move the subject away from a self-critical attitude toward an "objective" identification with the anonymous figures, so that the first association — with less censorship — is captured. Do not invent or alter the wording.

13. Handling difficulties during administration

Questions / clarification

Permissible clarification is limited to what keeps the standard set; the examiner must not suggest content.

Erased / changed replies

If a reply was erased or crossed out and another substituted, score the first reply if it can still be deciphered.

Too brief / ambiguous

Use the Inquiry to amplify enough for scoring — and to detect emotion in the voice not evident in the words.

Misunderstood situation

If a situation is descriptively misunderstood it cannot be scored normally; the Inquiry reveals the misunderstanding and elicits a reply to the intended meaning (though the misreading itself can be qualitatively revealing).

Group administration

The Inquiry is largely sacrificed; occasionally a reply must be marked unscorable for ambiguity or clear misreading.

What the examiner must NOT do

Do not lead or prompt content, do not change the instructions, do not let the subject revise first replies, do not leave items unanswered.

Why verbatim recording matters

Because scoring rests on the explicit words, and because qualitative analysis draws on vocabulary, tone and emotional intensity, responses are recorded verbatim — with spontaneous comments, pauses, refusals, and emotional reactions noted. The written booklet is the record; the Inquiry supplements it.

Part VI — The Scoring System

Every reply is scored for direction of aggression (E-A / I-A / M-A) and type of aggression (O-D / E-D / N-P). Crossing them gives the nine factors (plus two denial variants).

14. The two dimensions

Direction of aggression
  • Extraggression (E-A): aggression turned outward onto the environment.
  • Intropression (I-A): aggression turned inward, on the self.
  • Impression (M-A): aggression evaded / glossed over to minimise the frustration.
Type of aggression
  • Obstacle-Dominance (O-D): the barrier itself stands out in the reply.
  • Ego-Defence (E-D): the ego of the subject predominates (blame/guilt/denial).
  • Need-Persistence (N-P): the reply emphasises solving the problem.
Terminology (this edition)

This edition adopts a consistent terminology in which O-D, E-D and N-P are all treated as forms of aggression (the broad "enterprise/assertion" sense). Constructive aggression = Need-Persistence; negative aggression = Ego-Defence; Obstacle-Dominance is a curtailed reaction where the response is inhibited before either of the other modes takes over. The three directions are called extraggressive, introgressive, impressive; the older words extrapunitive, intropunitive, impunitive are now reserved for the Ego-Defensive factors specifically.

15. Notation you must be fluent in

The columnar convention
  • Written as O-D / E-D / N-P — Obstacle-dominance first, Ego-defence second, Need-persistence third.
  • O-D factors take an apostrophe: E', I', M'.
  • E-D factors are capitals: E, I, M.
  • N-P factors are lowercase: e, i, m.
  • Examples: E'/I = extrapeditive O-D + intropunitive E-D; I/e = intropunitive E-D + extrapersistive N-P.
  • Two factors within one column are separated by a semicolon: /E;M/.
  • Intrinsic combination scores — where a thinly-veiled underlying (usually compensatory) trend affects scoring — are boxed in a rectangle to show they are a single unit, e.g. [/E;M/] (outward aggression built on self-doubt) or [/M'E/] ("sour grapes": reducing frustration by belittling the goal).

16. The nine factors + two variants — definitions

The 11 scoring factors, as defined in the manual, with short recognition cues and original examples.

17. Extraggressive scores — E' vs E vs e

E' extrapeditive

Stresses the severity of the frustration or shows irritation — not blame, guilt or solution.

E extrapunitive

Aggression toward a person is evident. Note the split: protest against an inanimate object or fate ("Damn it!", "We are unlucky") → E'; aggression aimed at a person ("Damn you!", "You are very careless") → E.

e extrapersistive

Demands action from the other person, or insists the problem be solved the way the speaker wants; also plain constructive suggestions (e.g. "Please ask someone else").

Introgressive scores — I' vs I vs i

I' intropeditive

Denies any frustration exists (often a refusal to let others know of one's concern); or a martyr-like claim that the frustration is really an advantage. A blended reply with strong overt guilt is scored I, not I'.

I intropunitive

Remorse, guilt, self-blame. When conventional "sorry" accompanies an offer to amend, i alone may suffice; if the guilt is more than conventional, use both I and i.

i intropersistive

Emphasises the speaker's own activity to solve the problem / make amends.

Impressive scores — M' vs M vs m

M' impeditive

Minimises the frustration; conveys no embarrassment and that the situation is unimportant.

M impunitive

"Explains" or excuses the situation, absolving the frustrater.

m impersistive

Acceptance and conformity. If conformity is direct and unqualified → m alone. If it carries a request for permission/satisfaction-now with a promise to comply later → e + m. If grudging, with discomfort noted → E'//m.

Remember

Introductory softeners like "All right", "O.K.", "I don't care" that merely preface a stronger statement are not enough on their own to add M; they are usually casual and are omitted from the final score (though they would score M if standing alone). Situations 1, 6, 13 and 24 most often elicit these.

18. Special rules for superego situations

Superego scoring — memorise these
  • A reply that would be E' in an ego situation is scored I' here, because the consternation is a reaction to what the subject himself did (e.g. Sit. 2: "Oh, how awful" / "I wish I hadn't done it" → I').
  • Belittling the damage one caused is impertinent and scores E, not M' (Sit. 2: "Don't worry, throw these pieces out" → E).
  • Excuse for oneself → I (Sit. 10: "I just wasn't careful about what I was saying"). Excuse that shifts to the other / claims misunderstanding → M (Sit. 10: "You have misunderstood me").

Choosing between O-D, E-D and N-P in one reply

The developmental rule

Within a reply a subject may first register the block (O-D), then attack self/other (E-D), then move to a solution (N-P). When a single blended reply seems to involve two, favour the later factor developmentally — e.g. Obstacle-dominant elements are usually absorbed by Ego-defensive ones (so between E' and E, or M' and M, the E-D score wins if Ego-defence is clearly present). Two distinct statements, however, get two scores.

19. Number of factors & the Inquiry

  • Most replies need one factor. Two distinct phrases/sentences are usually needed before a second factor is scored.
  • A third factor is rare; treat as a two-factor combination, scoring the predominant factors.
  • When in doubt about how many factors, prefer the simpler scoring.
  • A minor element that merely introduces a clearly stronger statement is absorbed by the major emphasis.
  • Intrinsic combination scores (boxed) are reserved for thinly-veiled, essentially manifest underlying trends — the manual otherwise discourages deep interpretation; score the explicit meaning.

Part VIII — The Score Sheet & Calculations

Once every item is scored, the codes are transferred to the Score Sheet (Section VI of the manual), which is the main basis for interpretation. The Score Sheet has five working parts.

#Part of the Score SheetWhat it holds
1Item Scores (1–24) + GCR marksEach item's code entered in its O-D / E-D / N-P column; GCR agreement marked in the margin.
2Profile & Deviation PatternFrequencies & percentages of the six categories (E-A, I-A, M-A × O-D, E-D, N-P), split by first/second half.
3Super-Ego (S-E) PatternsDefensiveness indices from the superego factors.
4TrendsThe five within/cross trends across the two halves.
5Total Pattern & GCRThe three most frequent factors ordered; the GCR percentage.

20. Frequencies & percentages

The rules that trip people up
  • A split score (e.g. /E;e) contributes .5 to each factor — each factor gets equal weight.
  • Profile percentages use a denominator of 24 (each item = one unit). If items are omitted/unscorable, lower the denominator accordingly.
  • GCR percentages use a denominator of 12 — and here unscorable/unanswered items count as failures to meet the criterion (the base stays 12).
  • Appendix A (Percentage Calculation Table) converts counts to percentages for both the profile and the GCR.

Entry method: each category square (e.g. the O-D × E-A cell) is divided into four cells — first-half totals (items 1–12) go top-left; second-half totals (13–24) go bottom-right. Row and column totals give the six category totals; those become the profile percentages.

Worked fragment — entering E' (from the manual's sample protocol)

E' occurs in the first half as a full score 4 times (Sits. 3, 8, 11, 12) and once as a half score (Sit. 6) → 4.5 in the top-left cell. In the second half it occurs once full (Sit. 14) and three times as half scores (18, 20, 23) → 1 + 1½ = 2.5 in the bottom-right cell.

21. Super-Ego (S-E) Patterns

These gauge defensiveness — denying the act (Ē, the denial variant of E) or repudiating the blameworthy motive (Ī). The superego deviants are considered against factors E and I and the M-A category, because M-A and I both involve absolution from blame (excusing someone else, or excusing oneself). The sheet records: E, I, E+I, Ē−E, Ī−I, and M-A+I, each as a count and a percentage.

24. The Total Pattern

Take the three factors occurring most often in the record, regardless of type or direction, and write them in order of frequency joined by > / < / = symbols. In the manual's sample, E' occurs 7 times (highest), then I (4.5), then I' and E tie at 3 — giving the total pattern E' > I > I' = E.

Part IX — GCR (Group Conformity Rating)

Definition

GCR is a measure of how far an individual's scores conform to the modal (most typical) responses of the normative group. It is one index of the subject's adjustment to a normal group.

How the criterion (modal) responses are set

1
For each item, find the most frequent scored response in the standardisation sample.
2
Test whether it exceeds the next most frequent by a difference that is statistically significant — a critical ratio ≥ 3.0 (using the standard error of the difference between percentages).
3
If the CR is below 3.0, add the second most frequent response as an alternative criterion; test the 2nd vs 3rd the same way, adding a third only if needed. Never more than three modal responses; two are rarely exceeded.
4
In the Indian data, a 40% plurality held exactly for 11 items; item 9 was added (E' 38% / E 34%, plus American precedent), giving 12 composite GCR criterion items — matching the American version's count. (Items 14 & 15, at 39%, were deliberately omitted.)

Scoring a subject's GCR

  • Compare the subject's score on each of the 12 criterion items to the criterion response(s).
  • Full agreement → "+" (counts 1). Where a combination score only partly matches, part credit "½" (counts 0.5).
  • Where several criterion responses are listed, any one — or any combination — counts as full agreement.
  • Sum the credits and convert to a percentage of 12 (unscorable items count as failures; base stays 12). Use Appendix A.
Worked GCR — the manual's sample protocol
Full agreement on 9 items (1, 2, 3, 9, 10, 16, 19, 21, 24)+half-agreement on item 5 (½)
Total credit = 9 + 0.5 = 9.5→9.5 ÷ 12→GCR = 79%
Interpretive caution

GCR is "one measure of the individual's adjustment to a normal group." A high GCR is not proof of a healthy personality, nor is a low GCR proof of maladjustment — interpret it against the whole profile and the case context.

Part X — Trends

A trend is a consistent shift across the protocol — e.g. beginning extrapunitive and, after guilt accumulates, becoming intropunitive. Because the subject answers in sequence, trends are computed by comparing the first half (items 1–12) with the second half (items 13–24).

23. The five trends

TrendConcerned withKind
1E', I', M' (the O-D column)Within-column
2E, I, M (the E-D column)Within-column
3e, i, m (the N-P column)Within-column
4E-A, I-A, M-A (directions)Cross-trend
5O-D, E-D, N-P (types)Cross-trend

There are 15 possible trends in all, each capable of being positive or negative.

The formula

Trend value

Trend = (a − b) / (a + b)

a = amount of the factor/category in the first half (items 1–12); b = amount in the second half (items 13–24). A positive value means a shift away from that factor (higher early, lower late); a negative value means a shift towards it. Appendix B is a lookup table giving trend values of .20 and above (enter the higher of a/b in the first column, the lower in the first row).

Two requirements for a trend to count

  1. Base of at least 4 items — a + b (for categories) must be ≥ 4.
  2. Minimum value — the trend must reach the significance value in Table 2 (below), worked out for the Indian population (Pareek & Kumar, 1965).

Clinical pearl

A protocol with many trends may indicate inadequate adjustment and instability — the person keeps changing how he reacts to his own reactions. But read this against the whole picture, not in isolation.

Part XII — Interpretation

25. General principles

The "level" question

The core interpretive question is the behavioural level a protocol reflects. The manual sets out three possibilities: (1) a self-critical, censored picture; (2) what the person would actually say in the situation; (3) a more covert/latent orientation. For general purposes the second level — a sample of the person's gross, observable behaviour — is assumed. Use the end-of-test Inquiry ("Of whom were you thinking?"; "Is this what you'd really say?") to gauge the level, but treat those answers as themselves partly rationalised.

Do not over-read

The manual discourages deep/content analysis: the quantitative figures (factors, GCR, trends, pattern) already give a fairly dynamic picture, and the test is presumed to tap the second (behavioural) level. Interpret from the explicit words + the Score Sheet, always against the norms — never diagnose from a single score.

The manual's anchor interpretations

Total E-D

May be taken to represent strength or weakness of the ego.

High N-P

May show adaptive adequacy (a problem-solving orientation).

GCR

"One measure of the individual's adjustment to a normal group."

Many trends

May indicate inadequate adjustment and instability.

High O-D

Tends to be obsessed by the barrier; the manual's worked case links very high O-D to "anxiety" and embarrassment.

S-E patterns

Gauge defensiveness — denying the act or repudiating the motive.

26–29. Reading directions, types, frustration & aggression — cautiously

Aggression — the critical caution

The P-F Study measures the direction and type of aggressive response to frustration. It does not establish that a person is violent or will become violent. Extrapunitiveness is about where blame is directed in words, not about dangerousness.

Qualitative analysis (permitted, second)

A fuller clinical reading may consider vocabulary choice, tone/emotional intensity, whether the frustrater is adult or child, humour, sarcasm, passivity, persistence and recurrent themes — where these can be confirmed against the case history or other test results. State plainly what can and cannot be inferred.

Part XIV — Mini Cases (Profile Patterns)

Compact illustrations of how a leading pattern reads. All are fictional teaching examples; scores are read against the Indian composite norms (Part XI). Each notes the alternative explanation and what to check next — because no single pattern is a diagnosis.

EPredominantly Extraggressive

E-A well above ~47%. Reads as an outward, blame-externalising style under frustration. Alt: situational/cultural; not violence. Check: interview, behaviour across settings.

IPredominantly Introgressive

I-A above ~28%, with raised I. Self-blame/guilt style; strong super-ego. Alt: conscientiousness, culture — not "depression" per se. Check: mood assessment.

MPredominantly Impressive

M-A above ~24%. Minimising/glossing-over style. Alt: could be genuine tolerance or avoidance. Check: whether problems get solved in real life.

Mixed / balanced

No category far from its norm. Flexible responding — interpret from trends, GCR, qualitative tone.

High O-D

O-D ≫ ~26%, often via E'/I'. Preoccupation with the barrier; possible anxiety/embarrassment. Check: current stressors.

High E-D

E-D above ~51%. Ego-defensive emphasis. Caution: only meaningful as deviation in S.D. units.

High N-P

N-P above ~23%. Adaptive, problem-solving orientation. Corroborate with history.

High GCR

Conforms to modal group responses → superficial adjustment to the group. Not proof of health.

Low GCR

Diverges from modal responses. Not proof of maladjustment; read with the profile and context.

Significant trend

e.g. away-from E-A, toward I-A across the halves → a consistent shift of style during the test; many trends → possible instability.

Part XVI — Language Bank & Common Errors

Phrase bank — findings

  • "The profile shows a relative predominance of extraggressive responses…"
  • "Ego-defensive responses fall within one S.D. of the composite norm…"
  • "A significant trend away from E-A and toward I-A was observed across the protocol…"
  • "The GCR is consistent with adequate conformity to group modal responses…"

Phrase bank — interpretation/limits

  • "These findings are suggestive of… and should be interpreted alongside the clinical interview."
  • "As a semi-projective measure, the P-F Study samples habitual verbal reactions, not diagnoses."
  • "Norms are Indian adult norms; caution applies for other groups."
AvoidPreferWhy
"The patient is aggressive.""The profile shows a relative predominance of extraggressive responses, suggesting a tendency to direct blame outward under frustration."Describes the response tendency, not a trait/dangerousness claim.
"High I means depression.""Elevated intropunitive responses suggest a self-blaming style; mood should be assessed separately."Avoids equating a P-F factor with a diagnosis.
"High GCR = normal.""High GCR suggests conformity to group modal responses."GCR is adjustment-to-group, not normality.
Error checklist

Administration: leading/prompting, changing instructions, allowing revision, breaking sequence. Scoring: wrong direction/type, mis-handling superego rules, wrong split-score weighting, wrong denominator (24 vs 12), GCR/trend errors. Interpretation: diagnosing from one score, equating E with violence / I with depression / M with adjustment / GCR with normality, ignoring culture and current circumstances.

Part XVII — Psychometrics & Cultural Notes

Reliability (this adaptation)

Scoring (inter-scorer) reliability was reported high (two independent scorers). Stability coefficients ranged .27–.82 (Pareek & Devi, 1965). Consistency values ~46–74. Do not overstate: some coefficients are modest.

Validity / utility

The manual and its bibliography cite wide clinical, cross-cultural and research use; the guide keeps validity claims modest, in line with the source.

Cultural considerations

Nine situations were substantially re-worked for Indian culture, plus dress/setting changes throughout — evidence that Western norms cannot be assumed to transfer. Use the Indian composite norms for Indian adults; consider gender, social expectations and the culturally-shaped expression of aggression.

Semi-projective method

Findings presume the "second (behavioural) level"; corroboration from interview/other tests is expected, not optional.

Part XIX — Quick Revision

P-F Study in 5 minutes

24 cartoon frustrations; write the frustrated figure's first reply. Score direction (E-A/I-A/M-A) × type (O-D/E-D/N-P) → 9 factors (+2 denial variants). O-D = apostrophe, E-D = capitals, N-P = lowercase. Compute profile %s (÷24), GCR (÷12, 12 criteria), S-E patterns, trends ((a−b)/(a+b)), and the total pattern. Interpret against Indian adult norms; never diagnose from one score.

P-F Study in 10 minutes
  • 16 ego-blocking, 8 superego-blocking situations; superego items have special rules (E'→I'; belittling damage→E; self-excuse→I, misunderstanding→M).
  • Choice rule: favour the later factor; E-D absorbs O-D when clear.
  • Norms: average protocol is E-D dominant (~51%), E-A leading direction (~47%).
  • GCR high = adjusted to group; low = divergent (neither = diagnosis).
  • Trend + = away-from, − = towards; needs base ≥4 items and Table-2 value.
One-page master summary

Purpose: habitual reactions to frustration. Dimensions: direction × type. Factors: E' I' M' / E I M / e i m. Indices: profile %, GCR, S-E patterns, 5 trends, total pattern. Anchors: E-D≈ego strength/weakness; N-P high≈adaptive; GCR≈group adjustment; many trends≈instability. Golden rule: raw response → scoring → quantitative → qualitative → interpretation → integration → report; never score → diagnosis.
