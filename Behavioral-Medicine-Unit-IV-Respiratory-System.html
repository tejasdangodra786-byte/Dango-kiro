One golden rule for this whole unit

Everything in Topic 5 — relaxation, breathing retraining, CBT, biofeedback — is an adjunct (an added helper that works alongside) to proper medical treatment. These methods calm the fear, the over-breathing and the unhelpful habits that sit on top of airway disease; they do not replace inhalers, medicines or a doctor's care. Asthma is a real, sometimes life-threatening physical disease — never "all in the head".

1 Overview — the respiratory system & the mind–body link

The job of breathing is simple to state: take in oxygen and push out carbon dioxide. But breathing is also unusually close to the mind — it speeds up when we are afraid, catches when we cry, and freezes when we are shocked. That is exactly why airway disease, and asthma above all, became a classic example of a psychosomatic (mind-and-body-together) condition: the body's faulty airways and the person's emotions constantly feed into each other. This topic lays the groundwork — how breathing works, what each airway disease actually is, and why we study it with a biopsychosocial lens.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Open with the mind–body hook — one line that breathing and emotion are tightly linked, so airway disease is a classic psychosomatic condition.
  2. Describe how breathing works — walk the air from nose to alveoli, name the key parts, and say how the brain's respiratory centre controls it using carbon-dioxide levels.
  3. Define the airway diseases one by one — asthma, COPD and its three parts (chronic bronchitis, emphysema), plus hyperventilation syndrome and vocal cord dysfunction — in plain words with each hard term glossed.
  4. Explain why it is "psychosomatic" — give the old psychoanalytic view, say honestly why it failed, and land on the modern biopsychosocial view: biology causes the disease, but psychology shapes its severity and course.
  5. Add the epidemiology and the Indian data to show scale and relevance.
  6. End with significance and a one-line summary — the mind does not cause the disease, but it powerfully colours how it is lived with.

The detailed notes below follow exactly this order, so you can read top-to-bottom and write the answer in the same sequence.

Detailed notes

1. How breathing works — the system in plain words

The respiratory system (the set of organs that bring in oxygen and get rid of carbon dioxide) is a series of organs plus the chest muscles that move air. Follow one breath:

  • Air enters through the nose, where it is warmed, filtered and moistened, then passes the pharynx (the throat). The epiglottis (a small flap) stops food going down the wrong way.
  • It passes the larynx (the voice box, which holds the vocal cords) and down the trachea (the windpipe).
  • The trachea splits into two bronchi (the two main breathing tubes, one to each lung), which branch again and again into ever-smaller bronchioles (tiny breathing tubes). These tubes are lined with mucus and tiny hairs that trap dust.
  • The smallest tubes end in grape-like clusters of alveoli (microscopic air sacs), wrapped in capillaries (the thinnest blood vessels). Here oxygen crosses into the blood and carbon dioxide crosses out — this is the whole point of breathing.
  • The diaphragm (a dome-shaped muscle under the lungs) is the pump: it flattens to pull air in, and relaxes up to push air out.

Who tells us to breathe? A respiratory centre (a breathing-control area) in the medulla (the lowest part of the brainstem) sets the pace, and it mostly watches the blood's carbon dioxide (CO₂) level. If CO₂ rises too high, the centre speeds breathing up to blow it off; if CO₂ falls too low, it slows breathing down until the level returns to normal. Keep this CO₂ thermostat in mind — it is the key to understanding hyperventilation in Topic 3. The system also protects itself by coughing out trapped mucus and dust.

2. Asthma and the other airway diseases — defined one by one

Airway diseases can be set off by breathing in cigarette smoke, chemicals, allergens and other irritants, and genes also decide who is vulnerable. Here are the ones this unit needs, each in plain language.

Asthma — the central disease of this unit. Asthma affects the small breathing tubes. Every person with asthma is sensitive to certain triggers. When a trigger hits, three things happen together inside the tubes:

  1. the lining swells up — inflammation (red, puffy, irritated tissue);
  2. the tubes fill with thick, sticky mucus (phlegm); and
  3. the muscle around the tubes tightens and squeezes the airway smaller — bronchospasm (sudden muscle clamp around the breathing tubes).

All three together make breathing hard — an asthma flare-up (attack). Symptoms are coughing, wheezing, chest tightness and dyspnoea. (Wheeze (a whistling sound when air squeezes through narrowed tubes); dyspnoea (the feeling of not being able to get enough breath; "air hunger").) Attacks can be touched off by allergens (dust, animal dander, pollens, fungi) and also by emotional stress or exercise. A key modern word for asthma's core fault is airway hyperresponsiveness (the airways over-react and clamp down to triggers that would barely bother a normal airway); another is atopy (an inherited tendency to allergic reactions), which involves the antibody IgE — immunoglobulin E (the allergy antibody that triggers the allergic response).

A vital safety idea: reversible vs irreversible

In asthma the narrowing is largely reversible — it opens up again with treatment or time. In COPD the obstruction is largely fixed and progressive. This single difference shapes everything about how each is managed, so examiners love it.

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) — spell it out: a long-lasting (chronic) disease that blocks airflow out of the lungs and gets steadily worse. The American Thoracic Society (1995) treated COPD as the meeting-point of three conditions:

  • Airflow obstruction (air cannot be pushed out fast) — the defining, progressive feature, caused by damaged small airways and some bronchoconstriction.
  • Emphysema (the walls between the air sacs are destroyed, so many small sacs merge into fewer large, floppy ones) — this permanent enlargement of airspaces means less surface for gas exchange and lungs that cannot empty properly.
  • Chronic bronchitis (long-standing inflammation of the bronchi with a wet cough) — clinically, a productive (phlegmy) cough for ≥3 months in each of two years in a row, once other causes are ruled out.

Over time COPD patients show a rapid fall in lung function, worsening cough and dyspnoea, more chest infections, and later hypoxemia (too little oxygen in the blood). Smoking is the main cause.

Hyperventilation syndrome — hyperventilation (breathing faster and deeper than the body needs). During intense emotion people often over-breathe, which blows off too much CO₂, producing hypocapnia (low carbon dioxide in the blood). Because CO₂ normally widens blood vessels, low CO₂ narrows them and cuts blood flow to the brain — giving dizziness, blurred vision and trouble thinking clearly. This is a direct bridge to the panic material in Topic 3.

Vocal cord dysfunction (VCD) — a condition in which the vocal cords (the two bands in the voice box) close when they should open during breathing, causing sudden breathlessness and noisy breathing that mimics asthma but does not respond to asthma inhalers. It is often linked to stress and anxiety, and is a classic "looks-like-asthma-but-is-not" trap.

Supplement — the other respiratory disorders named in the teaching outline
  • Asphyxia (lack of oxygen with a build-up of CO₂) and anoxia (a shortage of oxygen alone) — usually short-lived, but dangerous if prolonged; in anoxia a person may grow confused, lose a sense of danger and slip toward a coma without breathing faster.
  • Hay fever — a seasonal allergic reaction to pollens and dust; the body releases histamines (allergy chemicals), the lung capillaries become inflamed and leak fluid, and violent sneezing results.
  • Viral infections — the common cold and the more serious influenza (which can occur in epidemics); bronchitis is inflammation of the lining inside the bronchi with heavy mucus and coughing.
  • Bacterial infections — strep throat, whooping cough and diphtheria; their main danger is secondary infection from lowered resistance, and some can damage other tissues such as the heart.
  • Pneumonia — lobar (a whole lobe of lung infected) or bronchial (confined to the bronchi, often a complication of a cold or flu).
  • Tuberculosis — an infection where bacteria invade lung tissue and get walled off into clumps called tubercles (small nodules of immune cells around the bacteria).
  • Lung cancer — uncontrolled growth of lung cells into tumours, caused mainly by smoking and by carcinogens such as air pollution and asbestos.

3. Why airway disease is a classic psychosomatic condition

In both language and clinic, breathing and emotion are entangled. We say a story "inspires" us — and inspire (to breathe in) also means to fill with feeling. Mental distress shows up in the breath as the fast breathing of anxiety or the sighing of low mood; and disturbed breathing wrecks any sense of calm, as in the terror of a severe asthma attack. So the two run in a loop.

  • The old psychoanalytic view. A famous 1941 review by French and Alexander cast asthma as truly psychosomatic, suggesting the asthmatic attack was a suppressed cry for the mother (a held-back cry of separation distress) rooted in separation anxiety, and even citing hypnosis preventing attacks. It was hugely influential.
  • Why that view failed — and why this matters. Later research could not confirm it. There is no convincing evidence that the biological fault behind airway hypersensitivity is caused by psychological factors. Clinging to the old view did real harm: it fed the cruel, false idea that asthma is "all in the head", which shames patients and can lead to dangerous under-treatment. State this clearly in any answer.
  • The modern biopsychosocial view. Today the field holds that biology and genes cause the disease, while psychological and social factors shape its severity, its course and how it is managed. In other words, the mind does not start asthma, but it can strongly influence how bad an episode feels and how often trouble happens — and it drives whether a patient takes their medicine and avoids triggers. Two psychological models (the dyspnoea–suffocation-fear theory and the cognitive theory, both in Topic 3) sit inside this modern view to explain severity and frequency.

4. How common is it? — epidemiology

  • Prevalence (how many people have the disease at a given time) of both COPD and asthma keeps climbing. The American Thoracic Society (1995) estimated 14 million people in the USA had COPD — a 42% rise since 1982 — and noted the rise was faster in women than men.
  • Childhood (paediatric (relating to children)) asthma was estimated at about 7% in the USA and rising faster than in the population as a whole.
  • Indian data. The Indian Council of Medical Research set up a National Asthma Task Force; its multi-centre study (Chandigarh as coordinating centre, with Delhi, Bangalore and Kanpur) found a mean asthma prevalence of 2.38% among 73,605 people over 15 years of age, with one or more respiratory symptoms in 4.3–10.5%. Higher odds of asthma went with female sex, older age, urban residence, lower socio-economic status, a history of atopy, asthma in a first-degree relative, and tobacco smoking in any form.

5. Significance — why this unit matters

Because airway disease is chronic, frightening and partly trigger-driven, the behavioural scientist has a genuine role beside the physician: not to cure the lungs, but to reduce the fear, correct the over-breathing, untangle unhelpful habits and family patterns, and boost self-care. The whole unit is the story of how emotion and thought ride on top of a physical disease — and what psychology can honestly do about it.

Overview at a glance
  • Breathing = oxygen in, CO₂ out; paced by a brainstem centre that watches blood CO₂, pumped by the diaphragm.
  • Asthma = reversible airway narrowing from inflammation + mucus + bronchospasm, driven by airway hyperresponsiveness and often atopy.
  • COPD = fixed, progressive airflow block = chronic bronchitis + emphysema; mainly from smoking.
  • Hyperventilation blows off CO₂ → hypocapnia → dizziness; VCD mimics asthma.
  • Biopsychosocial rule: biology causes the disease; psychology and society shape its severity, course and management. Asthma is not "all in the head".

2 Precipitants — emotional arousal & other external stimuli

A precipitant is a trigger — something that sets off or worsens an airway episode. The syllabus singles out emotional arousal first, so start there: strong feelings (fear, anger, excitement, crying, laughing) and stress can tip a vulnerable airway into trouble. But emotion sits alongside many external and physical triggers — allergens, exercise, cold air, irritants, infection — and the honest question is how a feeling in the mind becomes a squeeze in the lung. The answer runs through the autonomic nervous system (the automatic "background" nerves that run the body without our thinking).

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define "precipitant" in one line (a trigger that starts or worsens an attack) and say the syllabus wants emotional arousal plus external stimuli.
  2. Start with emotional arousal — list the strong emotions, and give the stress-and-attacks evidence (e.g. the Scottish children study).
  3. Explain the body pathway — how emotion reaches the airways through the autonomic nerves (the vagus/parasympathetic route and the sympathetic route), and the irritant-receptor reflex.
  4. List the external/physical triggers — allergens, exercise, cold air, irritants and smoke, infection — each in a line.
  5. Add the learning angle — suggestion, expectancy/placebo and classical conditioning, with their honest limits.
  6. End with the key caution — emotion does not cause asthma; it is one precipitant among many that can worsen episodes in some people, some of the time.

The detailed notes follow this exact order.

Detailed notes

1. Emotional arousal — the headline precipitant

A precipitant (a trigger; something that brings an attack on or makes it worse) is not the same as a cause. The underlying cause of asthma is biological; a precipitant is the match that lights an already-prepared fire. The syllabus names emotional arousal first because it is the most striking example of the mind touching the lung.

  • A common clinical observation — often confirmed by patients — is that attacks sometimes appear during or right after emotional stress. Emotional arousal (a stirred-up state of strong feeling, with the body revved up), especially anxiety, frequently accompanies asthma episodes.
  • The strong emotions that act as triggers include fear, anger, excitement, crying and laughing. Note that even "positive" arousal (excitement, a fit of laughter) can set off symptoms — it is the arousal and the forceful breathing that matter, not whether the feeling is pleasant.
  • Stress as a precipitant — the evidence. A strong prospective study (one that follows people forward in time, which is more trustworthy than looking back) of 90 Scottish children found that a severe acute stressor (such as the death or loss of a parent or grandparent) significantly predicted new asthma attacks, with a gap of about 4 weeks. When acute stress landed on top of ongoing chronic stress (long illness in the family, marital discord, bullying), it predicted attacks more strongly and faster — within about 2 weeks. So acute and chronic stress act together to raise the risk and speed the onset of attacks. [Sandberg et al., 2000]
Worked example — emotion as a precipitant

A 15-year-old with well-controlled asthma has a shouting argument with a parent. She cries hard, her breathing becomes forceful and ragged, her chest tightens and she wheezes. Emotion did not create her asthma — her airways were already hyperresponsive — but the anger, the crying and the gasping precipitated a flare in a lung primed to react. Treating her means both her inhaler (the disease) and calming the arousal (the precipitant).

2. The body pathway — how a feeling becomes a squeeze

For an answer to be strong, show the route from brain to bronchus. Psychological events can reach the lung through processes in the limbic system (the brain's emotion network), especially the hypothalamus (a small control hub that drives the automatic nerves and hormones), and from there through the autonomic nervous system down to the airway tissue. Three concrete routes:

  • The vagal / parasympathetic route. The vagus nerve (the main parasympathetic nerve, part of the "rest-and-digest" system) supplies the airway muscle; higher vagal tone (stronger parasympathetic drive) tends to tighten that muscle and narrow the tubes. This is why a parasympathetic-dominant state (seen in some depression) can nudge the airways toward constriction.
  • The sympathetic puzzle and the adrenergic defect. Here is a paradox worth knowing: emotional arousal fires the sympathetic nervous system (the "fight-or-flight" nerves), and the cornerstone asthma drugs are beta-adrenergic (acting on the same fight-or-flight receptors) bronchodilators (medicines that open the airways) — so why would arousal ever narrow the airways? One proposed answer (Mathe & Knapp) is that some asthmatics have a mild adrenergic defect (they make less of their own adrenaline under stress), shown by lower urinary adrenaline during induced stress, so the airway-opening signal is weaker than it should be.
  • The irritant-reflex (mechanical) route — probably the big one. Gold and colleagues showed that stimulating vagally-mediated epithelial irritant receptors (sensors in the airway lining wired to the vagus nerve) produces real reflex bronchospasm. This is now thought to explain much so-called "emotional" asthma: gasping in surprise, yelling in anger, and crying or laughing all force air across these sensors and can reflexively tighten the airways. In other words, a lot of "emotional" asthma is really the physical act of forceful breathing that emotion produces — only very indirectly "emotional".

3. Other external & physical triggers

Emotion is only one entry on a long trigger list. The main external and physical precipitants of airway symptoms are:

A good exam point: triggers stack. A child with a cold (infection) who then runs in cold air (exercise + cold) and gets scared when they start to wheeze (emotion) has three or four precipitants piling onto one vulnerable airway.

4. Suggestion, expectancy and conditioning — the learning angle

Some of the most compelling evidence that psychology can touch airway tone comes from learning and belief:

  • Suggestion / expectancy. In classic experiments (e.g. Luparello and colleagues), asthmatic people inhaled plain salt-water mist but were told it contained a substance they were allergic to — and some developed real bronchoconstriction (airway narrowing). When they then inhaled the same salt water but were told it was a reliever, the narrowing reversed. Belief alone moved the airways. (Honest caveat: these effects are real but modest, and show up only with very sensitive measuring instruments.)
  • Placebo effect. A placebo (a dummy treatment with no active drug) given before exercise has been shown to reduce exercise-induced bronchospasm — again, belief shaping the airway response.
  • Classical conditioning — a tempting but shaky idea. By theory, if the sight of a flower (a neutral cue) is repeatedly paired with the pollen that truly triggers an attack, the sight alone might eventually trigger wheezing (an old 1886 anecdote describes wheezing at an artificial rose under glass). But decades of careful lab work have not produced a convincing demonstration of "conditioned asthma". The honest verdict: conditioned bronchospasm is theoretically possible but unstable and hard to produce, because real life keeps "unpairing" the cue from the trigger (extinction trials).
The caution that must close this topic

Everything above is about worsening or setting off episodes, not about causing the disease. The safest, best-supported statement is that psychological precipitants matter for "some of the people, some of the time" — real but modest, varying from person to person and even occasion to occasion. Never write that emotion causes asthma.

Precipitants at a glance
  • Precipitant = trigger that starts/worsens an episode (not a cause).
  • Emotional arousal (fear, anger, excitement, crying, laughing) + stress — acute + chronic stress act together to predict attacks (Sandberg et al.).
  • Pathway: limbic brain/hypothalamus → autonomic nerves → (i) vagal tone tightens muscle, (ii) irritant reflex = reflex bronchospasm, (iii) gasping/crying = mechanical. Possible adrenergic defect explains the sympathetic paradox.
  • External/physical: allergens, exercise, cold air, irritants/smoke, infection — and they stack.
  • Learning: suggestion, expectancy and placebo move airways (modestly); conditioned asthma is theoretically possible but shaky.

3 Exacerbants — anxiety & panic symptoms

A precipitant starts a fire; an exacerbant (something that amplifies and keeps a problem going) pours petrol on it. The syllabus names anxiety and panic as the exacerbants, and they are the perfect example because airway disease and anxiety feed each other in a loop: breathlessness frightens the patient, fear makes them over-breathe, over-breathing worsens the breathlessness, and round it goes. This topic unpacks that two-way link, the hyperventilation that drives it, the vicious cycle it creates, two theories that explain it, and how depression quietly worsens the whole picture.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. State the bidirectional link — anxiety/panic and asthma each make the other worse; give the numbers (up to ~30% of asthmatics meet criteria for panic disorder/agoraphobia).
  2. Explain hyperventilation — what over-breathing does (blows off CO₂ → hypocapnia → dizziness, tingling, chest tightness) and how it can trigger a flare.
  3. Draw the vicious cycle — dyspnoea → anxiety → faster breathing/bronchoconstriction → more dyspnoea.
  4. Give the two theories — dyspnoea–suffocation-fear theory (Ley) and the cognitive theory (Clark), including symptom mislabelling and its real consequences.
  5. Cover depression and the high-risk patient — low self-esteem, poor adherence, parasympathetic drive, and the traits/family factors linked to severe or fatal episodes.
  6. End with the clinical bottom line — recognise and treat the anxiety/panic, because it is often mistaken for "just more asthma".

The detailed notes follow this exact order.

Detailed notes

1. The anxiety–asthma link runs both ways

An exacerbant (a factor that amplifies and maintains a problem) differs from a precipitant: it does not just light the fire, it keeps it burning hotter. Anxiety and panic are classic exacerbants of airway disease, and the relationship is bidirectional (each one worsens the other).

  • Coexisting anxiety or panic disorder probably worsens the course of asthma, and panic disorder and agoraphobia (fear of situations where escape feels hard or help unavailable) are more common in asthma patients than in the general population. As many as 30% of people with asthma meet criteria for panic disorder or agoraphobia.
  • The link also runs the other way: repeated experience of severe dyspnoea or near-suffocation gives little emotional "immunity" — instead it often traumatises patients and can help produce anxiety or panic disorder. Groups of panic-disorder patients are more likely than other anxious patients to have an earlier history of lung disease.
  • Why this is dangerous. Panic disorder is under-recognised in asthma patients, and its symptoms are easily mistaken — by patient and doctor alike — for "just another asthma flare". Patients with both asthma and panic have greater anticipatory anxiety (dread of the next attack before it happens) about dyspnoea, and the fear of dyspnoea can itself trigger attacks. Critically, very high anxiety predicts more hospital admissions and higher asthma-related mortality — so this is not a minor add-on.

2. Hyperventilation — the engine of the loop

Hyperventilation (breathing faster/deeper than the body needs) is a common symptom of anxiety and panic, and it is the mechanical link between a frightened mind and a worse airway.

  • When anxious, people take rapid, shallow "chest" breaths (thoracic breathing (breathing high in the chest rather than from the belly)) and may not even notice. This disturbs the oxygen and CO₂ balance.
  • Over-breathing blows off too much CO₂, causing hypocapnia (low blood carbon dioxide). Because CO₂ normally widens blood vessels, low CO₂ constricts them — reducing brain blood flow and producing dizziness, blurred vision, difficulty thinking, tingling (especially in the hands and around the mouth), and chest tightness. These scary sensations feel like a worsening attack, which cranks the anxiety higher.
  • Hyperventilation can also directly trigger a flare of pulmonary illness. One proposed mechanism is brainstem over-reactivity to low CO₂ (the breathing-control centre over-responding to the drop in carbon dioxide). Indeed, experimentally, breathing high-CO₂ air, lactate infusion, and voluntary hyperventilation can all induce panic attacks in people with panic disorder — voluntary hyperventilation being the most specific test and lactate the most sensitive.

3. The dyspnoea–anxiety–dyspnoea vicious cycle

Put the pieces together and you get the loop that defines this topic (Figure 3.1):

  1. Dyspnoea — the airway narrows and the patient feels air-hungry.
  2. Anxiety/panic — that feeling is frightening, and the patient catastrophises ("I'm suffocating").
  3. Faster, shallow breathing + muscle tension — fear drives hyperventilation and tightens the chest muscles.
  4. Worse airways + hypocapnia — over-breathing and tension worsen both the real narrowing and the scary dizziness/tingling, which feeds straight back into more dyspnoea and more fear.

The practical beauty of seeing it as a cycle is that you can break it at any point — and that is exactly what breathing retraining, relaxation and cognitive work in Topic 5 are designed to do.

4. Two theories that explain the fear

(a) Dyspnoea–suffocation-fear theory (Ley, 1989). Ley proposed this to explain panic attacks. The idea: the fear in a panic attack comes from severe dyspnoea induced by hyperventilation, and the attack takes off when the person feels they have little or no control over that dyspnoea. Dyspnoea is a very commonly reported sensation during panic.

  • The asthma twist. Research found that although people with asthma and people with panic disorder report similar levels of dyspnoea, the source of their panic differs: in asthma, panic symptoms are well explained by the dyspnoea itself; in pure panic disorder, they are not explained by dyspnoea level. So the suffocation-fear theory may be most useful for understanding dyspnoea's link to asthma rather than as a complete account of panic.

(b) Cognitive theory (Clark, 1986). The cognitive theory of panic says a panic attack grows from the catastrophic misinterpretation (reading harmless body sensations as a sign of imminent disaster) of benign bodily feelings.

  • Extended to asthma: a person who mislabels ordinary sensations as "an asthma attack" may have more frequent, more severe episodes. In studies of asthma inpatients, 19–27% consistently mislabelled relatively harmless sensations — fatigue, worry, irritability, anxiety — as asthma.
  • Real consequences, same lungs. The mislabellers were far more likely to be re-hospitalised at 6 months (40–84% of mislabellers vs 29–40% of non-mislabellers) — even though there was no measured difference in lung function between the groups. The likely mechanism: the anxiety produced by the catastrophic interpretation itself leads to increased bronchoconstriction. This is a superb, concrete illustration of thoughts shaping outcomes on top of unchanged biology. [Dirks & Schraa, 1983]

5. Depression and the "high-risk" asthma patient

Anxiety's quieter cousin, depression, also worsens the course of airway disease — and this is where the syllabus word low self-esteem first appears (explored fully in Topic 4).

  • Shame and low self-esteem — common in depression and in many chronically ill patients — are risk factors for a severe course. Patients who feel they are "worth little" tend to manage their asthma poorly, and medication adherence (taking medicines as prescribed) suffers.
  • The sleep disturbance and poor concentration of depression further harm self-care; sleep loss may even blunt the patient's proprioceptive capacity (the body's ability to sense its own internal state) to detect rising airway resistance, so warning signs are missed. In severe depression, unconscious suicidal wishes may show as self-neglect. There is also a hypothesised parasympathetic dominance in depression that could add to airway reactivity and constriction.
  • Traits and family factors in fatal/near-fatal asthma. There is no single "asthma-prone personality", but some patterns raise risk. Traits linked to greater medication use, longer hospital stays and worse outcomes include intense fear, emotional lability (rapidly swinging moods), sensitivity to rejection and lack of persistence in difficulty. High aggression, denial/disregard of symptoms, and psychotic signs are tied to poor or fatal outcomes. Families of severe asthmatics show higher rates of mood disorders, PTSD — post-traumatic stress disorder (lasting distress after a traumatic event), substance use and antisocial personality disorder; in adolescents, the normal push for independence can get tangled in battles over medication adherence.
Clinical bottom line

Anxiety and panic are treatable exacerbants that are routinely missed because they masquerade as "more asthma". Recognising and treating them (Topic 5) can break the vicious cycle and cut hospitalisations — but, again, always alongside proper medical control of the disease.

Exacerbants at a glance
  • Anxiety ⇄ asthma is a two-way street; up to 30% of asthmatics meet panic disorder/agoraphobia criteria; high anxiety predicts more admissions and higher mortality.
  • Hyperventilation → hypocapnia → dizziness, tingling, chest tightness; can trigger flares via brainstem over-reactivity to low CO₂.
  • Vicious cycle: dyspnoea → fear → over-breathing & tension → worse airways → more dyspnoea.
  • Dyspnoea–suffocation-fear theory (Ley) + cognitive theory (Clark); symptom mislabelling raises re-hospitalisation with no change in lung function.
  • Depression + low self-esteem → poor adherence, missed warning signs; traits & family psychopathology raise fatal-episode risk.

4 Effects — secondary gain, low self-esteem & the wider impact

Living with a chronic airway disease changes the person and the family, not just the lungs. The syllabus flags two effects by name — secondary gain and low self-esteem — but these sit inside a bigger picture of depression, avoidance, the sick role (the social role of "being a patient"), poor quality of life, adherence problems and stigma. This topic defines each effect in its own right, shows how it arises, and gives concrete examples, so you can write a full "psychosocial consequences" answer.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define secondary gain first (the syllabus names it): give the dictionary definition, the Freud link, and clear adult and child examples.
  2. Define low self-esteem and self-concept in airway disease: how chronic illness, restriction and dependence damage self-image, especially in children.
  3. Add the comorbid mood picture — depression and anxiety as effects (briefly, since Topic 3 covered the mechanism).
  4. Cover the impact on children, family and school — watching from the window, lost schooling, guilt, inconsistent parenting, the family revolving around the illness.
  5. Cover quality of life, avoidance, the sick role and adherence/stigma — the everyday costs.
  6. End with the clinical significance — these effects feed back and worsen the disease course, which is why Topic 5's methods target them.

The detailed notes follow this exact order.

Detailed notes

1. Secondary gain — defined and illustrated

Secondary gain (an indirect benefit a person gets from being ill) is the first named effect. Mosby's Medical Dictionary defines it as "an indirect benefit, usually obtained through an illness or debility."

  • Where the idea comes from. The concept dates to the 19th century and was re-popularised by Sigmund Freud, who described patients "clinging to their disease" as a way to hold on to the benefits the illness brings. (Contrast with primary gain (the inner relief of keeping a conflict out of awareness) — secondary gain is the outer, practical benefit.)
  • What the benefits look like. Secondary gain ranges from getting more attention or sympathy to escaping punishment or responsibilities. Adult examples include access to pleasurable drugs, getting out of unwanted chores, an excuse not to live up to career demands, or avoiding sex / having more children. Children gain too — extra attention from a parent, or staying home from school.
  • How behaviourists re-frame it. In learning terms, "secondary gain" is a biologic reaction operantly maintained by environmental contingencies — that is, symptom-related behaviour that is reinforced (rewarded, so it happens more) by sympathy, attention or by letting the person avoid something unpleasant. This re-framing is important because it points straight to the treatment: change what the environment rewards (operant methods, Topic 5).
Worked example — secondary gain in a child

A boy notices that when he coughs loudly, his busy parents stop and fuss over him, and he is excused from a disliked class. The cough is not faked — but its frequency is being shaped by the attention and the escape it earns. The behavioural plan is not to punish the cough but to stop reinforcing it (parents give calm, minimal attention to attention-seeking coughing; a time-out (briefly removing attention/rewards) may be used), while making sure genuine symptoms are always treated properly.

2. Low self-esteem and self-concept

Self-esteem (how much a person values and respects themselves) and self-concept (the overall picture one holds of oneself) are both commonly damaged by chronic airway disease — the second named effect.

  • Shame and low self-esteem are common symptoms of the depression that often accompanies chronic illness, and are risk factors for a severe course (as in Topic 3). The practical chain is clear: feeling "worth little" → neglecting self-care → poorer asthma control.
  • Poor self-concept in children. Children with early-onset asthma may grow up watching other children play from the living-room window. Poor self-concepts are common; academic and social development can suffer from time lost to illness and from restricted, specialised contacts with age-mates. The child may react with shame, embarrassment or extreme demandingness, and peers/adults may be over-indulgent or simply fail to understand.
  • Body/self-image in adults. Visible breathlessness, inhaler use in public, limits on sport, work and social life, and dependence on others can all erode an adult's sense of competence and worth.

3. Depression and anxiety as ongoing effects

Beyond being exacerbants (Topic 3), depression and anxiety are themselves consequences of living with a frightening, limiting disease. Importantly, the once-"obvious" personality differences between asthmatics and healthy people disappear when asthmatics are compared with people who have other chronic illnesses — strong evidence that these traits are a result of chronic illness itself, not a cause of asthma. Repeated brushes with status asthmaticus (a severe, prolonged asthma attack that does not respond quickly to usual treatment) can leave enduring fears of death, hospitals and treatment, and conditioned fear that fires at the first wheeze.

4. Impact on children, family and school

  • The family can come to revolve around the illness. Asthma may become the single focus of all family activity. Parents may feel responsible, guilty and helpless, and at other times angry and resentful — a mix that breeds inconsistent child-rearing.
  • Manipulation and the sick role. A child can learn to manipulate others with illness or use sickness to avoid unpleasant situations (secondary gain again). Because asthma is physically restricting, it can be hard for the child to discover their true capabilities, so maladaptive behaviour patterns develop on both sides.
  • Late-onset (adult) sufferers face a different blow: asthma disrupts an already-fixed lifestyle, forcing changes in activities and adjustment to a chronic disorder, with fear of symptoms and worry about medication side-effects.
  • Financial burden. For families of both young and old, the ongoing cost of fighting asthma is "almost always oppressive".

5. Quality of life, avoidance, the sick role and adherence

  • Activity restriction & avoidance. Fear of triggering an attack leads people to avoid exercise, outings, pets or emotional situations, narrowing life and lowering quality of life (QoL) (overall well-being and ability to do what matters to you).
  • The sick role. Chronic illness can pull a person into the sick role (the social position of a patient, excused from normal duties but expected to seek care); secondary gains can make this role "sticky" and hard to leave.
  • Adherence problems. Poor medication adherence remains a major barrier to proper treatment of both asthma and COPD — driven by low self-esteem, depression, denial, adolescent independence struggles, and fear of side-effects. (The negotiation-and-contracting approach to adherence appears in Topic 5.)
  • Stigma. The lingering, false belief that asthma is "all in the head" is "monstrously unfair" and can be psychologically damaging to patients and families, adding shame to an already heavy load.
Why these "effects" matter clinically

These are not just sad side-notes. As Figure 4.1's dashed arrow shows, the effects feed back: low self-esteem and depression worsen adherence and self-care, avoidance deconditions the body, and secondary gain reinforces symptom behaviour — all of which can worsen the disease course. That feedback loop is precisely what the adjunct strategies in Topic 5 aim to interrupt.

Effects at a glance
  • Secondary gain = indirect benefit of illness (attention, escape, avoiding duties); Freud's "clinging to disease"; behaviourally = symptom behaviour reinforced by the environment.
  • Low self-esteem / poor self-concept — shame, "watching from the window" in children, eroded body-image in adults → poorer self-care.
  • Depression & anxiety are effects too; asthmatic "personality" differences vanish vs other chronic illnesses → they are consequences, not causes.
  • Family/school: guilt, inconsistent parenting, illness as the family's focus, manipulation, lost schooling, financial strain.
  • QoL ↓, avoidance, sick role, poor adherence, stigma — and all of it feeds back to worsen the disease.

5 Psychological, behavioural & biofeedback strategies as adjunct management

Now the toolkit. The syllabus asks for psychological, behavioural and biofeedback strategies as adjunct in the management — and the single most important word is adjunct (an added helper that works alongside, never instead of). None of these methods fixes the diseased lung; what they do is calm the fear, correct the over-breathing, untangle unhelpful habits and family patterns, and build self-care, so the patient copes better and uses their medicine well. Below, each method is explained — what it is, how it works, a worked example, and the honest evidence stance.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Open with the "adjunct" rule and the two specialists (physician + behavioural therapist) and the three aims of asthma management.
  2. Psychoeducation first — teaching the patient about the disease and making them an ally of the physician.
  3. Relaxation & breathing retraining — PMR, autogenic training, diaphragmatic/belly breathing, and the named programmes (Buteyko, Papworth).
  4. CBT, stress management & cognitive restructuring — treating the panic and catastrophic beliefs from Topic 3; then the behavioural methods (systematic desensitisation, operant/contingency management, self-monitoring with peak-flow diaries).
  5. Biofeedback — explain the idea, then EMG, respiratory/capnometry, and HRV biofeedback, each with how it works and the evidence.
  6. Self-management & COPD pulmonary rehabilitation — Creer's self-management model and the structured COPD programme, plus family/adherence support.
  7. Close with the evidence verdict — these help the emotional and behavioural side reliably; direct effects on lung function are small; always an adjunct.

Detailed notes

0. The frame: two specialists, three aims

To deal well with the knock-on consequences of asthma, two specialists should be involved: the physician (for the disease) and the behavioural therapist (for the fear, habits and self-care). Management targets three distinct areas:

  1. modify the abnormal pulmonary function (medical job);
  2. change the untoward emotional disturbances (anxiety, panic, low mood);
  3. alter the maladaptive asthma-related behaviours, including inconsistent family patterns.

Historically the focus moved from Stage 1 — psychotherapy/psychoanalysis (1940s, French & Alexander), which faded as it failed to deliver, to Stage 2 — behavioural approaches (1960s–70s), which target treatable problems rather than hunting for hidden psychological causes of what is a physical disease. The five behavioural approaches that follow — relaxation, biofeedback, operant conditioning, systematic desensitisation and self-management — are the heart of Stage 2.

1. Psychoeducation

Psychoeducation (structured teaching about the illness and its management) is the foundation on which every other method rests. The aim is to teach patients skills so they become allies with their physician in managing their own condition.

  • Content covers what the disease is, what the triggers are, how the medicines work, correct inhaler technique, and how to recognise warning signs.
  • It directly counters the stigma and catastrophic beliefs of Topics 3–4: understanding that a sensation is a known, manageable symptom (not impending death) reduces the fear that feeds the vicious cycle.
  • In COPD rehabilitation, "general education" is explicitly the first content area — patients are taught the skills to partner with their doctors.

2. Relaxation training & breathing retraining

Why relaxation helps. When anxious, people chest-breathe fast and shallow, disturbing the O₂/CO₂ balance and raising heart rate, dizziness and muscle tension — a stress response that fuels anxiety and panic. Relaxation and breathing methods calm that response and so can help control asthma symptoms and break the loop.

(a) Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR). PMR (tensing then releasing muscle groups in turn to learn deep physical relaxation) was introduced by Jacobson (1938) and is the relaxation backbone of behaviour therapy. Early controlled work by Alexander and colleagues found that a brief course of modified progressive relaxation produced a statistically significant improvement in peak expiratory flow (about an 11% rise) compared with just sitting quietly — a real but modest effect.

(b) Autogenic training. Autogenic training (a relaxation method using self-suggestions of warmth and heaviness to calm the body) is a sister technique to PMR, useful for lowering arousal.

(c) Diaphragmatic / "belly" breathing. The core breathing-retraining skill. Instead of shallow chest breaths, the patient learns to breathe slowly from the diaphragm:

Worked example — belly-breathing steps taught to a patient
  1. Put one hand on the belly, the other on the chest.
  2. As you breathe in, push the belly out — the belly hand moves, the chest hand stays still.
  3. Close the eyes and push all the air out through almost-closed lips, as if blowing up a balloon.
  4. Take a slow, deep breath, filling the lungs and letting the belly rise.
  5. Repeat this deeper in-and-out breathing three times.

This slows the rate, raises CO₂ back toward normal (undoing hypocapnia), and tells the body it is safe.

(d) Named breathing programmes.

  • Buteyko method (a breathing programme that trains slower, shallower, nasal breathing to reduce over-breathing) — aims to correct chronic hyperventilation; trials suggest it can improve symptoms and reduce reliever use, though effects on underlying lung function are limited.
  • Papworth method (an integrated breathing-and-relaxation training, combining diaphragmatic breathing with relaxation and nose-breathing) — designed to retrain dysfunctional breathing and lower anxiety, improving symptoms and quality of life.
  • Pursed-lip breathing — a staple of COPD chest physiotherapy that slows exhalation and keeps airways open longer.
Honest evidence note on breathing/relaxation

These methods reliably help symptoms, anxiety, reliever use and quality of life, and relaxation can give small improvements in flow readings. They do not reverse the underlying airway disease. A genuine danger flagged in the literature: a patient relaxed into feeling "fine" might go home untreated while the physiology is still dangerous — so relaxation must never crowd out medical assessment.

3. CBT, stress management & behavioural methods

(a) Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT). CBT — cognitive behaviour therapy (a structured talking therapy that changes unhelpful thoughts and behaviours) directly targets the anxiety, panic and catastrophic misinterpretation from Topic 3.

  • Cognitive restructuring (spotting and challenging catastrophic thoughts and replacing them with realistic ones) tackles beliefs like "this tingling means I'm suffocating", which the mislabelling data (Dirks & Schraa) showed drive re-hospitalisation independent of lung function.
  • CBT also treats the panic disorder that is over-represented in asthma, using the same tools as for panic generally (education, breathing control, challenging catastrophic beliefs, graded exposure to feared sensations).

(b) Stress management. Broad stress-management training (relaxation + problem-solving + lifestyle change) addresses the acute and chronic stress that, per Sandberg et al., precipitates attacks.

(c) Systematic desensitisation. Systematic desensitisation (Wolpe's method: pairing deep relaxation with a step-by-step "ladder" of feared situations so the fear is unlearned) rests on reciprocal inhibition (you cannot be deeply relaxed and anxious at the same time). Because emotional arousal can catalyse attacks, desensitisation is used to abort the anxiety that complicates an attack; patients trained with desensitisation, assertiveness and progressive relaxation have reported clear improvement in controlling emotional upset from noxious triggers.

What desensitisation does — and does not — do

The best controlled study (Miklich and colleagues at the National Asthma Center, using twice-daily FEV₁) found systematic desensitisation gave no clinically significant, lasting effect on lung function in severely asthmatic children. The sound conclusion: anxiety-reduction therapies should not be expected to change the pulmonary response itself — they treat the emotional concomitants, which is still worthwhile.

(d) Operant conditioning / contingency management. Operant conditioning (shaping behaviour by its consequences — rewarding wanted behaviour, not rewarding unwanted behaviour) targets maladaptive asthma-related behaviours such as malingering, excessive secondary gains and inadequate social behaviour.

  • Positive reinforcement (rewarding a desired behaviour so it happens more) can teach a child to use a forced expiratory volume (FEV) device (a gadget that measures how hard you can blow out) correctly — though over-doing the reward can backfire. This does not change asthma severity; it removes the maladaptive secondary gains.
  • A learned maladaptive habit (e.g. an attention-seeking cough) can be extinguished if it is not reinforced (Moore, 1965): parents should give minimal attention to such coughing and may use a time-out to remove the reward.
  • Negotiation and contracting (Creer) was used to improve medication adherence in children: psychologist, physician and child jointly sign a contract specifying that when the child reliably self-medicates correctly, they earn a reward (e.g. discharge home), with monitoring gradually faded. Monthly reports showed it was highly effective for helping children control their asthma.

(e) Self-monitoring with peak-flow diaries. Self-monitoring (observing and recording your own symptoms and readings) via an asthma diary records daily symptoms, morning/evening peak expiratory flow (PEF) (the fastest speed you can blow air out, measured on a hand-held meter) and medicines taken. It both provides data and prompts the patient to stay engaged; targets should be clearly defined and recorded only for set periods so patients are not overwhelmed.

4. Biofeedback (EMG, respiratory/capnometry, HRV)

The core idea. Biofeedback (using sensors to show you a body signal in real time — as a sound or picture — so you can learn to change it) monitors some physiological function and feeds it back to the person, whose task is to shift that signal in the healthy direction. It turns an invisible body process into something you can see and practise controlling.

  • EMG (frontalis) biofeedback. EMG — electromyographic (measuring the electrical activity, i.e. the tension, in a muscle) biofeedback, often from the frontalis (the forehead muscle), teaches general muscle relaxation and lowers arousal — useful for the tension and anxiety that ride with attacks.
  • Respiratory biofeedback / capnometry. Capnometry-assisted biofeedback (showing the patient their exhaled CO₂ level in real time) directly retrains over-breathing: the patient learns to raise their CO₂ back to normal and so abolish hypocapnia — a precise way to fix the hyperventilation that drives the panic loop. Airway-resistance biofeedback has produced only very small, cautiously-interpreted decreases in airway resistance and needs complex, expensive equipment.
  • Heart-rate-variability (HRV) biofeedback. HRV — heart rate variability (the natural beat-to-beat variation in the time between heartbeats; more variability generally signals a flexible, well-regulated nervous system) biofeedback trains people to breathe at a slow resonance frequency (roughly six breaths a minute, the rate that makes heart rate swing most with the breath), boosting vagal tone and calming the autonomic system. In asthma it has been studied as an adjunct that can reduce symptoms and reliever medication use, chiefly by improving autonomic balance rather than curing the airways.
Worked example — biofeedback for a frightened asthmatic child

In an early case, a severely asthmatic child was trained using a galvanic skin response (a measure of sweat-gland activity that tracks arousal) signal to relax and so dampen the emotional reaction to upsetting family and environmental cues. His wheezing subsided and breathing became easier within about 15 minutes of an attack's onset. Promising — but the literature is honest that biofeedback has not become a dependable standalone therapy, partly because of equipment cost.

5. Self-management & COPD pulmonary rehabilitation

(a) Creer's self-management model. Self-management (the patient actively running their own day-to-day care, in partnership with professionals) is the integrating framework. Creer's model runs through: (i) goal setting (patient + physician agree targets such as taking medicines on schedule and avoiding triggers); (ii) information collection (self-monitoring via the diary); (iii) information processing & evaluation (detect changes, compare against a personal-best peak flow, judge causes and consequences); (iv) decision-making (thoughtful, cautious, generating options, not misreading severity); (v) action (performing self-management skills, with self-instruction); and (vi) self-reaction (judging one's own performance, building self-efficacy (Bandura's term for the belief that you can carry out a task)).

Evidence — what self-management achieves

Across paediatric and adult programmes, common outcomes include fewer hospitalisations and ER visits, less school/work absence, lower medication use, less depression and lower costs, alongside more knowledge, better skills, better adherence, higher self-efficacy and better quality of life. The results were strong enough that expert panels recommend self-management training for all asthma patients.

(b) Pulmonary rehabilitation for COPD. Because COPD is largely irreversible, the goal is to minimise disability, not reverse disease — by enhancing medical therapy, controlling symptoms, improving function and lifting quality of life. A comprehensive programme (Ries, 1995) uses a multidisciplinary team and has two parts:

  • Patient evaluation in five stages — interview (and team-building), medical evaluation, diagnostic testing (lung function, exercise and blood-gas testing), psychological assessment (mood, cognition, reaction to dyspnoea, sexual function, social support), and realistic goal-setting.
  • Program content in four areas — general education; respiratory & chest physiotherapy (bronchial hygiene, breathing retraining including pursed-lip breathing, correct equipment and oxygen use); exercise prescription (simple, useful exercises to build tolerance and endurance); and psychosocial support (combating hopelessness and helping the patient and significant others cope).
  • Expected gains: fewer respiratory and psychological symptoms and less use of medical resources; more quality of life, activity, exercise tolerance, daily-living ability, knowledge and independence; and possibly better lung function, return to work and longer survival.

(c) Family interventions & adherence support. Because family patterns can maintain maladaptive asthma behaviour (Topic 4), significant others are brought into treatment; contracting and reinforcement (above) support adherence — still the major barrier to good control of both asthma and COPD.

6. The evidence verdict — one honest paragraph

Pull it together: psychological, behavioural and biofeedback methods reliably help the emotional and behavioural side of airway disease — less anxiety and panic, corrected over-breathing, fewer maladaptive habits, better adherence, fewer admissions and better quality of life. Their direct effects on lung function are small and should not be oversold. That is exactly why the syllabus and every source call them adjuncts: they sit on top of proper medical treatment, making the disease easier to live with and manage — never replacing the inhaler, the controller medicine or the doctor.

Management at a glance
  • Adjunct rule: all methods add to — never replace — medical care; two specialists (physician + behavioural therapist); three aims (lung function, emotion, maladaptive behaviour).
  • Psychological/behavioural: psychoeducation; relaxation (PMR, autogenic); breathing retraining (diaphragmatic, Buteyko, Papworth, pursed-lip); CBT & cognitive restructuring; stress management; systematic desensitisation; operant/contingency management & contracting; self-monitoring with peak-flow diaries.
  • Biofeedback: EMG (tension), capnometry/respiratory (over-breathing), HRV/resonance breathing (autonomic balance).
  • Integrating frames: Creer's self-management (6 steps) for asthma; multidisciplinary pulmonary rehabilitation for COPD; family & adherence support throughout.
  • Verdict: strong on the emotional/behavioural layer; small direct effect on lung function; always an adjunct.
