1. Problem Statement

Substance use disorders (SUDs) represent one of the most significant global public health challenges, affecting over 35 million people worldwide according to the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC, 2023). In India, the magnitude of the problem is staggering—the National Survey on Extent and Pattern of Substance Use (2019) estimated that approximately 14.6% of the Indian population (aged 10–75 years) uses alcohol, 2.8% uses cannabis, and 2.1% uses opioids, with significant proportions meeting criteria for dependence.

The defining challenge in addiction treatment is not achieving initial abstinence but maintaining recovery and preventing relapse. Relapse rates across substance use disorders remain alarmingly high, with 40–60% of patients returning to substance use within the first year following treatment (McLellan et al., 2000; Brandon et al., 2007). Traditional relapse prevention approaches, while valuable, have shown only modest effect sizes (Irvin et al., 1999), highlighting the urgent need for innovative, evidence-based adjunctive interventions.

The Role of Craving

Craving—the intense, often overwhelming desire to use a substance—is recognized as a core feature of substance dependence in both DSM-5 and ICD-11. It serves as a primary trigger for relapse, with research demonstrating that craving intensity prospectively predicts treatment dropout, lapse episodes, and full relapse (Tiffany & Wray, 2012; Serre et al., 2015). Conventional relapse prevention strategies often attempt to suppress or avoid craving, which paradoxically may increase its intensity through rebound effects.

The Role of Impulsivity

Impulsivity—the tendency to act rashly without adequate forethought or consideration of consequences—is both a risk factor for and a consequence of chronic substance use. Elevated impulsivity compromises the ability to implement relapse prevention strategies, resist urges, and delay gratification (Verdejo-García et al., 2008). Impulsivity operates across multiple dimensions: motor (acting without thinking), attentional (inability to focus), and non-planning (lack of future orientation), each contributing uniquely to relapse vulnerability.

Mindfulness as a Therapeutic Mechanism

Mindfulness—defined as non-judgmental, present-moment awareness—offers a fundamentally different approach to managing cravings and impulsive urges. Rather than suppressing or avoiding these experiences, mindfulness-based approaches train individuals to observe cravings and impulses with acceptance, allowing them to arise and dissipate naturally without triggering habitual substance-seeking behaviour ("urge surfing"; Marlatt & Gordon, 1985; Bowen et al., 2014).

The Brief Intervention Challenge

While full-length Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP) programs (typically 8 weeks) have demonstrated efficacy, their feasibility in resource-limited settings, inpatient units with short stays, and populations with limited attention spans remains questionable. Brief adaptations (4–6 sessions) represent a pragmatic alternative but require rigorous evaluation to establish their therapeutic potency.

Core Problem: Despite the established role of craving and impulsivity in driving relapse among substance-dependent patients, there is insufficient evidence regarding the efficacy of brief, time-limited mindfulness-based relapse prevention interventions in reducing these relapse-precipitating factors and enhancing dispositional mindfulness within clinical addiction treatment settings.

2. Research Gap

2.1 Limited Research on Brief Adaptations

The majority of MBRP research has evaluated the standard 8-week protocol (Bowen et al., 2009, 2014). Brief adaptations (≤6 sessions) have received far less empirical attention, despite their greater clinical practicality. The few studies examining abbreviated protocols have yielded mixed results, and optimal dose-response parameters remain unclear (Garland et al., 2016).

2.2 Insufficient Focus on Mechanism Variables

While MBRP trials have primarily examined substance use outcomes (abstinence, relapse rates), fewer studies have simultaneously evaluated the mechanisms through which MBRP operates—specifically, whether changes in craving and impulsivity mediate treatment effects. Understanding these mechanisms is crucial for optimizing interventions and identifying active therapeutic ingredients.

2.3 Heterogeneous Substance Categories

Existing MBRP research has predominantly focused on alcohol use disorders or combined substance categories without disaggregating effects by primary substance. Whether brief MBRP demonstrates equivalent efficacy across alcohol, opioid, cannabis, and stimulant dependence remains an open empirical question with important clinical implications.

2.4 Scarcity of Indian Research

MBRP research in Indian populations is extremely limited despite India's massive substance use burden. Cultural factors—including collectivistic orientations, spiritual traditions that may enhance receptivity to mindfulness, different treatment infrastructure, and family involvement in recovery—may significantly moderate intervention effects. Contextually relevant evidence is essential for implementation.

2.5 Impulsivity as an Outcome and Moderator

While impulsivity is often studied as a predictor of substance use outcomes, its responsiveness to mindfulness interventions—particularly brief ones—is understudied. The question of whether impulsivity can be meaningfully reduced through short-term mindfulness training in actively dependent populations represents a significant empirical gap.

2.6 Lack of Pre-Post Design in Inpatient Settings

Most MBRP trials have been conducted in outpatient or aftercare settings. The efficacy of brief MBRP when delivered during inpatient rehabilitation—a period of enforced abstinence where patients are maximally accessible—remains insufficiently explored, particularly regarding whether gains during inpatient stay translate to post-discharge maintenance.

Justification: This study addresses the convergence of multiple research gaps: evaluating a brief (rather than full-length) MBRP protocol, examining craving and impulsivity as mechanism variables (rather than only substance use outcomes), conducting research in an Indian clinical context, and assessing whether mindfulness can be cultivated in substance-dependent patients within a compressed timeframe.

3. Aim of the Study

Primary Aim: To evaluate the efficacy of a brief Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP) intervention in reducing craving and impulsivity, and enhancing mindfulness, in patients with substance dependence undergoing treatment, as compared to treatment-as-usual (TAU).

4. Objectives

  1. To assess the level of craving (intensity and frequency) in substance-dependent patients before and after the brief MBRP intervention, compared to treatment-as-usual.
  2. To evaluate changes in impulsivity (motor, attentional, and non-planning dimensions) following the brief MBRP intervention as compared to the control group.
  3. To examine changes in dispositional mindfulness (awareness, non-reactivity, non-judgment, observation, and description) pre- and post-intervention in both groups.
  4. To compare the efficacy of brief MBRP plus TAU versus TAU alone on all outcome variables (craving, impulsivity, mindfulness).
  5. To explore the relationship between changes in mindfulness and changes in craving and impulsivity following the intervention.
  6. To examine whether baseline impulsivity moderates the treatment response to the brief MBRP intervention on craving reduction.

5. Hypotheses

5.1 Primary Hypotheses

Hypothesis 1: Effect on Craving

H₀: There is no significant difference in craving scores between the brief MBRP group and the TAU group at post-intervention.

H₁: Patients receiving the brief MBRP intervention will demonstrate significantly lower craving scores at post-intervention compared to patients receiving TAU alone.

Hypothesis 2: Effect on Impulsivity

H₀: There is no significant difference in impulsivity scores between the brief MBRP group and the TAU group at post-intervention.

H₁: Patients receiving the brief MBRP intervention will demonstrate significantly lower impulsivity scores at post-intervention compared to patients receiving TAU alone.

Hypothesis 3: Effect on Mindfulness

H₀: There is no significant difference in mindfulness scores between the brief MBRP group and the TAU group at post-intervention.

H₁: Patients receiving the brief MBRP intervention will demonstrate significantly higher mindfulness scores at post-intervention compared to patients receiving TAU alone.

5.2 Secondary Hypotheses

Hypothesis 4: Mindfulness as Mechanism

H₀: Changes in mindfulness are not significantly correlated with changes in craving and impulsivity.

H₁: Increases in mindfulness will be significantly associated with reductions in craving and impulsivity in the MBRP group (i.e., mindfulness mediates treatment effects).

Hypothesis 5: Within-Group Pre-Post Changes

H₀: There is no significant pre-to-post change in craving, impulsivity, or mindfulness within the MBRP group.

H₁: The MBRP group will show significant pre-to-post reductions in craving and impulsivity, and significant increases in mindfulness.

Hypothesis 6: Moderation by Baseline Impulsivity

H₀: Baseline impulsivity does not moderate the effect of brief MBRP on craving reduction.

H₁: Patients with higher baseline impulsivity will show greater benefit from brief MBRP on craving reduction (i.e., those who need it most benefit most).

7. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

7.1 Inclusion Criteria

  1. Diagnosis of Substance Dependence Syndrome as per ICD-11 (6C40–6C4F) or Substance Use Disorder (moderate to severe) as per DSM-5 criteria, confirmed by a qualified psychiatrist
  2. Age range: 18–55 years
  3. Male and female patients
  4. Currently admitted to or enrolled in a substance use treatment program (inpatient or intensive outpatient)
  5. Completed initial detoxification phase (at least 7 days since last substance use) to ensure cognitive capacity for mindfulness engagement
  6. Minimum education: Ability to read and comprehend assessment instruments (at least 5th standard education or equivalent literacy)
  7. Willing to attend all intervention sessions and complete assessments
  8. Informed written consent provided by the participant

7.2 Exclusion Criteria

  1. Active psychotic disorder (schizophrenia, delusional disorder, psychotic features) — may impair capacity for mindfulness practice
  2. Severe cognitive impairment or intellectual disability (IQ < 70) — may preclude understanding of intervention concepts
  3. Current active suicidal ideation or recent suicide attempt (within 3 months) — safety priority
  4. History of neurological disorder (epilepsy, traumatic brain injury, stroke) that may affect cognitive measures
  5. Current withdrawal symptoms requiring medical management (CIWA-Ar > 10 or equivalent) — cannot participate until medically stabilized
  6. Prior formal mindfulness or meditation training (> 10 hours lifetime) — may confound baseline mindfulness levels
  7. Inability to participate in group sessions due to severe behavioural disturbance or non-cooperation
  8. Concurrent participation in another structured psychological intervention study

8. Tools / Instruments

8.1 Primary Outcome Measures

Penn Alcohol Craving Scale (PACS) — Adapted for Substance Craving

Description: A 5-item self-report measure assessing frequency, intensity, and duration of craving in the past week. Originally developed for alcohol craving (Flannery et al., 1999), it has been successfully adapted for other substances. Items rated on 0–6 Likert scale.

Key Indices: Total craving score (range 0–30; higher = more intense craving).

Psychometric Properties: Internal consistency: α = 0.92; test-retest reliability: r = 0.82; predictive validity for relapse demonstrated (Flannery et al., 1999).

Visual Analogue Scale for Craving (VAS-C)

Description: A single-item 100mm visual analogue scale where patients mark their current craving intensity from "No craving at all" (0) to "Most intense craving ever experienced" (100). Administered at each session for tracking within-intervention change.

Psychometric Properties: Test-retest reliability: r = 0.71–0.86; convergent validity with multi-item craving measures established (Sayette et al., 2000).

Barratt Impulsiveness Scale – Version 11 (BIS-11)

Description: A 30-item self-report questionnaire measuring impulsivity across three dimensions: Attentional Impulsiveness (inability to focus/concentrate), Motor Impulsiveness (acting without thinking), and Non-Planning Impulsiveness (lack of future orientation). Items rated on 1–4 scale.

Key Indices: Total impulsivity score (range 30–120); three subscale scores.

Psychometric Properties: Internal consistency: α = 0.79–0.83; well-validated in substance use populations; Indian normative data available (Patton et al., 1995; Stanford et al., 2009).

Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ)

Description: A 39-item measure assessing five facets of dispositional mindfulness: Observing (noticing internal/external experiences), Describing (labelling experiences with words), Acting with Awareness (attending to present activity), Non-Judging (non-evaluative stance toward experiences), and Non-Reactivity (allowing experiences without reacting).

Key Indices: Total mindfulness score and five facet scores; items rated 1–5.

Psychometric Properties: Internal consistency: α = 0.75–0.91 across facets; sensitive to change following mindfulness interventions; validated in Indian samples (Baer et al., 2006; validated Hindi version available).

8.2 Secondary / Screening Measures

Severity of Dependence Scale (SDS)

Description: A 5-item self-report measure assessing psychological aspects of dependence including compulsion to use, perceived control, anxiety about use, and difficulty stopping. Applicable across substance types.

Psychometric Properties: Internal consistency: α = 0.80–0.90; validated for multiple substances (Gossop et al., 1995).

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7)

Description: Brief, validated screening instruments for depressive (PHQ-9, 9 items) and anxiety (GAD-7, 7 items) symptoms. Used to assess and control for comorbid affective symptoms that may confound outcome variables.

Psychometric Properties: PHQ-9: sensitivity 88%, specificity 88% for major depression; GAD-7: α = 0.92; both validated in Indian populations (Kroenke et al., 2001; Spitzer et al., 2006).

Sociodemographic and Clinical Data Sheet

Description: Semi-structured proforma collecting: age, gender, education, occupation, marital status, socioeconomic status, primary substance of dependence, duration of use, age at first use, duration of dependence, number of prior treatments, current medications, family history of substance use, and motivation for treatment.

Session Attendance and Home Practice Log

Description: Intervention fidelity tracking form documenting session attendance, engagement ratings (therapist-rated), and self-reported home mindfulness practice (minutes/day, days/week). Essential for dose-response analysis.

9. Methodology

9.1 Research Design

Quasi-experimental, pre-test post-test control group design. Participants will be assigned to either the experimental group (Brief MBRP + TAU) or the control group (TAU alone). Assessments will be conducted at baseline (pre-intervention) and immediately post-intervention (after the final session). This design allows evaluation of between-group differences and within-group changes.

Design Notation:

Experimental Group: O₁ → X (Brief MBRP + TAU) → O₂

Control Group: O₁ → TAU → O₂

Where O₁ = Pre-test, O₂ = Post-test, X = Brief MBRP Intervention

9.2 Setting

The study will be conducted at a de-addiction centre / substance use treatment facility attached to a tertiary psychiatric hospital. Both inpatient and intensive outpatient settings will be utilized for recruitment.

9.3 Sampling Technique

Purposive consecutive sampling with non-random assignment: Consecutive patients meeting inclusion criteria will be recruited and assigned to experimental or control groups. Assignment may follow an alternating pattern or be based on admission timing to minimize selection bias while maintaining feasibility.

9.4 Sample Size Justification

Based on G*Power analysis for between-group comparison (independent t-test / repeated measures ANOVA):

  • Effect size: d = 0.60 (medium-to-large, based on prior MBRP studies: Bowen et al., 2014; Witkiewitz & Bowen, 2010)
  • Power: 0.80 (β = 0.20)
  • Alpha level: 0.05 (two-tailed)
  • Minimum required per group: n = 36
  • Accounting for 20% dropout (common in addiction research): Target: n = 45 per group; Total N = 90

9.5 The Brief MBRP Intervention Protocol

Intervention Structure:

  • Duration: 6 sessions over 3 weeks (twice weekly)
  • Session Length: 60–75 minutes per session
  • Format: Closed group (6–10 participants per group)
  • Facilitator: Trained clinical psychologist with mindfulness training

Session Content (Adapted from Bowen, Chawla & Marlatt, 2011):

  1. Session 1 — Automatic Pilot and Relapse: Mindful awareness introduction; raisin exercise; body scan; psychoeducation on automatic patterns in addiction
  2. Session 2 — Awareness of Triggers: Identifying internal/external triggers; sitting meditation focusing on breath; discussion of trigger-craving-use cycle
  3. Session 3 — Mindfulness in Daily Life & Urge Surfing: Introduction to "urge surfing" technique; mindfulness of cravings practice; allowing cravings without acting on them
  4. Session 4 — Mindfulness in High-Risk Situations: Identifying personal high-risk situations; SOBER breathing space (Stop, Observe, Breathe, Expand, Respond); cognitive defusion from craving thoughts
  5. Session 5 — Acceptance and Skilful Action: Balancing acceptance and change; compassion meditation; responding vs. reacting to difficult emotions
  6. Session 6 — Social Support, Integration, and Relapse Prevention Plan: Developing a personalized mindfulness-based relapse prevention plan; commitment to ongoing practice; review and consolidation

Home Practice:

Participants will be provided guided audio recordings (10–15 minutes) for daily practice between sessions. Practice logs will be maintained.

9.6 Treatment-as-Usual (TAU) — Control Condition

The TAU group will receive standard addiction treatment including: pharmacotherapy as prescribed by the treating psychiatrist, individual counselling, group therapy (non-mindfulness-based), psychoeducation sessions, family involvement, and 12-step facilitation (where applicable). The TAU condition will be documented to characterize the comparison treatment.

9.7 Procedure (Step-by-Step)

  1. Ethical Approval: Obtain Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) approval
  2. Screening and Recruitment: Screen admitted patients against inclusion/exclusion criteria; approach eligible patients after detoxification phase
  3. Informed Consent: Explain study procedures, voluntary nature, right to withdraw; obtain written informed consent
  4. Baseline Assessment (O₁): Administer sociodemographic form, SDS, PACS, VAS-C, BIS-11, FFMQ, PHQ-9, GAD-7
  5. Group Assignment: Assign to MBRP + TAU or TAU alone
  6. Intervention Delivery: Deliver 6-session Brief MBRP to experimental group over 3 weeks; control group continues TAU
  7. Session Monitoring: Record attendance, administer VAS-C at each session, maintain practice logs
  8. Post-Intervention Assessment (O₂): Within 3 days of final session, re-administer PACS, VAS-C, BIS-11, FFMQ
  9. Data Entry and Analysis: Double-entry verification; statistical analysis per analysis plan

9.8 Statistical Analysis Plan

  • Descriptive Statistics: Mean, SD, frequencies for demographic and clinical variables
  • Baseline Equivalence: Independent t-tests/chi-square to confirm groups are comparable at baseline
  • Primary Analyses: Mixed-design ANOVA (2 groups × 2 time points) for each outcome variable; ANCOVA controlling for significant covariates
  • Within-Group Changes: Paired t-tests / Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for pre-post changes within each group
  • Effect Sizes: Cohen's d for between-group differences; Hedge's g if sample sizes unequal
  • Correlation/Mediation: Pearson's correlations between change scores; mediation analysis (mindfulness change → craving/impulsivity change) using Baron & Kenny or bootstrapping approach
  • Moderation: Hierarchical regression to test baseline impulsivity as moderator
  • Software: SPSS v.26 or JASP; significance level set at p < 0.05

9.9 Ethical Considerations

  • Institutional Ethics Committee approval obtained prior to recruitment
  • Informed written consent ensuring voluntary participation; no coercion
  • Right to withdraw at any time without impact on standard treatment
  • Control group receives full standard treatment (TAU is not a no-treatment condition)
  • Confidentiality maintained through coded identifiers; data stored securely
  • Mindfulness intervention is low-risk; however, monitoring for adverse reactions (increased anxiety during early meditation) with appropriate clinical support
  • Referral for additional support if distress emerges during intervention
  • MBRP offered to control group after study completion (wait-list component)
  • Compliance with Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and ICMR Ethical Guidelines (2017)

10. Review of Literature

10.1 Substance Use Disorders: Relapse as the Central Challenge

Substance use disorders are characterized by a chronic relapsing course, with relapse being the norm rather than the exception. McLellan et al. (2000) demonstrated that relapse rates in addiction (40–60%) are comparable to those in other chronic medical conditions (diabetes, hypertension, asthma), reframing relapse as a management challenge rather than treatment failure. However, the personal, social, and economic costs of relapse—including overdose risk, family disruption, employment loss, and criminal justice involvement—necessitate continued innovation in relapse prevention strategies.

Traditional cognitive-behavioural relapse prevention (Marlatt & Gordon, 1985) focuses on identifying high-risk situations, developing coping skills, and enhancing self-efficacy. While effective (Irvin et al., 1999; meta-analytic effect size d = 0.14–0.35), these approaches have limitations: they rely on cognitive control resources that are depleted during stress and craving states, and they frame cravings as problems to be eliminated rather than experiences to be navigated.

10.2 Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention: Theoretical and Empirical Foundations

Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP) was developed by Bowen, Chawla, and Marlatt (2011) as an integration of mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR; Kabat-Zinn, 1990), mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT; Segal, Williams & Teasdale, 2002), and cognitive-behavioural relapse prevention. MBRP proposes that mindfulness addresses addiction through several mechanisms:

  • Decoupling stimulus and response: Creating a gap between craving experience and substance-seeking behaviour
  • Reducing experiential avoidance: Teaching tolerance of uncomfortable internal states rather than escape through substance use
  • Enhancing metacognitive awareness: Recognizing cravings as transient mental events rather than imperatives requiring action
  • Disrupting automaticity: Shifting from habitual, reactive patterns to deliberate, chosen responses

Key Empirical Evidence:

  • Bowen et al. (2009) — Randomized trial: MBRP reduced substance use and craving compared to TAU at 2-month follow-up
  • Bowen et al. (2014) — Landmark RCT (n = 286): MBRP showed lower relapse rates than standard relapse prevention and TAU at 12-month follow-up for drug use
  • Witkiewitz & Bowen (2010) — Mediation analysis: Showed that MBRP's effects on substance use were mediated by increased acceptance and decreased craving reactivity
  • Grant et al. (2017) — Meta-analysis of mindfulness-based interventions for addiction: overall effect size d = 0.33 for substance use outcomes
  • Li et al. (2017) — Systematic review: MBRP showed moderate effects on craving (d = 0.42) and substance use frequency

10.3 Craving: Neurobiological and Psychological Perspectives

Craving is conceptualized as a multidimensional construct involving cognitive (expectancies, thoughts about use), emotional (affective states triggering desire), physiological (somatic sensations), and behavioural (approach tendencies) components. Neuroscientifically, craving involves activation of reward circuitry (ventral striatum, orbitofrontal cortex) and reduced prefrontal regulatory control (Goldstein & Volkow, 2011).

Mindfulness may modulate craving through multiple pathways: enhancing prefrontal cortical regulation, reducing amygdala reactivity to drug cues, strengthening attention to present-moment experience versus future substance use fantasies, and promoting acceptance that reduces craving escalation through struggle (Brewer et al., 2013; Garland et al., 2014).

Witkiewitz et al. (2013) demonstrated that MBRP participants showed a decoupling of craving and substance use—craving still occurred but no longer automatically led to use, suggesting a fundamental shift in the craving-behaviour relationship.

10.4 Impulsivity and Substance Use: A Bidirectional Relationship

Impulsivity is consistently identified as both a predisposing trait for substance use initiation and a consequence of chronic substance exposure. Meta-analyses confirm elevated impulsivity across all substance use disorders compared to healthy controls (Smith et al., 2014; effect sizes d = 0.50–0.80).

The relationship between mindfulness and impulsivity is theoretically compelling: mindfulness cultivates the capacity to pause between stimulus and response, directly counteracting the defining feature of impulsivity (acting without reflection). Research supports this:

  • Murphy & MacKillop (2012) — Higher mindfulness associated with lower impulsivity and better delay discounting in substance users
  • Peters et al. (2011) — Mindfulness training reduced impulsive decision-making in laboratory tasks
  • Karyadi et al. (2014) — Meta-analysis: mindfulness negatively correlated with impulsivity (r = -0.34) across substance-using populations
  • However, whether brief interventions (vs. extensive training) can meaningfully shift trait impulsivity remains debated (Korponay et al., 2019)

10.5 Brief Mindfulness Interventions: Feasibility and Efficacy

The question of minimal effective dose is critical for clinical implementation. Emerging research on brief/abbreviated mindfulness protocols suggests:

  • Zeidan et al. (2010) — Even 4 days of brief mindfulness training (20 min/day) improved cognition and mood
  • Garland et al. (2010) — A 10-session Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement (MORE) showed significant effects on craving and stress reactivity
  • Glasner-Edwards et al. (2017) — A 4-session mindfulness intervention for stimulant users showed preliminary efficacy on craving and negative affect
  • Zgierska et al. (2009) — Even short-form mindfulness practices (8 minutes) produced acute reductions in craving in alcohol-dependent patients
  • Gap: Systematic evaluation of 6-session MBRP protocols with rigorous pre-post measurement of craving, impulsivity, and mindfulness remains limited, particularly in Indian clinical populations

10.6 Indian Context: Substance Use and Mindfulness Research

India faces a growing substance use crisis compounded by limited treatment infrastructure, stigma, and diverse patterns of substance use (alcohol, tobacco, opioids, cannabis). While mindfulness has deep cultural roots in Indian contemplative traditions (Vipassana, yoga-based practices), its systematic application in addiction treatment within formal clinical settings is nascent. Notable Indian studies include:

  • Sharma et al. (2019) — Yoga-based intervention reduced craving in alcohol dependence
  • Kumar et al. (2014) — Vipassana meditation in prison populations reduced substance use
  • However, structured MBRP protocols tested in Indian de-addiction settings with standardized outcome measures remain rare

Research Trends (2019–2024): Recent literature emphasizes: (a) digital/app-based mindfulness delivery for addiction populations; (b) neuroimaging studies showing MBRP-related brain changes in craving circuitry; (c) personalized approaches matching mindfulness techniques to individual craving profiles; and (d) integration of mindfulness with pharmacotherapy for synergistic effects.

11. Theoretical Framework

11.1 Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention Model (Bowen, Chawla & Marlatt, 2011)

The MBRP model integrates Marlatt's cognitive-behavioural relapse prevention framework with mindfulness principles. It proposes that relapse is driven by a cascade: High-risk situation → Automatic conditioned response (craving) → Habitual behavioural pattern (substance use). Mindfulness interrupts this cascade by:

  • Increasing awareness of the cascade as it unfolds (metacognitive monitoring)
  • Creating a "space" between trigger and response (response inhibition)
  • Reducing the distress value of craving through acceptance (reducing experiential avoidance)
  • Enabling choice of alternative responses (cognitive flexibility)

Relevance to Current Study: This model directly informs the intervention protocol and predicts that increases in mindfulness should mediate reductions in craving (through changed relationship with craving experiences) and impulsivity (through enhanced response inhibition).

11.2 Incentive Sensitization Theory (Robinson & Berridge, 1993, 2008)

This neurobiological theory proposes that repeated substance exposure sensitizes the mesolimbic dopamine system, producing pathological "wanting" (craving/incentive salience) that becomes dissociated from "liking" (pleasure). Sensitized craving is triggered automatically by substance-associated cues, operating largely below conscious awareness.

Relevance to Current Study: Mindfulness may counteract incentive sensitization by: (a) bringing automatic craving processes into conscious awareness, (b) reducing the motivational power of craving through observation rather than engagement, and (c) strengthening top-down prefrontal regulation over sensitized subcortical reward circuits. The study's measurement of craving reduction tests whether brief MBRP can modulate this fundamental addiction mechanism.

11.3 Dual-Process Theory of Addiction (Wiers & Stacy, 2006; Bechara, 2005)

Dual-process models propose that addiction reflects an imbalance between two cognitive systems: (a) an impulsive, automatic system (fast, associative, cue-reactive) that generates craving and approach tendencies, and (b) a reflective, executive system (slow, deliberate, controlled) that enables regulation and goal-directed behaviour. In addiction, the impulsive system dominates, particularly under conditions of stress, intoxication, or resource depletion.

Relevance to Current Study: Impulsivity (BIS-11) measures the dominance of the automatic system. Mindfulness training may strengthen the reflective system by enhancing attentional control, reducing automaticity of substance-related cognitions, and increasing the capacity for deliberate response selection. Brief MBRP tests whether even short-term training can shift this balance.

11.4 Self-Regulation Failure Theory (Baumeister & Heatherton, 1996)

This theory posits that substance use represents a failure of self-regulatory capacity—the ability to override prepotent impulses in service of long-term goals. Self-regulation draws on limited cognitive resources that become depleted under stress, negative affect, and craving. Mindfulness may operate as a "self-regulatory training" that:

  • Strengthens attentional control (the foundation of self-regulation)
  • Reduces emotional reactivity (decreasing regulatory demand)
  • Builds acceptance of discomfort (reducing reliance on avoidant coping/substance use)
  • May increase self-regulatory "capacity" through repeated practice (neuroplasticity)

11.5 Integrative Theoretical Model for the Current Study

The present study integrates these theoretical frameworks into the following working model:

  1. Substance dependence creates sensitized craving responses (incentive sensitization) and impaired reflective control (dual-process imbalance)
  2. Brief MBRP intervention cultivates mindfulness—the capacity for present-moment, non-judgmental awareness
  3. Enhanced mindfulness reduces craving intensity through: changed relationship with craving (observation vs. engagement), increased prefrontal regulation, and reduced experiential avoidance
  4. Enhanced mindfulness reduces impulsivity through: strengthened response inhibition, improved attentional control, and disruption of automatic behavioural patterns
  5. These changes (reduced craving + reduced impulsivity + enhanced mindfulness) collectively reduce relapse vulnerability in the post-treatment period

Testable Pathway: Brief MBRP → ↑ Mindfulness → ↓ Craving + ↓ Impulsivity → ↓ Relapse Risk

12. Significance of the Study

12.1 Clinical Implications

  • Relapse Prevention Enhancement: If efficacious, brief MBRP provides a time-efficient, cost-effective adjunct to standard addiction treatment that can be integrated into existing programs without requiring extensive additional resources.
  • Mechanism Understanding: Demonstrating that MBRP reduces craving and impulsivity (not just substance use) clarifies the therapeutic mechanisms and identifies specific treatment targets for personalized intervention planning.
  • Inpatient Application: Establishing efficacy within the treatment setting validates MBRP as a feasible component of inpatient/residential programs where patients are accessible but stays are time-limited.
  • Skill Generalization: Mindfulness skills taught during treatment provide patients with portable, self-administered coping tools that can be applied indefinitely post-discharge without therapist dependency.
  • Addressing Impulsivity: Demonstrating impulsivity reduction through brief mindfulness training has implications beyond addiction—for impulse control difficulties across psychiatric conditions.

12.2 Academic Contribution

  • Contributes to the limited literature on brief/abbreviated MBRP protocols and establishes minimum effective dose parameters
  • Simultaneously examines multiple mechanism variables (craving, impulsivity, mindfulness) rather than only substance use outcomes
  • Tests theoretically-derived hypotheses regarding mindfulness as a mediator of treatment effects
  • Provides data from an Indian clinical sample, enabling cross-cultural comparison with Western MBRP research
  • Informs the broader "how much is enough?" question in mindfulness-based intervention research

12.3 Relevance in the Indian Context

  • Cultural Resonance: India's rich contemplative traditions (yoga, Vipassana, pranayama) create cultural familiarity with mindfulness concepts, potentially enhancing treatment acceptability and engagement in Indian populations
  • Resource Constraints: Brief interventions are particularly relevant for Indian treatment settings where long-term psychotherapy access is limited, therapist-patient ratios are unfavorable, and inpatient stays are often short
  • Growing Burden: India's substance use epidemic (particularly alcohol and opioids) requires innovative, scalable interventions; brief MBRP offers a potentially disseminable approach
  • Policy Relevance: Findings can inform NIMHANS/WHO guidelines and the National Action Plan for Drug Demand Reduction (NAPDDR) regarding evidence-based psychological interventions for addiction
  • Training Implications: If brief MBRP is effective, it suggests that clinical psychologists and addiction counsellors can be trained to deliver impactful interventions within realistic timeframes

13. Expected Outcomes

13.1 Anticipated Results

  1. Craving Reduction: The MBRP group is expected to demonstrate significantly lower craving scores (PACS, VAS-C) at post-intervention compared to both their own baseline and the TAU control group. Anticipated effect size: d = 0.50–0.70 (medium-to-large), based on prior MBRP studies showing moderate craving effects (Li et al., 2017).
  2. Impulsivity Reduction: Moderate reductions in BIS-11 total scores and particularly the Attentional Impulsiveness subscale are expected in the MBRP group, given that mindfulness primarily targets attentional processes. Effect size: d = 0.30–0.50 (small-to-medium), as trait impulsivity is more resistant to brief intervention than state measures.
  3. Mindfulness Enhancement: Significant increases in FFMQ total scores and particularly the Non-Reactivity and Acting with Awareness facets are anticipated. These facets are most directly targeted by MBRP practices. Expected effect size: d = 0.60–0.80 (medium-to-large).
  4. Between-Group Differences: Significant Group × Time interaction effects are expected on all outcome variables, with the MBRP group showing greater improvement than TAU.
  5. Mechanism Evidence: Changes in mindfulness (FFMQ) are expected to significantly correlate with changes in craving (negative correlation) and impulsivity (negative correlation), supporting mindfulness as the active mechanism.
  6. Moderation Finding: Patients with higher baseline impulsivity may paradoxically show greater benefit from MBRP, as they have more room for improvement and the intervention directly targets their core deficit.

13.2 Clinical Interpretation

If confirmed, these findings would support the following clinical recommendations:

  • Brief MBRP (6 sessions) should be integrated as a standard adjunct in addiction treatment programs, particularly for patients with high craving and impulsivity profiles
  • Mindfulness skills training should begin during inpatient/residential stays to maximize accessible treatment time
  • Post-discharge maintenance mindfulness practice should be structured and supported (e.g., through mobile apps, booster sessions)
  • Treatment matching: patients with high impulsivity may be prioritized for MBRP as they stand to benefit most
  • The intervention can be feasibly delivered by trained clinical psychologists/counsellors within existing treatment infrastructure

14. Limitations

  1. Quasi-Experimental Design: The absence of true randomization introduces potential selection bias. Non-random assignment may result in group differences on unmeasured confounders despite baseline equivalence checks.
  2. No Long-Term Follow-Up: Post-intervention assessment only captures immediate effects. Without follow-up (e.g., 1-month, 3-month, 6-month), the durability of gains and actual relapse prevention effects cannot be determined.
  3. Self-Report Measures: Craving, impulsivity, and mindfulness are assessed via self-report, which is subject to social desirability bias (particularly in the MBRP group who may report improvement to please the facilitator), demand characteristics, and limited introspective accuracy.
  4. No Active Control: The TAU comparison does not control for non-specific factors (therapist attention, group cohesion, expectancy effects, time devoted to intervention). A time-matched active control (e.g., relaxation training, health education) would strengthen causal attribution.
  5. Heterogeneous Substance Types: Including patients with different primary substances may introduce variability, as craving mechanisms and impulsivity profiles differ across substances. Sample size may be insufficient for substance-specific subgroup analyses.
  6. Adherence and Dose: Home practice compliance is typically variable in addiction populations. Those who practice more may show greater benefits, confounding the group-level analysis. Intention-to-treat analysis partially addresses this.
  7. Facilitator Effects: A single facilitator delivering the intervention introduces potential therapist effects. Multi-site studies with multiple therapists would enhance generalizability.
  8. Trait vs. State Measurement: The BIS-11 measures trait impulsivity, which may be resistant to change within a 3-week intervention. Behavioural/performance-based impulsivity measures (stop-signal task, delay discounting) might be more sensitive to short-term change.
  9. Reactivity to Assessment: Repeated measurement of craving may itself produce reactivity (heightened craving awareness), potentially affecting both groups' scores.
  10. Generalizability: Single-site study limits generalizability; results may not extend to outpatient, community-based, or rural settings with different patient characteristics and treatment contexts.

15. Suggestions for Future Research

  1. Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) with Active Control: A fully randomized design comparing brief MBRP to an active control condition (e.g., progressive muscle relaxation, psychoeducation group of equivalent duration) would establish whether effects are specific to mindfulness or attributable to non-specific therapeutic factors.
  2. Longitudinal Follow-Up: Extending assessment to 1-month, 3-month, and 6-month follow-ups to determine durability of craving/impulsivity changes and actual relapse prevention efficacy (rates of return to substance use).
  3. Neuroimaging Integration: Incorporating fMRI (cue-reactivity paradigms) or EEG measures pre- and post-MBRP to examine neural correlates of mindfulness-based craving reduction, providing neurobiological evidence for intervention mechanisms.
  4. Dose-Response Studies: Systematically comparing different brief MBRP durations (4, 6, 8 sessions) to identify optimal minimal dose for clinical significance across different outcome variables.
  5. Digital/App-Based MBRP: Developing and testing smartphone-delivered brief MBRP for post-discharge maintenance, addressing the challenge of sustaining mindfulness practice after leaving structured treatment environments.
  6. Substance-Specific Analyses: Conducting larger studies with sufficient power for substance-specific subgroup analyses (alcohol, opioid, cannabis, stimulant) to determine whether MBRP is equally effective across substance types or whether modification is needed.
  7. Combination with Pharmacotherapy: Examining whether brief MBRP shows synergistic effects when combined with anti-craving medications (naltrexone, acamprosate, varenicline) versus either alone.
  8. Behavioural Impulsivity Measures: Including laboratory-based impulsivity tasks (Go/No-Go, Stop-Signal Task, Delay Discounting) alongside self-report to capture more objective, state-sensitive aspects of impulsive behaviour.
  9. Mediator and Moderator Analysis: With larger samples, conducting formal mediation (does mindfulness change mediate craving/impulsivity change?) and moderation (who benefits most from MBRP? — by baseline severity, substance type, personality, motivation) analyses.
  10. Qualitative Component: Adding qualitative interviews to understand participants' subjective experience of the intervention, barriers to practice, and perceived mechanisms of change.

16. Making the Research Unique: Innovative Approaches

The following practical suggestions can elevate this MPhil dissertation from a standard intervention study to a more innovative and impactful research contribution:

1. Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) of Craving

Approach: Supplement pre-post questionnaires with daily or twice-daily craving assessments via brief phone calls or text message responses during the 3-week intervention period. This provides granular, real-time data on craving trajectories and their relationship to mindfulness practice on the same day.

Feasibility: High — most patients have access to mobile phones. Even simple text-based ratings (0–10 craving scale) provide valuable repeated-measures data. Adds minimal participant burden (30 seconds per assessment).

2. Inclusion of a Behavioural Measure of Impulsivity

Approach: Add a brief computerized Go/No-Go task or Delay Discounting task alongside the BIS-11 self-report. This captures the behavioural (performance-based) dimension of impulsivity, which may be more sensitive to short-term mindfulness effects and less susceptible to self-report bias.

Feasibility: Moderate — requires a laptop/tablet and task software (freely available, e.g., PsyToolkit, PEBL). Administration takes 10–15 minutes. Provides stronger evidence for genuine impulsivity change versus reporting bias.

3. Cue-Exposure Craving Paradigm

Approach: Before and after the intervention, expose participants to substance-related cues (images/olfactory cues) and measure craving reactivity. This tests whether MBRP changes the craving response to triggers (the core relapse mechanism) rather than only baseline craving levels.

Feasibility: Moderate — requires standardized substance cue images (International Affective Picture System or custom sets), controlled presentation, and VAS craving measurement. Adds 15–20 minutes to each assessment. Provides mechanistically informative data aligned with incentive sensitization theory.

4. Heart Rate Variability (HRV) as a Physiological Marker

Approach: Measure resting HRV (using a simple pulse oximeter or chest strap) pre- and post-intervention. HRV indexes parasympathetic/vagal tone and is associated with self-regulatory capacity. Mindfulness interventions have been shown to increase HRV, and low HRV predicts relapse in substance users.

Feasibility: Moderate — basic HRV measurement requires affordable wearable devices (cost: ₹2000–5000) and free analysis software (Kubios HRV). Provides an objective, physiological complement to self-report measures. Strengthens the evidence for genuine psychophysiological change.

5. Integration of Self-Compassion as an Additional Variable

Approach: Add the Self-Compassion Scale – Short Form (SCS-SF; 12 items, 5 minutes) as an additional outcome/mechanism variable. Self-compassion is theoretically linked to mindfulness and may be particularly relevant in addiction recovery (reducing shame, self-criticism, and the "abstinence violation effect" that precipitates full relapse after a lapse).

Feasibility: High — brief, validated self-report measure with minimal additional burden. Self-compassion is an emerging construct in addiction research and its inclusion would add theoretical depth and clinical relevance.

6. Comparison Across Motivation Stages

Approach: Assess readiness to change (using the Stages of Change Readiness and Treatment Eagerness Scale — SOCRATES, or University of Rhode Island Change Assessment — URICA) and examine whether motivational stage moderates MBRP response. This addresses the clinically important question: "Who is ready to benefit from mindfulness-based intervention?"

Feasibility: High — SOCRATES is a brief (19-item) validated measure. Moderation analysis with baseline motivation provides clinically actionable treatment-matching information without requiring additional intervention time.

7. Culturally Adapted MBRP with Indian Contemplative Elements

Approach: Develop a culturally adapted version of brief MBRP that integrates familiar Indian contemplative practices (pranayama awareness, body scan drawing from yoga nidra traditions, use of culturally resonant metaphors) while maintaining MBRP's core principles. Compare this adapted version with standard MBRP to test whether cultural adaptation enhances engagement and outcomes.

Feasibility: Moderate-to-High — requires initial adaptation work with expert consultation (clinical psychologists, yoga practitioners, cultural advisors). However, the adapted protocol serves the same session structure. This unique Indian contribution would be highly publishable and clinically innovative.
