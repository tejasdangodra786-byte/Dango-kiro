How to read these notes

These notes are written so you can reproduce them in an exam. Each numbered section is one therapy, written in the order you would actually write the answer.

Every topic follows the same simple flow: definition → main focus → key concepts → goals → techniques → process/stages → indications → current status. The weight is on what the therapy does and how, not on dates and history.

  • A one-line summary opens each topic so you know the core idea instantly.
  • An SVG flowchart shows the model at a glance.
  • A definition box gives a clean opening line.
  • A ⭐ Must-write points box lists what the answer cannot do without.
  • A How to remember & write box gives a practical writing strategy.
  • A mnemonic gives a memory hook.

Hard words are explained in plain English in a coloured bracket right after the term, like this: transference (the client unconsciously reacts to the therapist as if they were someone from their past).

Evidence honestyLabels you will see: Law binding rule, Code professional code, Guide guideline, Theory theoretical view, Eg example, Sec secondary source. No authors, results, or numbers are invented.

IntroIntroduction to affective psychotherapy

"Affective" means to do with feelings and emotion. Affective psychotherapies are the talking therapies that work mainly through emotion, the therapy relationship, inner experience and insight, rather than only through changing outward behaviour.

In Unit IV, fourteen approaches are grouped together under this one heading. They look very different on the surface — one uses free association on a couch, another uses an empty chair, another uses yoga — but they share a family resemblance. They all believe that lasting change comes from the inside: from feeling things more clearly, understanding yourself better, and meeting a genuine, caring human relationship in the therapy room.

Why these fourteen are grouped together

They are not grouped by one founder or one technique. They are grouped by their shared spirit and target. Each one treats the person's emotional and inner life as the main thing that needs to shift. Even the very practical ones (reality therapy, supportive therapy) and the body-based ones (yoga, tai chi) still work by changing how a person feels, relates and experiences their life.

What they broadly share

The common thread
  • Feelings matter first. Emotion is treated as meaningful information, not just a symptom to remove.
  • The relationship heals. A warm, genuine, accepting therapist is seen as a major agent of change, not just a technician.
  • Insight and awareness. Many aim to help the person understand or become aware of something they could not see before.
  • Experience over lecturing. Change happens by experiencing something in the room (a feeling, a role, a relationship), not just by being told facts.
  • The whole person. They look at the person as a whole human being with a past, values and relationships — not only at the problem behaviour.

Contrast this with purely behavioural or cognitive approaches, which lead with changing actions and thoughts. Affective therapies lead with feeling, meaning and relationship, and trust that behaviour and thinking will then follow. Keep this contrast ready — it is a clean way to open almost any Unit IV answer.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • "Affective" = relating to emotion; these therapies work through feeling, relationship, insight and experience.
  • Grouped by a shared spirit, not one founder or technique.
  • Shared features: feelings first, healing relationship, insight/awareness, change through experience, whole person.
  • Contrast with behavioural/cognitive therapies, which lead with action and thought.
How to remember & write this answer

Open with the one-line meaning of "affective" (= emotion). Then give the shared-features list — the five bullets above are enough. Close with the contrast against behavioural/cognitive therapy. Remember it with FRIEW: Feelings first, Relationship heals, Insight, Experience, Whole person.

1Psychodynamic psychotherapy

One line: A modern, briefer descendant of Freud's psychoanalysis. It assumes our symptoms carry hidden meaning, and it uses the therapy relationship, free talking and interpretation to make the unconscious conscious so that change lasts.

DefinitionTherapy derived from psychoanalysis that focuses on unconscious processes showing up in present behaviour, and aims at insight into how the past shapes the present (paraphrased, APA Dictionary). Founder (one line): Freud, who began with Breuer's "talking cure" and moved to free association plus interpretation.

Main focus / core idea

The core belief is psychic determinism (nothing in the mind is random — every slip, dream or symptom has a hidden cause). A symptom is a compromise formation (a middle solution that partly satisfies a forbidden wish and partly defends against it). So the therapist does not just remove the symptom; they listen for the hidden wish and conflict behind it.

Key concepts (explained)

  • The unconscious. Most of the mind is out of awareness. Topographic view (mind split into conscious, preconscious and unconscious) and structural view (id = raw wishes/pleasure, ego = reality and reason, superego = conscience).
  • Psychosexual stages. Oral, anal, phallic, latency, genital. Fixation (getting emotionally stuck at an early stage) leaves its mark on the adult personality.
  • Defence mechanisms. The ego protects itself from anxiety using tricks like repression (pushing a thought out of awareness), denial, projection, displacement and sublimation (channelling an urge into something socially useful).
  • Transference. Transference (the client reacts to the therapist as if they were a parent or old figure from the past) — this is treated as gold, because it shows the old pattern live in the room.

Goals

Make the unconscious conscious; build real insight; strengthen the ego; replace crude defences with mature ones; and produce lasting change, not just quick symptom removal. Freud's famous aim: "where id was, there ego shall be."

Techniques

  • Free association — say whatever comes to mind, uncensored.
  • Dream analysis — read the manifest content (the dream as remembered) for the latent content (its hidden meaning); dreams are "the royal road to the unconscious".
  • Interpretation — a tactful, well-timed explanation of the hidden meaning.
  • Analysis of resistance — noticing lateness, silence or intellectualising as the mind defending itself.
  • Analysis of transference — working with the old relationship pattern as it appears toward the therapist.

The therapist keeps a stance of neutrality, abstinence and anonymity — a "blank screen" — so the client's own patterns show up clearly.

Process / stages

  1. Opening: assessment, contract, building the alliance.
  2. Middle: transference and resistance emerge and are worked through (an insight is revisited again and again until it actually changes the person).
  3. Termination: planned, worked-on ending.

Indications & current status

Good for: clients with ego strength, psychological-mindedness and motivation; neurotic, personality and relationship problems. Avoid / adapt for: acute psychosis, high suicide risk or acute crisis (use supportive therapy instead). Research: substantial, lasting benefit; short-term psychodynamic therapy is supported for depression and anxiety, and specialised forms help borderline personality disorder. Long analysis is hard to study. Gains may even grow after therapy ends — the "sleeper effect".

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Symptom = meaningful compromise between a hidden wish, reality and conscience.
  • Three-part mind: id, ego, superego; most of it unconscious.
  • Four core techniques: free association, dream analysis, interpretation, analysis of resistance & transference.
  • Working through = repeating an insight until it truly changes the person.
  • Goal = insight + stronger ego + lasting change, not just symptom removal.
How to remember & write this answer

Open with the "symptoms are meaningful" idea and the three-part mind. Then list the techniques — this is where marks sit, so give the four big ones with a line each. Lead with transference and working through if time is short. Use FIDO-WT to recall the techniques in order, then finish with goals and indications.

Mnemonic
FIDO-WT

Free association · Interpretation · Dream analysis · analysis of Resistance & Transference · Working through · Termination. Aim: make the unconscious conscious.

2Brief psychotherapy

One line: A planned, time-limited therapy that works on one agreed focus with an active, directive therapist. The deadline itself boosts motivation. The goal is to relieve symptoms and restore functioning, not to overhaul the whole personality.

DefinitionA group of therapies sharing three features: a planned time limit, a single focus, and an active therapist. Roughly half of clients improve within about eight sessions. Models include Malan (focal), Sifneos (short-term anxiety-provoking), Mann (time-limited), Davanloo (intensive short-term dynamic), plus IPT and solution-focused therapy.

Main focus / core idea

The time limit is itself the treatment. Knowing there is a deadline concentrates the work, mobilises motivation, and guards against drifting into dependency. Everything stays tied to one agreed theme, and the therapist stays active, directive and selective.

Key concepts (explained)

  • Therapeutic time limit. A set number of sessions pushes client and therapist to get to work quickly.
  • Focal formulation. Focal formulation (picking one central theme — a recurring relationship pattern, a current loss, or an unresolved conflict — and sticking to it).
  • IPT's four problem areas. Grief, role disputes, role transitions, and interpersonal deficits — the therapist chooses one as the focus.
  • Small change spreads. The optimistic assumption that one small shift sets off larger changes.

Goals

Resolve the focal problem, relieve symptoms, restore day-to-day functioning, and teach useful skills.

Techniques

  • Focused interpretation and selective attention — follow the focus, gently ignore side-tracks.
  • Gentle challenge of defences and use of positive transference.
  • Solution-focused tools: the miracle question ("if you woke up and the problem was gone, what would be different?"), scaling questions, spotting exceptions (times the problem was absent), and complimenting strengths.

Process / stages

  1. Assessment & selection — Sifneos' criteria: a clear complaint, at least one meaningful past relationship, psychological-mindedness, motivation, and ability to tolerate anxiety. A contract of 6–20 sessions (often 12–16) with a fixed end date set at the start.
  2. Active middle phase on the focus, with a mid-point review.
  3. Explicit termination — discussed from day one; booster/follow-up possible.

Indications & current status

Good for: acute or focal problems, adjustment disorders, mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety, grief, and crises in motivated clients. Avoid: chronic multi-problem or severe high-risk cases. Research: strong for IPT and brief CBT; supportive for short-term psychodynamic and solution-focused work; often as effective as longer therapy for depression, anxiety and adjustment. Now standard in primary care, student counselling and telehealth.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Three shared features: time limit, single focus, active therapist.
  • The time limit is therapeutic — it mobilises motivation.
  • Careful patient selection (Sifneos' criteria).
  • Fixed end date set at the start; termination discussed from session one.
  • Aim = relieve symptoms and restore functioning, not full personality change.
How to remember & write this answer

Open by naming the three shared features, then explain why the time limit helps. Give selection criteria and the fixed end date as the "procedure" marks. Use FAST to structure the whole answer: Focus, Active therapist, Selection, Time limit.

Mnemonic
FAST

Focus (one theme) · Active/directive therapist · Selection of suitable clients · Time limit set in advance.

3Humanistic psychotherapy

One line: The "third force" in psychology. It trusts that every person has an inborn drive to grow, centres on their own experience and free will, treats the whole person, and relies on a genuine human relationship — not technique — as the main agent of change.

DefinitionAn approach that trusts the person's capacity for growth, self-direction and self-actualisation, and treats the quality of the relationship as central (paraphrased, APA Dictionary). It arose as the "third force" (after psychoanalysis and behaviourism), led by Maslow, Rogers, May, Frankl and Perls.

Main focus / core idea

People are seen as basically good and growth-oriented. The engine is the actualising tendency (an inborn drive pushing every person toward growth and fulfilment). The therapist's job is to remove the blocks so this natural drive can flow again.

Key concepts (explained)

  • Subjective experience & free will. What matters is how the person sees their own world, here and now, and that they can choose and are responsible.
  • Holism. Treat the whole person, not a symptom or a label.
  • Conditions of worth. Conditions of worth (feeling valued only when you meet other people's standards) pull a person away from their own true experience and cause distress.

Goals

Build self-awareness, self-acceptance, authenticity, responsibility and growth — movement toward self-actualisation.

Techniques

Technique is secondary; the relationship is the main agent of change. The therapist offers genuine, accepting, empathic presence. Tools include empathic listening, reflection, unconditional acceptance, a non-directive (client-led) stance, and experiential work such as focusing on the bodily felt sense or chair work.

Process / stages

There are no fixed therapist-set stages — the process is client-led and unfolding. The therapist's genuine presence and congruence lower the client's defences so self-exploration deepens naturally.

Indications & current status

Good for: mild-to-moderate distress, identity and relationship problems, depression, anxiety, and people seeking personal growth. Avoid / adapt: severe psychosis or where structured skills are needed. Research: Elliott and colleagues' meta-analyses show large, stable gains, broadly equal to CBT. It lives on in emotion-focused therapy and motivational interviewing. Critics call it vague, hard to test, and over-optimistic.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • "Third force" reacting against determinism and mechanism.
  • Actualising tendency — inborn drive toward growth.
  • Distress = growth blocked by conditions of worth / losing touch with one's own experience.
  • Relationship, not technique, is the main agent of change.
  • Holism, free will, here-and-now, self-actualisation.
How to remember & write this answer

Open with "third force" and the actualising tendency — that one sentence frames everything. Then explain conditions of worth as the cause of distress, and stress that the relationship heals. Use GROWTH to lay out the points in order.

Mnemonic
GROWTH

Growth-oriented · Relationship as agent · Own actualising tendency · Whole person · Third force · Here-and-now.

4Existential psychotherapy

One line: It helps people face the unavoidable facts of being human — death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness — honestly, turning the anxiety this brings into a more authentic, responsible and meaningful life.

DefinitionTherapy built on four ultimate concerns (the unavoidable givens of existence — death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness — that naturally provoke anxiety). Frankl's logotherapy adds the will to meaning (the drive to find meaning, even in unavoidable suffering). Clinically developed by Binswanger, Boss, May, Frankl and Yalom.

Main focus / core idea

This anxiety cannot be "cured away" because it comes from simply being alive. The idea existence precedes essence (we are not born with a fixed nature; we create who we are through our choices) leads to the central aim: authenticity (living by your own values and owning your choices) instead of bad faith (hiding from freedom by pretending you have no choice).

Key concepts (explained)

  • The four givens. Death, freedom (and its burden of responsibility), isolation, and meaninglessness.
  • Existential vacuum. Existential vacuum (an inner emptiness and boredom from having lost a sense of meaning), which Frankl called a "noogenic neurosis".
  • Being-in-the-world. Heidegger's idea that a person can only be understood together with their world, not in isolation.

Goals

Help the person face life's givens honestly; increase awareness, responsibility, authenticity and meaning; and be able to act even while feeling anxious.

Techniques

Mostly relational and exploratory: phenomenological exploration of the client's worldview, bracketing assumptions, Socratic dialogue, using the here-and-now relationship, and "I" statements. Logotherapy adds three named techniques:

  • Paradoxical intention — deliberately try to do the very thing you fear (loosens the grip of anticipatory anxiety).
  • Dereflection — shift attention away from yourself and onto a meaningful activity.
  • Attitudinal modification — find meaning in suffering that cannot be changed.

Process / stages

Collaborative and exploratory rather than technique-driven: build a genuine relationship and explore the worldview → identify avoidance and inauthenticity → deepen awareness of freedom and responsibility → committed action.

Indications & current status

Good for: existential crises (life transitions, midlife, bereavement), terminal or chronic illness, and depression marked by emptiness. Avoid: acute psychosis or when quick symptom control is wanted. Research: moderate; meaning-centred interventions improve meaning, mood and quality of life, especially in cancer care. The concepts are abstract and hard to measure.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Four ultimate concerns: death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness.
  • Existence precedes essence → authenticity vs bad faith.
  • Psychopathology = inauthentic living + loss of meaning (existential vacuum).
  • Frankl's three logotherapy techniques: paradoxical intention, dereflection, attitudinal modification.
  • Goal = face the givens, live authentically and meaningfully, act despite anxiety.
How to remember & write this answer

Open by listing the four givens (use DFIM). State that the anxiety is normal and cannot be cured away. Lead the technique section with Frankl's three named techniques — examiners look for those. Close with meaning and authenticity as the goal.

Mnemonic
DFIM

Death · Freedom · Isolation · Meaninglessness — the four ultimate concerns.

5Gestalt therapy

One line: An experiential, here-and-now therapy (Fritz and Laura Perls) in which awareness itself is the cure. The therapist sets up experiments instead of giving interpretations, so the client can finish old unfinished business and reown the split-off parts of themselves to become whole.

DefinitionAn experiential therapy focused on present experience and awareness, integrating feelings, perceptions and actions into a whole person (paraphrased, APA Dictionary). Its paradoxical theory of change (you change when you fully accept what you are, not when you try to force yourself to be something else) is central.

Main focus / core idea

Work with the what and how of present experience, not the why of the past. Raising awareness is itself healing. The person and their environment form one field (a single whole, so a person can only be understood together with their situation).

Key concepts (explained)

  • Figure & ground. The strongest present need stands out as the "figure" against the background; a healthy person naturally self-regulates like this.
  • Unfinished business. Unfinished business (unexpressed feelings from the past that keep intruding on the present).
  • Interruptions to contact. Ways people block healthy contact: introjection (swallowing others' rules whole), projection, retroflection (turning back onto yourself what you want to do to others), deflection, confluence.
  • Top-dog vs under-dog. The inner battle between demanding "shoulds" and the resisting, wanting side.

Goals

Increase awareness, take responsibility, integrate split-off parts, finish unfinished business, and move from leaning on the environment to self-support.

Techniques

  • Empty-chair work — speak to an imagined person or a part of yourself placed in an empty chair.
  • Exaggeration and reversal — amplify a gesture, or try the opposite.
  • Staying with the feeling — don't run from a difficult emotion.
  • "I" language — say "I" not "it/you", and "I won't" not "I can't" (owning responsibility).
  • Dream work — act out each part of a dream as a disowned piece of yourself.

Process / stages

Build contact and an honest I-Thou relationship (a genuine, equal, present-moment meeting between two people) → awareness work and experiments → integration and closure. The therapist is authentic, present and non-interpretative, offering experiments and honest feedback.

Indications & current status

Good for: anxiety, depression, interpersonal and psychosomatic problems, and trauma-related unfinished business. Avoid / adapt: fragile or psychotic clients, who can be overwhelmed by confrontational experiments. Research: limited but growing; strongest through emotion-focused therapy, where Greenberg has shown empty-chair work for unfinished business is effective.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Here-and-now, awareness is curative, the "what/how" not the "why".
  • Paradoxical theory of change — accept what you are to change.
  • Unfinished business and interruptions to contact.
  • Empty-chair technique and "I" language.
  • Goal = awareness, responsibility, integration, self-support.
How to remember & write this answer

Open with "awareness is the cure" and the here-and-now focus. Then explain unfinished business, name the empty-chair technique (the signature of Gestalt), and the paradoxical theory of change. Use AWARE to march through the points.

Mnemonic
AWARE

Awareness curative · What-and-how not why · Appropriate experiments / empty chair · Responsibility & "I" language · Ending unfinished business.

6Person-centred (client-centred) therapy

One line: Carl Rogers' non-directive approach. When the therapist offers three core conditions — genuineness, unconditional positive regard and empathy — the client's own growth drive is freed, and the client (the real expert on themselves) directs their own change.

DefinitionA non-directive therapy in which the therapist's genuineness, unconditional positive regard and empathic understanding foster the client's growth (paraphrased, APA Dictionary). Rogers set out six "necessary and sufficient" conditions for change, grounded in the actualising tendency.

Main focus / core idea

Every person has a single inbuilt actualising tendency (a drive toward growth and fulfilment). Problems come from incongruence (a gap between the self-concept and actual experience, or between the real self and the ideal self), usually caused by conditions of worth (feeling valued only when meeting other people's standards). Restoring congruence restores health.

Key concepts (explained) — the three core conditions

  • Congruence — the therapist is genuine and integrated, not playing a role.
  • Unconditional positive regard — the therapist accepts and prizes the client with no conditions attached.
  • Empathy — the therapist accurately understands the client's inner world and reflects it back.

Alongside these sit the organismic valuing process (a healthy person trusts their own direct experience to judge what is good for them) and the aim of becoming a fully functioning person, open to experience.

Goals

Greater congruence, self-acceptance, an internal locus of evaluation (judging by your own standards rather than others'), less anxiety and defensiveness, and becoming fully functioning.

Techniques

Strictly non-directive — no diagnosing, interpreting, advising or reassuring. The client is trusted as the expert. The main skills are active/reflective listening, reflection of feeling, paraphrasing, clarification and allowing silences — all inside a genuine relationship.

Process / stages

Rogers described a seven-stage continuum: the client moves from being rigid and remote from their feelings toward experiencing feelings fluidly, immediately and with trust. As defences lower, self-exploration deepens and change becomes self-directed.

Indications & current status

Good for: a wide range — mild-to-moderate emotional, identity and relationship problems, grief, adolescents, and student counselling. Avoid / adapt: clients who want direct advice, severe disorders, or acute crises. Research: Rogers pioneered the recording and study of sessions; Elliott and colleagues find empathy, positive regard and congruence correlate modestly but reliably with good outcomes. Critics question whether the conditions are truly "sufficient".

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Three core conditions: congruence, unconditional positive regard, empathy.
  • Actualising tendency freed by the right relationship.
  • Problem = incongruence from conditions of worth.
  • Strictly non-directive; client is the expert.
  • Goal = congruence + internal locus of evaluation + fully functioning person.
How to remember & write this answer

The whole answer hangs on the three core conditions — define each in one line and you have the heart of it (CUE). Then explain incongruence as the cause and the actualising tendency as the engine. Stress "non-directive, client is the expert".

Mnemonic
CUE

Congruence · Unconditional positive regard · Empathy — the three core conditions.

7Adlerian therapy (Individual Psychology)

One line: Adler's approach sees the person as a whole, goal-directed and socially connected being. Problems are not illness but discouragement and mistaken beliefs about life. Therapy re-educates the client toward courage, social interest and useful contribution.

DefinitionIndividual Psychology sees the person as an indivisible whole whose behaviour is goal-directed and socially shaped. The key measure of mental health is social interest (an inborn capacity and willingness to cooperate with and contribute to others). Founded by Adler; carried forward by Dreikurs, Mosak and Dinkmeyer.

Main focus / core idea

Behaviour is pulled toward a future goal — teleology (explaining behaviour by its goal rather than its cause) and fictional finalism (a self-created guiding goal we move toward). We all start with inferiority feelings (a sense of being small or not enough) that spur us to strive; this can be healthy ambition or a self-defeating complex.

Key concepts (explained)

  • Lifestyle. Lifestyle (the person's unique pattern of beliefs and goals, formed around age five from the family setup and birth order).
  • Private logic vs common sense. Private logic (idiosyncratic, mistaken beliefs that only make sense to that person — their "basic mistakes").
  • Social interest as the yardstick of mental health.
  • Mistaken goals. Dreikurs' four goals behind children's misbehaviour: attention, power, revenge, and display of inadequacy.

Goals

Increase social interest and a sense of belonging, correct mistaken beliefs, build courage and self-worth, and replace self-centred striving with useful contribution.

Techniques

  • Encouragement — the central technique, running throughout.
  • Acting "as if" — behave as though you already had the quality you want.
  • "Spitting in the client's soup" — gently expose the hidden payoff of a behaviour so it loses its appeal.
  • Catching oneself, the push-button technique, and homework tasks.

Process / stages (the four phases)

  1. Relationship — equal, respectful, collaborative.
  2. Lifestyle assessment — family constellation, birth order, and early recollections (the earliest memories, read as clues to current beliefs).
  3. Insight — gently and warmly disclose the mistaken beliefs and private logic.
  4. Reorientation — turn insight into action, driven by encouragement.

Indications & current status

Good for: a broad range — children and adolescents, parent education, family therapy, school and career counselling, groups, and collectivist cultures. Severe psychopathology needs adaptation. Research: limited but positive (Adlerian parent education/STEP, Adlerian play therapy and counselling). Adler's ideas anticipated cognitive, humanistic and family-systems therapies.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Whole person, goal-directed, socially embedded.
  • Inferiority feelings → striving; social interest = measure of health.
  • Lifestyle and private logic / basic mistakes.
  • The four phases: relationship → lifestyle assessment → insight → reorientation.
  • Encouragement is the engine; therapy is re-education, not cure of illness.
How to remember & write this answer

Frame it as "problems of living, not illness" from the start. The four phases (RAIR) are the backbone of the procedure section — write them in order. Keep saying "encouragement" and "social interest" — they are the two words that mark this answer as Adlerian.

Mnemonic
RAIR

Relationship · Assessment of lifestyle · Insight · Reorientation. Engine = encouragement toward social interest.

8Transactional analysis (TA)

One line: Eric Berne's approach. Personality is made of three ego states — Parent, Adult, Child. Problems come from contaminated ego states, repetitive "games" and a limiting life script. Using a clear contract, therapy restores autonomy and game-free intimacy.

DefinitionA therapy in which every transaction (a single stimulus-and-response exchange between two people) comes from one of three ego states. The aim is autonomy through awareness, spontaneity and intimacy. Founded by Berne; developed by Harris, Steiner, Karpman and the Gouldings.

Main focus / core idea

Three ego states (consistent patterns of feeling and behaviour): Parent (taught, judging), Adult (thinking, objective, here-and-now), and Child (feeling, spontaneous). Health means a free, uncontaminated Adult that can choose which state to use.

Key concepts (explained)

  • Transactions. Complementary (talk flows on), crossed (talk breaks off), and ulterior (a hidden message runs under the social one — this is how games start).
  • Strokes. Strokes (units of recognition or attention), which can be positive/negative and conditional/unconditional; people structure time to get them.
  • Games & rackets. Games (repetitive ulterior transactions that always end in a predictable bad feeling), e.g. "Why don't you — Yes but".
  • Life script. Life script (an unconscious life plan decided in childhood from parental messages), with injunctions ("don't be close") and drivers ("be perfect").
  • Four life positions (OK Corral): I'm OK–you're OK (healthy) → the three unhealthy ones (blaming, depressive, hopeless).
  • Drama triangle. Karpman's Persecutor / Rescuer / Victim roles, which people switch between.

Goals

Autonomy and game-free intimacy; a free, uncontaminated Adult; a revised script ("script cure"); social control and symptom relief.

Techniques

  • Four analyses: structural (ego states), transactional (exchanges), game (hidden payoffs), script (life plan).
  • The contract — a clear, mutual, adult-to-adult agreement on goals from session one.
  • Redecision work — re-experience and change early childhood decisions, often using chair work.
  • Egograms, stroke work, role play and homework, usually in groups.

Process / stages

Contracting and assessment → strengthen and decontaminate (clear out distortions from) the Adult and redecide the Child → relearn behaviours → autonomy and termination.

Indications & current status

Good for: neurotic, relationship and communication problems, personality issues, couples and families, groups, education and organisations. Avoid / adapt: acute psychosis (adapted forms only). Research: modest — some positive reviews but few rigorous trials. It is widely taught and accredited (ITAA/EATA), but criticised for jargon and limited validation. Its plain language means clients keep using it.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Three ego states: Parent, Adult, Child.
  • Transactions: complementary, crossed, ulterior (→ games).
  • Games, life script, life positions (I'm OK–you're OK).
  • The contract and the four analyses.
  • Goal = autonomy and game-free intimacy via a free Adult.
How to remember & write this answer

Draw the three stacked circles in your head and start there (PAC). Then move outward: transactions → games → script → life positions. Name "the contract" and "the four analyses" in the techniques section. Finish with autonomy as the goal.

Mnemonic
PAC

Parent · Adult · Child. The contract + four analyses free the Adult toward autonomy.

9Reality therapy (and choice theory)

One line: William Glasser's present-focused approach, built on choice theory. All behaviour is chosen to meet five basic needs. Using the WDEP system inside a warm, non-blaming relationship, the therapist helps the client evaluate their choices and plan more effective, responsible ones.

DefinitionA present-focused therapy grounded in choice theory (we choose almost all of our behaviour to meet basic needs, and we can only directly control our own acting and thinking). Most problems are seen as relationship problems. Founded by Glasser; the WDEP system was systematised by Wubbolding.

Main focus / core idea

All behaviour is chosen. We directly control only our acting and thinking, and these steer our feelings and physiology — Glasser's "four wheels of a car". We behave to close the gap between what we want (our quality world (the inner picture album of what we most want)) and what we are getting.

Key concepts (explained)

  • Five basic needs: survival, love & belonging (most important), power, freedom, fun.
  • Relationships are central. The seven "deadly habits" (criticising, blaming, complaining...) damage relationships; the seven "caring habits" (supporting, listening, respecting...) build them.
  • Responsibility + present focus. Meet your needs without stopping others; no excuses, blame or punishment; little attention to the past or to symptoms.

Goals

Take responsibility, make effective choices, improve relationships, and meet needs in ways that are realistic, responsible and right (the "three Rs").

Techniques — the WDEP system

  • W — Wants: what do you want and need?
  • D — Doing: what are you actually doing, and in what direction?
  • E — Evaluation: the client's own honest self-evaluation — "Is what you are doing helping? Is it realistic?"
  • P — Planning: a committed, workable plan — SAMI2C3 (simple, attainable, measurable, immediate, involving, controlled, committed, consistent).

Plus questioning, metaphor, humour, paradox, role play, contracts and self-disclosure — and firmly no excuses.

Process / stages

Build the environment first (a trusting, friendly bond; good listening; no arguing or criticising) → Wants → Doing → self-Evaluation → Plan and commitment → follow-up and revise.

Indications & current status

Good for: schools, adolescents, offenders/corrections, addictions, marital and group counselling, and workplaces. Caution: severe mental illness or trauma needing deeper exploration. Research: moderate but often quasi-experimental; gains in self-esteem, internal locus of control and relationships across school, correctional and cross-cultural settings. Critics attack its rejection of diagnosis and its simple view of choice.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Built on choice theory — behaviour is chosen; we control acting and thinking.
  • Five basic needs (love & belonging most important).
  • WDEP system + build the environment first.
  • Client's own self-evaluation: "Is what you are doing helping?"
  • Present-focused, responsibility, no excuses/blame/punishment.
How to remember & write this answer

Open with "all behaviour is chosen to meet five needs". The whole procedure/technique section is WDEP — write the four letters out with a line each and you have most of the marks. Remember to say "build the environment first" and "no excuses".

Mnemonic
WDEP

Wants · Doing · Evaluation · Planning — inside a warm, non-blaming environment.

10Supportive psychotherapy

One line: The ego-strengthening end of the supportive–expressive continuum. Instead of uncovering the unconscious, it works to maintain and restore ego functions, self-esteem and coping, through a warm, active relationship using reassurance, advice, praise and problem-solving.

DefinitionTherapy at the supportive end of Luborsky's supportive–expressive continuum. It aims to maintain or improve ego functions and self-esteem rather than uncover conflict. The relationship is the main agent of change, with the therapist acting as a trusted auxiliary ego (a dependable figure who lends the ego strength the client temporarily lacks).

Main focus / core idea

The aim is shifted: not to make the unconscious conscious, but to shore up existing defences and coping, keep the person functioning, and protect self-esteem. Focus stays on the here-and-now — present stresses, coping and daily life — not childhood reconstruction. Positive transference is used but not interpreted, and deep interpretation is avoided.

Key concepts (explained)

  • Ego functions. The mind's everyday skills (reality testing, impulse control, judgement) that supportive therapy aims to protect and build.
  • Auxiliary ego. The therapist temporarily "lends" strength the client cannot yet supply.
  • Strengthen, don't uncover. Adaptive defences are reinforced, not dismantled.

Goals

Symptom relief and stabilisation, better coping and adaptation, support through crisis or illness, prevention of relapse and rehospitalisation, and higher self-esteem.

Techniques

  • Reassurance, encouragement and praise.
  • Empathic listening and ventilation — letting the client talk out and release pent-up feelings.
  • Advice and guidance, psychoeducation, clarification and anticipatory guidance.
  • Problem-solving and reality testing, reframing, and environmental modification (involving family, arranging services).
  • Modelling, limit-setting, strengthening adaptive defences, and crisis intervention.

Process / stages

Assess strengths and ego capacities → build the alliance and define problems/goals → apply supportive techniques → monitor, then either gradually reduce or continue as ongoing maintenance. Frequency and length are flexible (weekly to monthly; brief to long-term) and the ending is open-ended.

Indications & current status

Good for: schizophrenia and severe mental illness, bipolar disorder, personality disorders with low ego strength, crises and bereavement, chronic or terminal medical illness, the elderly, and clients with low insight. Research: Wallerstein's Menninger study found more lasting change than expected; later work shows it is comparable to other therapies. It is under-researched and often used only as a control condition; critics warn it can foster dependency.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Supportive end of the supportive–expressive continuum.
  • Aim = maintain/restore ego functions and self-esteem, not uncover conflict.
  • Relationship heals; therapist = auxiliary ego.
  • Techniques: reassurance, ventilation, advice, problem-solving, reframing.
  • Best for low ego strength — severe illness, crisis, medical illness, elderly.
How to remember & write this answer

Define it against psychodynamic therapy: "strengthen, don't uncover". Draw the continuum line in your mind. The techniques are the easy marks — list them (SUPPORT). Finish with the indications, since knowing who it suits is heavily tested.

Mnemonic
SUPPORT

Strengthen ego · Use the relationship · Praise/reassure · Problem-solve · Offer advice/psychoeducation · Reality-test/Reframe · Tackle the crisis.

11Clinical hypnotherapy

One line: It uses hypnosis — focused attention plus a heightened readiness to respond to suggestion — to change perception, emotion and behaviour. It moves through five set stages and is used for pain, anxiety and habits, usually as an add-on to other treatment.

DefinitionHypnosis is a state of focused attention with reduced side-awareness and a heightened response to suggestion (paraphrased, APA Division 30). It is not sleep and not a loss of control. Erickson's style was permissive and indirect, using the client's own imagery and language.

Main focus / core idea

In the hypnotic state there is absorption (being deeply caught up in an experience), focused attention, suggestibility and reduced critical analysis. Symptoms, habits, pain and anxiety are seen as kept going by automatic learned responses and negative imagery, which focused attention plus suggestion can change. A key idea is hypnotisability (a fairly stable trait of how easily a person responds, measured by the Stanford/Harvard scales).

Key concepts (explained)

  • State vs non-state debate. Is hypnosis a special altered state (Hilgard's neodissociation (part of the mind splits off and acts separately)) or just ordinary role-taking, expectancy and imagination? Neuroimaging shows real changes in attention networks.
  • Suggestion. The main active ingredient — ideas offered that the client's mind then acts on.

Goals

Symptom control (especially pain and anxiety), habit change, relaxation, greater self-efficacy and ego strength, and teaching the client self-hypnosis.

Techniques (across the stages)

  • Pre-induction: consent, history, explaining hypnosis and dispelling myths (no loss of control, no secrets revealed), building expectancy and rapport.
  • Induction: eye fixation, progressive relaxation, breathing, imagery; Ericksonian confusion and storytelling; pacing and leading.
  • Deepening: counting down, staircase imagery, fractionation (repeatedly bringing the client out of trance and back in to deepen it).
  • Therapeutic phase: direct and indirect suggestions, metaphors, ego-strengthening, imagery rehearsal, hypnotic analgesia (suggested numbness, e.g. "glove anaesthesia"); deeper work (age regression, hypnoanalysis (uncovering buried material under hypnosis), post-hypnotic suggestion) used cautiously.
  • Termination: re-alerting by counting up and reorienting, discussion, and teaching self-hypnosis.

Process / stages

A fixed sequence: pre-induction → induction → deepening → therapeutic phase → termination.

Indications & current status

Good for: chronic and procedural pain, labour and dental pain, anxiety and phobias, IBS, insomnia, habit disorders and psychosomatic problems. Cautions: psychosis, severe personality disorder, and the false-memory risk in forensic use. Research: adding hypnosis to CBT improves outcomes; strong evidence for pain, IBS and procedural distress; moderate for anxiety; weak/inconclusive for smoking and weight.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Hypnosis = focused attention + heightened suggestibility; NOT sleep, NOT loss of control.
  • The five stages: pre-induction, induction, deepening, therapeutic, termination.
  • State vs non-state debate; hypnotisability as a trait.
  • Teach self-hypnosis; usually an adjunct.
  • Strongest evidence for pain and IBS.
How to remember & write this answer

Correct the two myths up front ("not sleep, not loss of control") — it shows understanding. The stages (PIDTT) are the skeleton of the procedure/technique section; attach a technique or two to each stage. Close with where the evidence is strong (pain, IBS).

Mnemonic
PIDTT

Pre-induction · Induction · Deepening · Therapeutic phase · Termination.

12Play therapy

One line: Play is the child's natural language — "toys are their words and play is their speech". In a safe playroom with a trained therapist (directive or non-directive), children express and master emotions, trauma and relationship difficulties.

DefinitionPlay is the child's natural language — Landreth: "toys are the child's words and play is the child's speech". Axline set out the non-directive/child-centred form and eight guiding principles (Rogers' conditions, for children). Carried forward by Landreth (child-centred play therapy) and Knell (CBT play therapy).

Main focus / core idea

Young children cannot easily put abstract thoughts and feelings into words, so they express them through symbolic play instead. Play has specific therapeutic powers (the ways play actually heals — communication, emotional release, stronger relationships, and building personal strengths).

Key concepts (explained)

  • Symbolic distance. A child can face a scary feeling safely by acting it out through toys rather than talking about it directly.
  • Axline's eight principles. A warm relationship, accept the child as they are, reflect feelings, and respect the child's own capacity to solve problems.
  • Many theory bases. Psychoanalytic, humanistic/child-centred, attachment, cognitive-behavioural and developmental — leading to directive or non-directive styles.

Goals

Emotional expression and regulation, a better self-concept and self-esteem, social skills and coping, resolving trauma and conflict, and strengthening the parent–child attachment.

Techniques

  • Non-directive: tracking the play aloud, reflecting feelings, returning responsibility ("you can decide"), and encouragement that follows the child rather than steering.
  • Limit-setting via ACT: Acknowledge the feeling, Communicate the limit, Target an acceptable alternative.
  • Structured/directive: puppets, sandplay, drawing, storytelling (Gardner's mutual storytelling), bibliotherapy, role play, games, and CBT play with modelling and exposure.
  • Parent-mediated: filial therapy, child–parent relationship therapy (CPRT), and Theraplay (structure, engagement, nurture, challenge).

Process / stages

Assessment, parent consultation and consent → relationship building (the child explores and tests the therapist) → themes appear → working-through (aggression, regression, limit-testing and dramatic play give way to mastery) → termination, prepared early. Usually about 12–20 weekly sessions.

Indications & current status

Good for: children roughly 3–12 with anxiety and fears, trauma or abuse, bereavement, parental divorce, adjustment and behaviour problems, and selective mutism. Adolescents may need talk, art or drama instead. Research: Bratton and colleagues' (2005) meta-analysis of 93 studies found a large mean effect, larger when parents took part; later child-centred play therapy reviews show moderate-to-large effects. Critics note methodological weaknesses.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Play is the child's natural language ("toys are words, play is speech").
  • Directive vs non-directive (child-centred) styles.
  • Axline's eight principles; therapeutic powers of play.
  • Limit-setting via ACT; parent-mediated forms (filial therapy).
  • Best for ages ~3–12; strong evidence, stronger when parents are involved.
How to remember & write this answer

Open with the Landreth quote — it is the single best opening line for this topic. Then split techniques into non-directive, directive and parent-mediated. Name the ACT limit-setting steps. Use TRACK to order the whole answer and don't forget the age range.

Mnemonic
TRACK

Toys are words · Relationship first · Accept the child · Child-centred/non-directive · Keep safe limits (via ACT).

13Psychodrama

One line: J. L. Moreno's action-based group therapy. The client (the "protagonist") enacts conflicts instead of just describing them, using a director, auxiliary egos, an audience and a stage. Through warm-up, action and sharing, spontaneity and new roles are restored.

DefinitionA group method in which the protagonist acts out their issue on a stage. Moreno coined "group psychotherapy" and developed sociometry (measuring the relationships within a group). For Moreno, spontaneity (a fresh, adequate response to a situation, new or old) plus creativity are the basis of mental health.

Main focus / core idea

Distress comes from a loss of spontaneity and rigid, stuck roles. By enacting a scene in the here-and-now, old conflicts and relationships are brought into the open where they can be experienced and changed. Change comes through doing, not just talking.

Key concepts (explained)

  • Role theory. We take, play and create roles; problems arise from role conflict or role rigidity.
  • Tele. Tele (a genuine two-way flow of feeling and understanding between people — mutual, unlike one-way transference).
  • Surplus reality. Surplus reality (enacting what never happened, or what is feared or wished for, as if it were real), plus catharsis and encounter.

Goals

Catharsis and release, insight, a wider role repertoire, renewed spontaneity, better interpersonal skills, resolving unfinished business, and group cohesion and support.

Techniques

  • Role reversal — play the other person to see through their eyes (the most powerful technique).
  • Doubling — an auxiliary voices the protagonist's unspoken feelings.
  • Mirroring — the protagonist watches an auxiliary portray them, from the outside.
  • Soliloquy, empty chair, future projection, concretisation, sculpting, social atom, magic shop, and sociodrama (action methods on shared social issues).

Process / stages — the five instruments across three phases

The five instruments are the protagonist (whose issue is explored), the director (who guides), auxiliary egos (who play significant others), the audience (who witness) and the stage. The three phases:

  1. Warm-up — build trust; a protagonist emerges.
  2. Action — set and enact the scene (often moving present → past), using role reversal, doubling and mirroring to a peak, then try new solutions and close.
  3. Sharing — the audience share their own similar experiences (not analysis or advice), which reduces isolation and supports re-entry.

Indications & current status

Good for: group settings — trauma, grief, addictions, family and relationship issues, adolescents, social-skills training, and psychiatric inpatients. Caution: acute psychosis or a very fragile client without containment; it needs a skilled director. Research: Kipper and Ritchie's (2003) meta-analysis found a large effect, with role reversal and doubling the most effective; later reviews are moderately positive, though rigorous RCTs are few.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Action-based group therapy; enact, don't just describe.
  • Spontaneity + creativity = basis of mental health.
  • The five instruments: protagonist, director, auxiliary egos, audience, stage.
  • Three phases: warm-up, action, sharing.
  • Role reversal is the most powerful technique; also doubling and mirroring.
How to remember & write this answer

Lead with "enact, don't describe" and "spontaneity is health". List the five instruments (PDAAS), then the three phases in order. In techniques, put role reversal first and label it the most powerful. Mention sharing is support, not advice.

Mnemonic
PDAAS

Protagonist · Director · Auxiliary egos · Audience · Stage. Flow: warm-up → action → sharing; role reversal is most powerful.

14Oriental approaches

One line: Asian mind-body practices used for mental health — yoga, meditation, shavasana, pranic healing, reiki and tai chi. They share a focus on attention, breath and relaxation. The evidence ranges from genuinely supportive (mindfulness, yoga, tai chi) down to weak or inconclusive (energy healing).

DefinitionA set of Asian mind-body and healing practices. Clinical mindfulness (non-judgemental, present-moment awareness) was brought into medicine by Kabat-Zinn (MBSR). Energy healing (pranic healing, reiki — clearing or channelling a supposed life energy) has no credible scientific evidence.

Main focus / core idea

Despite their differences, these practices share a common mechanism: training attention, slowing and deepening the breath, and switching on the body's relaxation response. This improves self-regulation, raises parasympathetic (vagal) tone, lowers arousal and cortisol, and improves attention and emotion regulation.

Key practices (explained)

  • Yoga. Systematised by Patanjali as an eight-limbed discipline; "union" of body, mind and spirit. In therapy: gentle postures (asanas), pranayama (breath control) and relaxation. NIMHANS studies and meta-analyses show real benefit as an add-on for mild-to-moderate depression and anxiety.
  • Meditation. Adapted as MBSR (Kabat-Zinn) and MBCT (Segal, Williams, Teasdale). Works by training attention and decentring (seeing thoughts as passing mental events, not facts), which lowers rumination. MBCT is recommended to prevent relapse in recurrent depression; Goyal et al. (2014) found moderate evidence for anxiety, depression and pain.
  • Shavasana. The "corpse pose" final relaxation, and the related Yoga Nidra ("yogic sleep"). Triggers the relaxation response (lowered sympathetic arousal, heart rate, blood pressure and muscle tension), like Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation. Small trials show less anxiety and better sleep.
  • Pranic healing. (Choa Kok Sui) claims to scan, cleanse and energise a depleted energy body, usually without touch. Evidence is very limited and poorly controlled; any benefit is probably relaxation and expectancy. Must not replace treatment.
  • Reiki. (Usui) channels a "universal life energy" through the hands. Evidence is weak and at high risk of bias (a Cochrane review found insufficient evidence for anxiety/depression). A complementary comfort measure only; must not delay treatment.
  • Tai chi. "Meditation in motion" — slow flowing movements with breathing and focused attention. Evidence is reasonably good for reduced stress, anxiety and depression, as an add-on in late-life depression, and for fall prevention, chronic pain and cardiac rehab.
  • Others. Morita therapy (accept your feelings and do what needs doing) and Naikan (structured introspection to build gratitude), from Japan; plus qigong.

Goals, indications & current status

Mindfulness, yoga and tai chi have a real evidence base as add-ons for stress, anxiety, depression and chronic pain. Reiki and pranic healing are weak or inconclusive, with a real risk of delaying proper care. These practices fit Indian and Asian cultural beliefs, cost little and have few side effects, but research quality is variable — strongest for mindfulness-based therapies, weakest for energy healing.

⭐ Must-write points for the answer
  • Group them, then rank by evidence: strong (mindfulness, yoga, tai chi) vs weak (reiki, pranic healing).
  • Common mechanism: attention + breath + relaxation response → better self-regulation.
  • MBSR/MBCT; MBCT prevents depressive relapse.
  • Energy healing lacks credible evidence; must not replace treatment.
  • Mostly used as adjuncts; culturally fitting, low cost, few side effects.
How to remember & write this answer

This answer is really a list with a judgement. Name the practices (YMSPRT), then split them into "good evidence" and "weak evidence" — that split is the mark-scoring move. Give the common mechanism once, and add the honest caution about energy healing.

Mnemonic
YMSPRT

Yoga · Meditation · Shavasana · Pranic healing · Reiki · Tai chi.
