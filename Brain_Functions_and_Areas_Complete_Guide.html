1. Overview of Brain Organization

The human brain weighs approximately 1.4 kg (about 2% of body weight) yet consumes roughly 20% of the body's oxygen and glucose supply. It contains approximately 86 billion neurons and an equal number of non-neuronal (glial) cells. The brain, along with the spinal cord, forms the Central Nervous System (CNS).

Key Concept for Clinical Psychology MPhil

The principle of localization of function (specific brain areas mediate specific functions) operates alongside distributed processing (complex behaviors require networks working together). Modern neuropsychology recognizes both focal lesion effects AND network dysfunction in understanding clinical presentations. Understanding these principles helps in neuropsychological assessment, differential diagnosis, and treatment planning.

2. Frontal Lobe

The frontal lobe is the largest lobe, occupying approximately one-third of the total cortical surface. It extends from the frontal pole anteriorly to the central sulcus (Rolandic fissure) posteriorly and the lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure) inferiorly. It is the last brain region to fully myelinate, completing development around age 25. The frontal lobe is considered the "executive brain" and the seat of personality.

2.1 Primary Motor Cortex (Precentral Gyrus) - Brodmann Area 4

  • Located immediately anterior to the central sulcus
  • Contains the motor homunculus - topographic map of contralateral body movements
  • Initiates voluntary, skilled movements on the opposite side of the body
  • Somatotopic organization: legs at medial/top, face at lateral/bottom
  • Contains large Betz cells (giant pyramidal neurons in Layer V)
  • Sends axons via the corticospinal (pyramidal) tract to spinal motor neurons
  • Low threshold for electrical stimulation to produce movement

Clinical Relevance

Lesion: Contralateral hemiplegia/hemiparesis. Upper Motor Neuron (UMN) signs: spasticity, hyperreflexia, positive Babinski sign, clonus. Middle cerebral artery (MCA) stroke commonly affects the face and upper limb representation.

2.2 Premotor Cortex (PMC) - Brodmann Area 6 (Lateral)

  • Anterior to primary motor cortex on lateral surface
  • Plans and programs complex motor sequences
  • Prepares the motor system for upcoming movements based on external cues
  • Contains mirror neurons - fire when performing OR observing an action
  • Involved in imitation learning and understanding others' actions
  • Guides movements based on visual and sensory information

Clinical Relevance

Lesion: Ideomotor apraxia - inability to perform skilled movements on command despite intact motor and comprehension abilities. Difficulty with pantomiming (e.g., "show me how you use a hammer").

2.3 Supplementary Motor Area (SMA) - Brodmann Area 6 (Medial)

  • Located on medial surface of frontal lobe, anterior to leg area of motor cortex
  • Plans and initiates internally-generated voluntary movements
  • Coordinates bilateral (bimanual) movements
  • Sequences complex motor actions
  • Generates the "readiness potential" (Bereitschaftspotential) before movement
  • Important for speech initiation

Clinical Relevance

Lesion: Alien hand syndrome, transient mutism, akinesia (reduced spontaneous movement), difficulty with bimanual coordination. SMA syndrome following surgery presents with contralateral neglect and speech arrest.

2.4 Frontal Eye Fields (FEF) - Brodmann Area 8

  • Posterior middle frontal gyrus
  • Controls voluntary saccadic eye movements
  • Directs visual attention and visual search
  • Works with parietal eye fields for attention shifting

Clinical Relevance

Destructive Lesion: Eyes deviate TOWARD the lesion side (cannot look away). Irritative Lesion (Seizure): Eyes deviate AWAY from the lesion. Key in stroke laterality diagnosis.

2.5 Broca's Area - Brodmann Areas 44 & 45

  • Located in inferior frontal gyrus of dominant hemisphere (usually left)
  • BA 44 (Pars Opercularis): Motor speech programming, phonological processing, syntax production
  • BA 45 (Pars Triangularis): Semantic retrieval, verbal working memory, sentence comprehension
  • Verbal fluency, grammar, sentence construction
  • Also involved in music processing and working memory for sequences

Clinical Relevance - Broca's Aphasia (Expressive/Non-fluent Aphasia)

Patient understands language but cannot produce fluent speech. Speech is effortful, telegraphic, agrammatic, with short phrases. Comprehension relatively preserved. Writing impaired. Patient is aware of deficit (may be frustrated/depressed). Often co-occurs with right hemiparesis (proximity to motor cortex for face/arm).

2.6 Prefrontal Cortex (PFC) - Brodmann Areas 9, 10, 11, 12, 46, 47

(Detailed breakdown in Section 8)

  • Occupies the anterior pole of the frontal lobe - largest in humans vs other species
  • Last area to fully myelinate (~age 25)
  • Divided into: Dorsolateral PFC (DLPFC), Ventromedial PFC (vmPFC), Orbitofrontal Cortex (OFC), Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC)
  • Executive functions: Planning, decision-making, working memory, cognitive flexibility, inhibition
  • Social cognition: Theory of mind, empathy, moral reasoning
  • Personality: Social behavior, motivation, drive, initiative
  • Temporal organization: Organizing behavior over time

Disorders Associated with Frontal Lobe Damage

  • Dysexecutive Syndrome: Impaired planning, sequencing, abstraction, mental flexibility, poor judgment
  • Broca's Aphasia: Non-fluent, effortful, agrammatic speech with relatively preserved comprehension
  • Personality Change (Phineas Gage syndrome): Disinhibition, impulsivity, social inappropriateness (OFC damage)
  • Abulia/Apathy: Loss of will, motivation, spontaneity (medial frontal/ACC damage)
  • Akinetic Mutism: Extreme apathy - awake but no speech or movement (bilateral medial frontal)
  • Perseveration: Inability to switch responses or stop repetitive behaviors
  • Confabulation: Fabricating memories without conscious intent to deceive
  • Utilization Behavior: Compulsive use of objects in the environment
  • Imitation Behavior: Automatic mimicking of examiner's actions
  • Grasp Reflex (Primitive reflexes): Release of frontal inhibition reveals infantile reflexes
  • ADHD: Prefrontal hypofunction - inattention, impulsivity, hyperactivity
  • Schizophrenia: Hypofrontality - reduced prefrontal activation during cognitive tasks
  • Major Depression: Left DLPFC hypoactivation (target for repetitive TMS)
  • Antisocial Personality Disorder: Reduced OFC/vmPFC function - poor moral reasoning
  • OCD: Hyperactivity in OFC and ACC - excessive error monitoring
  • Frontotemporal Dementia (bvFTD): Progressive personality and behavioral changes

3. Parietal Lobe

The parietal lobe lies posterior to the central sulcus and anterior to the parieto-occipital sulcus. It is superior to the temporal lobe (separated by the lateral sulcus). The parietal lobe is the primary center for somatosensory processing, spatial awareness, and integrating sensory information to construct a coherent perceptual representation of the world and body.

3.1 Primary Somatosensory Cortex (S1) - Brodmann Areas 1, 2, 3

  • Located in the postcentral gyrus, immediately posterior to the central sulcus
  • Contains the sensory homunculus - topographic representation of contralateral body sensations
  • Processes touch, pressure, temperature, pain, proprioception from the opposite side of body
  • BA 3a: Proprioception (muscle stretch)
  • BA 3b: Cutaneous touch (primary processing)
  • BA 1: Texture discrimination
  • BA 2: Size, shape recognition (stereognosis)
  • Areas with more sensory receptors (fingers, lips, tongue) have disproportionately large cortical representation

Clinical Relevance

Lesion: Contralateral loss of fine touch discrimination, two-point discrimination, proprioception, stereognosis (inability to identify objects by touch), graphesthesia loss (cannot identify numbers drawn on skin). Pain and temperature may be relatively preserved (thalamic processing).

3.2 Secondary Somatosensory Cortex (S2) - Brodmann Area 43

  • Located in the parietal operculum (superior bank of lateral sulcus)
  • Receives bilateral sensory input
  • Higher-order somatosensory processing
  • Integration of tactile information from both sides of body
  • Important for tactile learning and memory

3.3 Superior Parietal Lobule (SPL) - Brodmann Areas 5, 7

  • Located above the intraparietal sulcus
  • BA 5: Somatosensory association area - integrates tactile and proprioceptive info
  • BA 7: Visuospatial processing, spatial attention, reaching movements
  • Hand-eye coordination and visually-guided reaching
  • Body schema - internal representation of body position in space
  • Part of the dorsal visual stream ("where/how" pathway)

Clinical Relevance

Lesion: Optic ataxia (misreaching for objects under visual guidance despite intact vision and motor function), impaired spatial attention, difficulty with visually-guided movements, tactile agnosia.

3.4 Inferior Parietal Lobule (IPL) - Brodmann Areas 39, 40

  • BA 39 (Angular Gyrus):
    • Reading comprehension (visual word processing)
    • Mathematical operations (calculation)
    • Semantic processing and conceptual knowledge
    • Cross-modal integration (linking visual, auditory, tactile info)
    • Part of Wernicke's area (language comprehension network)
    • Involved in spatial cognition and memory retrieval
  • BA 40 (Supramarginal Gyrus):
    • Phonological processing (sound properties of words)
    • Short-term phonological memory
    • Part of Wernicke's area / language comprehension
    • Reading and writing
    • Tool use and gestural knowledge
    • Empathy and understanding others' emotions

Clinical Relevance

Angular Gyrus Lesion (BA 39): Gerstmann's Syndrome (finger agnosia, acalculia, agraphia, left-right confusion), alexia, semantic paraphasias.
Supramarginal Gyrus Lesion (BA 40): Conduction aphasia (repetition impairment), ideomotor apraxia, phonological errors.

3.5 Intraparietal Sulcus (IPS)

  • Divides superior and inferior parietal lobules
  • Numerical cognition and approximate quantity estimation
  • Spatial attention and eye movement planning
  • Grasping and object manipulation
  • Part of the dorsal attention network

Disorders Associated with Parietal Lobe Damage

  • Hemispatial Neglect (right parietal): Ignoring left side of space/body (most severe with right-hemisphere lesions)
  • Gerstmann's Syndrome (left angular gyrus): Finger agnosia, acalculia, agraphia, left-right confusion
  • Constructional Apraxia: Cannot draw, copy, or construct spatial designs
  • Optic Ataxia: Misreaching for objects despite intact vision (Balint's syndrome component)
  • Balint's Syndrome (bilateral parietal): Simultanagnosia + optic ataxia + oculomotor apraxia
  • Anosognosia: Unawareness of one's own deficit (right parietal)
  • Astereognosis: Cannot identify objects by touch alone
  • Ideomotor Apraxia (left IPL): Cannot perform skilled movements on command
  • Dressing Apraxia: Cannot orient clothing to put it on
  • Acalculia: Loss of mathematical ability
  • Conduction Aphasia: Impaired repetition with fluent speech and intact comprehension
  • Autotopagnosia: Cannot localize body parts

4. Temporal Lobe

The temporal lobe lies below the lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure), extending posteriorly to the parieto-occipital junction. It wraps around medially to include the hippocampus and parahippocampal gyrus. The temporal lobe is critical for auditory processing, language comprehension, memory formation, emotional processing, and object recognition.

4.1 Primary Auditory Cortex (A1) - Brodmann Areas 41, 42

  • Located in Heschl's gyrus (transverse temporal gyri), hidden in the lateral sulcus
  • First cortical processing station for auditory information
  • Tonotopic organization (different frequencies mapped to different locations)
  • BA 41: Core auditory processing - frequency, intensity, timing of sounds
  • BA 42: Auditory association - early sound pattern recognition
  • Bilateral representation (each ear projects to both hemispheres)

Clinical Relevance

Unilateral Lesion: Subtle hearing difficulties, poor sound localization. Bilateral Lesion: Cortical deafness (rare) or auditory agnosia (inability to recognize sounds despite intact hearing at brainstem level). Word deafness may occur with bilateral damage.

4.2 Auditory Association Cortex - Brodmann Area 22

  • Surrounds the primary auditory cortex on the superior temporal gyrus
  • Higher-order sound processing and interpretation
  • Left hemisphere: Speech sound processing, phonological analysis
  • Right hemisphere: Music perception, prosody (emotional tone of speech), environmental sounds
  • Part of the auditory "what" pathway

4.3 Wernicke's Area - Brodmann Areas 22, 39, 40 (posterior portion)

  • Located in the posterior superior temporal gyrus and extending to angular/supramarginal gyrus
  • Dominant hemisphere (usually left)
  • Comprehension of spoken and written language
  • Semantic processing - understanding meaning of words
  • Connected to Broca's area via the Arcuate Fasciculus
  • Part of a broader language comprehension network

Clinical Relevance - Wernicke's Aphasia (Receptive/Fluent Aphasia)

Speech is fluent but empty of meaning ("word salad"). Contains paraphasias (substituted words), neologisms (made-up words), press of speech. Comprehension is severely impaired. Repetition impaired. Patient is often UNAWARE of deficit (anosognosia). No motor deficits typically. Reading and writing also impaired.

4.4 Middle Temporal Gyrus - Brodmann Area 21

  • Semantic processing and conceptual knowledge
  • Word meaning retrieval (semantic memory)
  • Sentence comprehension at discourse level
  • Visual motion perception (junction with BA 37)
  • Integration of auditory and visual information

4.5 Inferior Temporal Gyrus - Brodmann Area 20

  • Part of the ventral visual stream ("what" pathway)
  • Complex object recognition
  • Color processing
  • Visual memory of objects
  • Face perception (contributing area)

4.6 Fusiform Gyrus (Occipitotemporal) - Brodmann Area 37

  • Located on the ventral (bottom) surface of the temporal lobe
  • Fusiform Face Area (FFA): Face recognition and processing
  • Visual Word Form Area (VWFA): Recognition of written words (left hemisphere)
  • Body recognition and biological motion
  • Expert-level visual discrimination (e.g., bird experts, car experts)

Clinical Relevance

Prosopagnosia: Inability to recognize familiar faces (can see faces but cannot identify who they are). Can be acquired (fusiform damage) or developmental/congenital.

4.7 Medial Temporal Lobe Structures

  • Hippocampus (detailed in Section 12): Episodic memory formation, spatial navigation
  • Parahippocampal Gyrus (BA 27, 28, 34, 35, 36): Memory encoding, spatial context, scene recognition
  • Entorhinal Cortex (BA 28): Gateway to hippocampus, spatial navigation (grid cells)
  • Perirhinal Cortex (BA 35, 36): Object recognition memory, familiarity judgment
  • Amygdala (detailed in Section 13): Emotional processing, fear conditioning

4.8 Temporal Pole - Brodmann Area 38

  • Anterior tip of the temporal lobe
  • Social cognition and theory of mind
  • Semantic memory (encyclopedic knowledge)
  • Emotional processing and emotional memory
  • Person identification and biographical knowledge
  • Naming of unique entities (proper nouns)

Clinical Relevance

Lesion: Semantic dementia (progressive loss of word and object meaning), difficulty naming famous people, impaired emotional processing. Bilateral temporal pole atrophy is characteristic of semantic variant primary progressive aphasia (svPPA).

Disorders Associated with Temporal Lobe Damage

  • Wernicke's Aphasia: Fluent but meaningless speech, impaired comprehension
  • Amnesia (Anterograde): Cannot form new memories (bilateral hippocampal damage - e.g., H.M. case)
  • Prosopagnosia: Inability to recognize faces
  • Auditory Agnosia: Cannot recognize sounds despite intact hearing
  • Semantic Dementia: Progressive loss of meaning/knowledge about words and objects
  • Temporal Lobe Epilepsy: Most common form; auras, deja vu, emotional experiences, automatisms
  • Kluver-Bucy Syndrome (bilateral): Hyperorality, hypersexuality, visual agnosia, placidity, hypermetamorphosis
  • Musical Amusia: Loss of ability to perceive or produce music (right temporal)
  • Anomia: Word-finding difficulties
  • Auditory Verbal Agnosia (Pure Word Deafness): Cannot comprehend spoken words but hearing is intact
  • Alzheimer's Disease: Begins with hippocampal and entorhinal cortex degeneration

5. Occipital Lobe

The occipital lobe is the smallest of the four major lobes, located at the posterior pole of the brain. It is bounded anteriorly by the parieto-occipital sulcus (medially) and an imaginary line connecting this sulcus to the pre-occipital notch (laterally). The occipital lobe is almost entirely devoted to visual processing.

5.1 Primary Visual Cortex (V1/Striate Cortex) - Brodmann Area 17

  • Located along the banks of the calcarine sulcus (medial surface)
  • Called "striate" cortex due to the stripe of Gennari (visible white line in Layer IV)
  • First cortical processing station for visual information from the retina via LGN
  • Retinotopic organization: Map of the visual field is preserved
  • Upper visual field maps to lower bank of calcarine sulcus (and vice versa)
  • Contralateral visual field representation
  • Foveal vision (central) has disproportionately large representation (cortical magnification)
  • Processes: orientation, spatial frequency, color (blobs), binocular disparity, motion direction

Clinical Relevance

Complete Unilateral Lesion: Contralateral homonymous hemianopia (loss of vision in opposite visual field of both eyes). Bilateral Lesion: Cortical blindness (Anton's syndrome if patient denies blindness - anosognosia for blindness). Macular sparing may occur due to dual blood supply from PCA and MCA.

5.2 Visual Association Cortex (V2) - Brodmann Area 18

  • Surrounds V1; receives direct input from V1
  • Second stage of visual processing
  • Processes: contour detection, figure-ground segregation, illusory contours
  • Distributes information to higher visual areas (V3, V4, V5/MT)
  • Beginning of complex form analysis

5.3 Higher Visual Areas - Brodmann Area 19

  • V3: Dynamic form processing, global motion
  • V4: Color perception, form recognition, moderate complexity shape analysis
  • V5/MT (Middle Temporal area): Motion perception, speed, direction of movement
  • V8: Color constancy

Disorders Associated with Occipital Lobe Damage

  • Homonymous Hemianopia: Loss of contralateral visual field in both eyes
  • Cortical Blindness: Complete bilateral V1 destruction - no conscious vision
  • Anton's Syndrome: Cortical blindness + denial of blindness (confabulates vision)
  • Blindsight: Ability to respond to visual stimuli without conscious awareness (V1 damaged but subcortical pathways intact)
  • Visual Agnosia (Apperceptive): Cannot form visual percepts/recognize shapes (early processing failure)
  • Visual Agnosia (Associative): Can perceive but cannot assign meaning to objects (ventral stream)
  • Cerebral Achromatopsia: Loss of color vision due to V4 damage (see world in grey)
  • Akinetopsia: Loss of motion perception (V5/MT lesion) - world appears as static "snapshots"
  • Prosopagnosia: Inability to recognize faces (fusiform/ventral stream)
  • Alexia without Agraphia (Pure Alexia): Cannot read but can still write (left occipital + splenium of corpus callosum)
  • Visual Hallucinations: Charles Bonnet syndrome (complex hallucinations in visually impaired)
  • Balint's Syndrome (bilateral parieto-occipital): Simultanagnosia + optic ataxia + oculomotor apraxia

6. Insular Cortex (The "Hidden" 5th Lobe)

The insula (Island of Reil) is a cortical region hidden deep within the lateral sulcus, beneath the frontal, parietal, and temporal opercula. It can only be seen when these opercula are pulled apart. Despite being hidden, it plays crucial roles in interoception, emotional awareness, consciousness, and many psychiatric conditions.

6.1 Anatomy of the Insula

  • Anterior Insula: Connected to limbic and prefrontal areas - emotional and cognitive integration
  • Posterior Insula: Connected to somatosensory and temporal areas - sensory processing
  • Brodmann Areas 13, 14, 15, 16 (various classification schemes)
  • Divided by central insular sulcus into anterior (3 short gyri) and posterior (2 long gyri)

  • Autism Spectrum: Altered interoception and emotional awareness processing
  • Frontotemporal Dementia: Early insular atrophy in behavioral variant

7. Limbic Lobe & Limbic System

The limbic system is a set of interconnected structures forming a ring ("limbus" = border) around the brainstem on the medial surface of the brain. Originally described by Paul Broca (1878) and later elaborated by James Papez (1937) and Paul MacLean (1952), the limbic system is the emotional brain - mediating emotion, motivation, memory, and autonomic regulation.

7.1 Limbic Lobe (Cortical Components)

  • Cingulate Gyrus (BA 23, 24, 25, 26, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33): Emotion, attention, pain processing, error detection
  • Parahippocampal Gyrus (BA 27, 28, 34, 35, 36): Memory encoding, spatial context
  • Subcallosal Gyrus (BA 25): Mood regulation, sadness processing
  • Retrosplenial Cortex (BA 29, 30): Spatial memory, navigation, episodic memory retrieval

7.3 Papez Circuit (Memory Circuit)

Hippocampus → Fornix → Mammillary Bodies → Mammillothalamic Tract → Anterior Thalamus → Cingulate Gyrus → Parahippocampal Gyrus → Hippocampus

Clinical Significance

Damage to any component of the Papez circuit can cause amnesia. This circuit is critical for the consolidation of episodic (autobiographical) memories from short-term to long-term storage.

7.4 Reward Circuit (Mesolimbic Pathway)

Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) → Nucleus Accumbens → Prefrontal Cortex (also amygdala, hippocampus)

Clinical Significance

The mesolimbic dopamine pathway is the "reward pathway" - crucial for motivation, pleasure, and reinforcement learning. Dysregulation is central to addiction (drugs hijack this system), schizophrenia (positive symptoms from excess dopamine), depression (reduced reward processing/anhedonia), and ADHD.

7.5 Functions of the Limbic System - Complete

  • Emotion: Generation, experience, and regulation of emotions
  • Memory: Encoding and consolidation of declarative memories, emotional memories
  • Motivation: Drive states (hunger, thirst, sex), reward-seeking, pleasure
  • Autonomic Regulation: Heart rate, blood pressure, respiration via hypothalamus
  • Endocrine Control: HPA axis stress response, hormonal regulation
  • Olfaction: Processing of smell (oldest sensory limbic connection)
  • Learning: Associative learning, conditioning (classical and operant)
  • Social Behavior: Attachment, bonding, parenting, social hierarchy
  • Homeostasis: Temperature, fluid balance, energy balance regulation
  • Sleep-Wake Cycle: Circadian rhythm regulation (suprachiasmatic nucleus)
  • Fight-Flight-Freeze: Threat response coordination

8. Prefrontal Cortex (PFC) - Detailed Subdivisions

The prefrontal cortex is the most anterior portion of the frontal lobe, occupying nearly one-third of the neocortex in humans. It is considered the "personality center" and is the region that makes us uniquely human. It is the last area to fully myelinate (around age 25) and is highly susceptible to early environmental influences, stress, and neurodevelopmental disruption.

8.1 Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC) - BA 9, 46

  • Located on the lateral surface of the frontal lobe, anterior to premotor areas
  • Functions:
  • Working memory (maintaining and manipulating information online)
  • Cognitive flexibility / set-shifting (ability to switch between tasks/rules)
  • Planning and organization of complex goal-directed behavior
  • Abstract reasoning and problem-solving
  • Selective and sustained attention
  • Temporal ordering (sequencing events in time)
  • Strategic retrieval from long-term memory
  • Verbal fluency (left DLPFC) and design fluency (right DLPFC)

Clinical Relevance

DLPFC dysfunction manifests as Dysexecutive Syndrome: Poor planning, inability to organize behavior, concrete thinking (impaired abstraction), perseveration (stuck on previous response), reduced verbal/design fluency, impaired working memory. Seen in: Schizophrenia (hypofrontality), Major Depression (target for rTMS therapy), ADHD, Traumatic Brain Injury, and dementias. Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (WCST) and Tower of London test specifically assess DLPFC functions.

8.2 Ventromedial Prefrontal Cortex (vmPFC) - BA 10, 11, 12, 25

  • Located on the medial and ventral surface of the frontal lobe
  • Overlaps significantly with orbitofrontal cortex
  • Functions:
  • Emotional decision-making (integrating emotion with cognition)
  • Value-based decision making (assigning value to choices)
  • Social cognition and moral reasoning
  • Self-referential processing (thinking about oneself)
  • Regulation of emotional responses
  • Fear extinction (learning that a threat is no longer dangerous)
  • Risk assessment and reward processing
  • Somatic marker hypothesis (Damasio) - body signals guide decisions

Clinical Relevance

vmPFC damage (Phineas Gage syndrome): Impaired social and emotional decision-making despite intact intelligence. Poor judgment, inability to learn from consequences, socially inappropriate behavior, flat affect, risk-taking. Iowa Gambling Task specifically tests vmPFC function. Relevant to: Psychopathy/Antisocial PD, addiction (impaired valuation), anxiety disorders (impaired fear extinction), depression (reduced self-worth processing).

8.3 Orbitofrontal Cortex (OFC) - BA 11, 12, 47

  • Located on the orbital (bottom) surface of the frontal lobe, above the eye sockets
  • Functions:
  • Impulse control and behavioral inhibition
  • Processing reward and punishment
  • Social behavior regulation and social norms
  • Personality expression
  • Olfactory identification (smell processing)
  • Taste (secondary gustatory area)
  • Emotional valuation of stimuli
  • Response reversal (changing behavior when contingencies change)
  • Anger and aggression modulation

Clinical Relevance

OFC damage: Disinhibition, impulsivity, social inappropriateness, poor emotional control, lability, childish humor (Witzelsucht), anosmia (if olfactory tract involved), perseverative responses, hyperphagia. "Pseudopsychopathic" personality. Relevant to: OCD (hyperactive OFC), behavioral variant FTD, traumatic brain injury (common coup-contrecoup site), antisocial behavior.

8.4 Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC) - BA 24, 25, 32, 33

  • Located on the medial surface, wrapping around the corpus callosum
  • Dorsal ACC (dACC - "cognitive division" BA 24, 32):
  • Conflict monitoring and error detection
  • Cognitive control and attention regulation
  • Response selection under competition
  • Pain processing (affective component)
  • Ventral/Subgenual ACC (sgACC - "emotional division" BA 25):
  • Mood regulation and emotional processing
  • Autonomic regulation
  • Sadness processing
  • Regulation of amygdala activity

Clinical Relevance

dACC: Damage causes apathy, reduced motivation, abulia, akinetic mutism (bilateral). Hyperactivity seen in OCD (excessive error monitoring) and anxiety (excessive conflict detection).
sgACC (BA 25): Hyperactive in treatment-resistant depression (target for deep brain stimulation). "Gateway to depression." Reduces activity in response to antidepressants and CBT. Critical for mood regulation.

8.5 Frontopolar Cortex - BA 10

  • Most anterior portion of the brain
  • Largest granular prefrontal area - most evolved
  • Functions:
  • Metacognition (thinking about thinking)
  • Prospective memory (remembering to do things in the future)
  • Multitasking / branching (maintaining goals while pursuing subgoals)
  • Relational reasoning (understanding complex relationships)
  • Autobiographical memory retrieval
  • Default mode network hub

10. Basal Ganglia

The basal ganglia are a group of subcortical nuclei located deep within the cerebral hemispheres. They form complex loops with the cerebral cortex and thalamus, playing critical roles in movement control, habit formation, reward processing, and cognitive/emotional functions. They do NOT initiate movement but modulate and fine-tune it.

10.3 Direct and Indirect Pathways

  • Direct Pathway (GO signal): Cortex → Striatum (D1 receptors) → GPi/SNr (inhibition) → Thalamus (disinhibition/excitation) → Cortex = Facilitates desired movement
  • Indirect Pathway (STOP signal): Cortex → Striatum (D2 receptors) → GPe (inhibition) → STN (disinhibition/excitation) → GPi/SNr (excitation) → Thalamus (inhibition) → Cortex = Suppresses unwanted movement
  • Hyperdirect Pathway: Cortex → STN (direct) → GPi = Rapid global inhibition ("emergency brake")

Disorders of the Basal Ganglia

  • Parkinson's Disease: Loss of dopaminergic neurons in SNc → Reduced direct pathway activation → Bradykinesia, rigidity, resting tremor, postural instability. Also: depression, cognitive slowing, executive dysfunction
  • Huntington's Disease: Degeneration of caudate/putamen (indirect pathway neurons first) → Chorea (involuntary jerky movements), personality change, dementia, psychiatric symptoms (often depression precedes motor symptoms)
  • OCD: Hyperactivity in cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) loops, especially caudate and OFC
  • Tourette's Syndrome: Basal ganglia disinhibition → Motor and vocal tics
  • Dystonia: Abnormal basal ganglia output → Sustained muscle contractions, abnormal postures
  • Hemiballismus: STN lesion (usually vascular) → Violent flinging movements of contralateral limbs
  • Addiction: Hijacking of nucleus accumbens reward circuitry
  • ADHD: Caudate/prefrontal loop dysfunction
  • Wilson's Disease: Copper deposition in putamen → Movement disorder + psychiatric symptoms

12. Hippocampal Formation

The hippocampus (Greek: "seahorse") is a curved structure located in the medial temporal lobe, forming the floor of the inferior horn of the lateral ventricle. It is arguably the most studied brain structure in neuroscience due to its critical role in memory formation. The hippocampal formation includes the hippocampus proper (CA1-CA4 fields), dentate gyrus, subiculum, and associated cortices.

12.2 Functions of the Hippocampus

  • Episodic Memory: Encoding of personal experiences (what happened, where, when)
  • Spatial Navigation: Cognitive maps, place cells (O'Keefe, Nobel Prize 2014)
  • Memory Consolidation: Transfer of memories from short-term (hippocampal) to long-term (cortical) storage during sleep
  • Contextual Memory: Binding items to their spatial/temporal context
  • Declarative Memory Formation: Both episodic and semantic (initially)
  • Relational Memory: Associating disparate elements into coherent memories
  • Future Thinking: Imagining future scenarios (constructive episodic simulation)
  • Stress Regulation: Negative feedback on HPA axis (glucocorticoid receptors)
  • Neurogenesis: One of only two brain regions with confirmed adult neurogenesis (dentate gyrus)

12.3 Hippocampal Lateralization

  • Left Hippocampus: Verbal/narrative memory (word lists, stories, names)
  • Right Hippocampus: Visuospatial/non-verbal memory (faces, routes, locations, designs)

Clinical Relevance

  • Anterograde Amnesia: Cannot form new declarative memories (bilateral damage - famous case: H.M./Henry Molaison)
  • Alzheimer's Disease: Hippocampal atrophy is earliest structural change; episodic memory loss is first symptom
  • PTSD: Reduced hippocampal volume; impaired contextual processing (cannot distinguish safe from dangerous contexts)
  • Major Depression: Hippocampal volume reduction (stress/cortisol-mediated); target for antidepressant neurogenesis
  • Temporal Lobe Epilepsy: Hippocampal sclerosis is most common cause; mesial temporal sclerosis
  • Transient Global Amnesia: Sudden temporary inability to form new memories (hippocampal ischemia)
  • Stress Effects: Chronic stress/elevated cortisol damages hippocampal neurons (dendritic retraction, reduced neurogenesis)
  • Schizophrenia: Reduced hippocampal volume, disorganized place cell activity
  • Korsakoff's Syndrome: Disconnection from mammillary bodies (Papez circuit disruption)

Key Neuropsychological Distinction

Hippocampal damage impairs: Declarative (explicit) memory - episodic and initially semantic
Hippocampal damage SPARES: Procedural memory (riding a bike), priming, classical conditioning, working memory (short-term). This dissociation (demonstrated in H.M.) established that memory is not a single system.

13. Amygdala

The amygdala (Greek: "almond") is an almond-shaped cluster of nuclei located deep within the medial temporal lobe, anterior to the hippocampus. It is the brain's primary threat detection and emotional processing center, playing a crucial role in fear, anxiety, aggression, social cognition, and emotional memory.

13.2 Functions of the Amygdala

  • Fear Conditioning: Learning that a stimulus predicts danger (Pavlovian fear learning)
  • Threat Detection: Rapid, automatic evaluation of potentially dangerous stimuli
  • Emotional Memory: Enhancing memory for emotional events (modulates hippocampal encoding)
  • Social Cognition: Reading facial expressions (especially fear, anger), judging trustworthiness
  • Aggression: Mediating aggressive and defensive behaviors
  • Reward Processing: Processing both appetitive (positive) and aversive stimuli
  • Attention to Emotion: Prioritizing processing of emotional over neutral stimuli
  • Autonomic Responses: Triggering fight-flight responses (via hypothalamus/brainstem)
  • Modulating Memory Consolidation: Stress hormones (noradrenaline) enhance emotional memory via amygdala
  • Social Hierarchy: Processing dominance and submission cues

13.3 Two Pathways to the Amygdala (LeDoux Model)

  • Low Road (Fast/Subcortical): Thalamus → Amygdala (direct). Quick, dirty, approximate - enables rapid threat response BEFORE conscious awareness
  • High Road (Slow/Cortical): Thalamus → Cortex → Amygdala. Slower, detailed, accurate - allows conscious appraisal and appropriate response

Clinical Relevance

  • Anxiety Disorders: Amygdala hyperreactivity to threat; reduced PFC regulation (top-down control failure)
  • PTSD: Hyperactive amygdala (exaggerated fear responses) + impaired extinction (vmPFC cannot inhibit amygdala)
  • Phobias: Conditioned fear response (amygdala-mediated) to specific stimulus
  • Social Anxiety: Exaggerated amygdala response to faces, especially critical/angry faces
  • Psychopathy/ASPD: Reduced amygdala activation to distress cues → lack of empathy, reduced fear
  • Autism Spectrum: Altered amygdala-social processing → difficulty reading emotions/faces
  • Depression: Increased amygdala reactivity to negative stimuli; negativity bias
  • Borderline Personality Disorder: Amygdala hyperreactivity to emotional stimuli → emotional dysregulation
  • Temporal Lobe Epilepsy: Amygdala often seizure focus → fear auras, ictal fear
  • Kluver-Bucy Syndrome (bilateral amygdala destruction): Emotional flattening, hyperorality, visual agnosia, hypersexuality, loss of fear
  • Urbach-Wiethe Disease: Bilateral amygdala calcification → inability to recognize fear in faces (Patient S.M.)

The cerebellum ("little brain") contains approximately 50% of all neurons in the brain despite being only 10% of brain volume. Located in the posterior cranial fossa below the occipital lobe, it is primarily known for motor coordination but is increasingly recognized for cognitive and emotional functions as well.

  • Motor coordination and smooth execution of voluntary movements
  • Balance and posture maintenance (via vestibular connections)
  • Motor learning and adaptation (learning new motor skills)
  • Timing and rhythm of movements
  • Error correction during movement (comparator function)
  • Eye movement coordination (smooth pursuit, VOR)
  • Speech articulation (motor component - cerebellar dysarthria)
  • Muscle tone regulation

B. Cognitive Functions (Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome - Schmahmann)

  • Executive function (planning, set-shifting, abstract reasoning)
  • Spatial cognition (visuospatial processing)
  • Language processing (verbal fluency, grammar, word retrieval)
  • Working memory contributions
  • Attention regulation
  • Timing perception (interval timing, rhythm)
  • Sequence learning and processing
  • Procedural learning (implicit skill learning)

C. Emotional/Affective Functions

  • Emotional regulation and modulation
  • Personality expression
  • Affective processing (vermis = "limbic cerebellum")
  • Social cognition contributions

Clinical Relevance

  • Cerebellar Ataxia: Incoordination, dysmetria (over/undershoot), intention tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, gait unsteadiness
  • Cerebellar Dysarthria: Scanning/staccato speech (irregular rhythm and volume)
  • Cerebellar Cognitive Affective Syndrome (CCAS/Schmahmann Syndrome): Executive dysfunction, spatial cognition deficits, personality changes, linguistic difficulties following cerebellar damage
  • Autism Spectrum Disorder: Cerebellar abnormalities (Purkinje cell loss) commonly reported
  • ADHD: Reduced cerebellar volume (vermis) - timing and attention deficits
  • Schizophrenia: "Cognitive dysmetria" theory - cerebellar-cortical circuit disruption
  • Dyslexia: Cerebellar theory - impaired automatization and timing
  • Essential Tremor: Cerebellar circuit dysfunction
  • Alcoholic Cerebellar Degeneration: Anterior vermis atrophy → gait ataxia

15.4 Reticular Formation & ARAS

  • Diffuse network of neurons throughout the brainstem core
  • Ascending Reticular Activating System (ARAS): Maintains wakefulness, consciousness, alertness

  • Sleep-wake transitions
  • Sensory gating (filtering irrelevant stimuli)

Clinical Relevance

ARAS damage: Coma, persistent vegetative state, disorders of consciousness. Locus Coeruleus dysfunction: PTSD (hyperarousal), depression (low NE), ADHD (NE/DA dysregulation). Raphe Nuclei dysfunction: Depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, pain sensitivity (serotonergic medications target these).

  • Multiple Sclerosis: Demyelination of white matter → varied neurological/cognitive symptoms depending on location

Clinical Psychology Application

Understanding neurotransmitter systems helps explain:
1. Mechanism of psychotropic medications: SSRIs increase serotonin; antipsychotics block dopamine D2; benzodiazepines enhance GABA; stimulants increase DA/NE
2. Biological basis of disorders: Monoamine hypothesis of depression; dopamine hypothesis of schizophrenia; GABA deficit in anxiety
3. Side effects: Why antipsychotics cause movement problems (nigrostriatal blockade) and metabolic effects
4. Comorbidity patterns: Shared neurotransmitter dysfunction explains why certain disorders co-occur

18. Cerebral Lateralization

Cerebral lateralization refers to the functional specialization of the two hemispheres. While both hemispheres contribute to most functions, certain processes are predominantly controlled by one hemisphere (cerebral dominance). This concept is critical in clinical neuropsychology for understanding lesion effects and planning assessments.

18.1 Left Hemisphere Functions (Dominant for Language in ~95% right-handers, ~70% left-handers)

  • Language Production: Speech articulation, grammar, syntax (Broca's area)
  • Language Comprehension: Word meaning, sentence understanding (Wernicke's area)
  • Reading and Writing: Grapheme-phoneme conversion, spelling
  • Verbal Memory: Remembering words, stories, names, lists
  • Calculation: Mathematical operations, number processing
  • Logic and Analytical Thinking: Sequential, detail-oriented processing
  • Verbal Fluency: Generating words quickly (phonemic and semantic)
  • Praxis: Skilled learned movements, tool use (dominant hand motor programs)
  • Right Body Control: Motor and sensory for right side

18.2 Right Hemisphere Functions (Non-dominant/Minor)

  • Visuospatial Processing: Spatial relationships, mental rotation, navigation
  • Spatial Attention: Attending to both sides of space (right hemisphere attends to BOTH sides; left only to right)
  • Prosody: Emotional tone of speech (understanding and producing emotional intonation)
  • Face Recognition: Holistic face processing, identifying familiar faces
  • Music Perception: Melody, pitch, harmony, musical emotions
  • Non-verbal Memory: Remembering faces, routes, designs, spatial layouts
  • Emotional Processing: Recognizing and expressing emotions (especially negative)
  • Constructional Ability: Drawing, building, spatial construction
  • Holistic/Gestalt Processing: Seeing the "big picture," global patterns
  • Humor and Metaphor: Understanding jokes, figurative language, sarcasm
  • Body Schema: Awareness of left body space and body image
  • Left Body Control: Motor and sensory for left side

Clinical Relevance for Assessment

Left hemisphere damage: Aphasia, verbal memory loss, reading/writing deficits, apraxia, right-sided motor/sensory deficits. Patient often aware and frustrated. Depression common.
Right hemisphere damage: Hemispatial neglect, visuospatial deficits, prosopagnosia, emotional processing deficits, anosognosia (denial of illness), constructional problems. Patient often unaware of deficits. Mania/euphoria sometimes seen.
Assessment implication: Always use BOTH verbal AND non-verbal tests in neuropsychological batteries to assess each hemisphere independently.

Triple Network Model (Menon, 2011)

Three core networks interact dynamically:
1. Default Mode Network (DMN): Active during rest, self-reflection, internal mentation
2. Central Executive Network (CEN): Active during cognitive tasks, external attention
3. Salience Network (SN): Acts as a "switch" between DMN and CEN; detects relevant stimuli

Clinical Application: Many psychiatric disorders can be understood as dysfunction in one or more of these networks or their interactions. For example, in depression, the DMN is hyperactive (rumination), CEN is hypoactive (poor concentration), and SN is biased toward negative stimuli. In schizophrenia, the SN fails to properly switch between DMN and CEN, leading to confused internal/external processing.

Key Principles of Neuropsychological Assessment for MPhil

  • Double Dissociation: If patient A has deficit X but not Y, and patient B has Y but not X, then X and Y are mediated by different brain systems
  • Qualitative vs Quantitative: HOW a patient fails is as important as WHETHER they fail (error analysis)
  • Ecological Validity: Tests should predict real-world functioning
  • Premorbid Estimation: Always estimate premorbid functioning to determine actual decline
  • Cultural Sensitivity: Use culturally appropriate norms and tests
  • Pattern Analysis: Look for patterns of strengths/weaknesses across domains to localize dysfunction
