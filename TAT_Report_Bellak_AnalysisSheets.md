# THEMATIC APPERCEPTION TEST (TAT)
## Psychodiagnostic Report — Bellak Analysis-Sheet Method

**Instrument:** The Indian Modification of the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT), Dr. Uma Chaudhary edition
**Interpretive system:** Bellak, L. & Abrams, D. M., *The T.A.T., the C.A.T., and the S.A.T. in Clinical Use* — the Bellak T.A.T. Blank & Analysis Sheet (10-variable scheme; descriptive → interpretive → diagnostic levels)
**Cards administered:** Card 1 to Card 10 + Card 11 (blank card)
**Language of production:** Hinglish (transcribed verbatim by the examinee)

---

### A NOTE ON METHOD (per the manual)

Bellak instructs the interpreter to read each story on three levels: the **descriptive** (what literally happens), the **interpretive** (a generalised conditional clause, usually framed as *"If one…"*), and the **diagnostic** (a definite statement about the person). The interpreter then condenses each record onto the Analysis Sheet's ten variables. Throughout, Bellak warns that fantasy material is "prima facie only the fantasy needs of the subject," that the relation of fantasy needs to behaviour must be checked against clinical/biographical data, and that single signs are never diagnostic in isolation — consistency *across* stories is what carries weight.

Two features Bellak stresses repeatedly are honoured below:
1. **Card 1 (violin)** is treated as the single richest card for the **self-image/body-image** and for the parental relationship and the achievement need (real vs. *deus ex machina* success).
2. The **"crime and punishment" sequence** is read for superego adequacy, and **loss of psychological distance** (telling the story openly as oneself) is flagged as clinically significant.

## CARD 1 — The boy and the musical instrument (Veena/violin card)

**1. Main theme**
- *Descriptive:* A boy who loves music sits before a stringed instrument (veena) trying to draw out the correct notes. He works very hard but cannot get it right, becomes sad and just stares at it, then doubts whether he can succeed. He keeps trying again and again; finally, after repeated effort, he composes a tune and is overjoyed that effort paid off.
- *Interpretive:* *If* one applies oneself to a valued, difficult task, one will move through discouragement and self-doubt, but sustained effort eventually brings genuine mastery and great joy.
- *Diagnostic:* A goal-directed achievement orientation in which success is *earned piece by piece* (not magical), but the path runs through marked sadness and self-doubt before resolution.

**2. Main hero** (✓)
- a) Age: child/adolescent boy ~ b) Sex: male ~ c) Vocation: student/aspiring musician
- d) Abilities: musical aptitude, capacity for sustained effort ~ e) Interests: music, mastery, "perfection"
- f) Traits: persevering, self-critical, sensitive, prone to discouragement
- g) Body/self-image: **intact and competent**; the instrument is *not* described as broken/dead (Bellak's danger sign for emotional impoverishment is absent) — instead it is an object to be mastered, signalling a fundamentally workable self-image overlaid with performance doubt
- h) Adequacy: **adequate** — carries the task through under internal difficulty to a realistic, satisfying end

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**
- a) Behavioural needs (as in story): **need for achievement and mastery**; need for perfection; persistence/endurance. *Dynamic inference:* achievement is a need that is high in both fantasy and behaviour but evidently frustrated enough to require gratification — i.e., a real, ego-syntonic striving accompanied by fear of falling short.
- b) Figures/objects/circumstances introduced: the *tune that finally emerges* (introduced as the reward) → implies need for tangible proof of competence and for self-validation.
- c) Figures/objects/circumstances omitted: **no parental figures introduced** (Card 1 usually evokes the parent relationship); their omission suggests the achievement struggle here is **internalised and self-referential** rather than framed around pleasing/defying a parent — autonomy in the achievement sphere.

**4. Conception of environment (world) as:** neutral, non-threatening; a private workspace in which the only obstacle is the self and the task. The world neither helps nor punishes — outcome depends on the hero's own effort.

**5. Figures seen as**
- a) Parental figures: *not introduced* (see 3c).
- b) Contemporary figures: none.
- c) Junior figures: none.

**6. Significant conflicts:** achievement vs. discouragement/giving-up; aspiration ("perfect ho jaun") vs. self-doubt ("nikal paaunga ya nahi"). This is a conflict **between two drives/ego-functions** (achievement vs. the pull to quit) rather than drive-vs-superego.

**7. Nature of anxieties** (✓)
- (✓) **disapproval / failure to meet an internal standard** (fear of not being "perfect")
- (✓) **inadequacy / helplessness** (transient — "kar paaunga ya nahi")
- (–) physical harm, loss of love, illness, desertion not salient

**8. Main defenses against conflicts and fears** (✓)
- (✓) **Sublimation** (channelling tension into musical creation) — an adaptive, mature defence
- (✓) **Persistence / reality-based mastery** (counteractive effort)
- (–) no repression/denial of the difficulty (the sadness is consciously owned)

**9. Adequacy of superego** (✓)
- (✓) **Appropriate** — standards are high but not punitive; the hero is not harmed for "failing," he is simply required to keep trying. Self-demand is firm but proportionate.

**10. Integration of the ego** (✓)
- a) Hero: **adequate**
- b) Outcome: **happy** and **realistic**
- c) Drive control: good — frustration is tolerated and converted to renewed effort
- d) Thought processes: **structured, appropriate, complete, original** (a coherent striving-to-success narrative with a clear beginning/middle/end and a created product)
- Intelligence: **average to above average** | Maturation level: age-appropriate to slightly mature | Organic signs: none (the instrument is correctly recognised and elaborated)

## CARD 2 — Rural / village scene

- *Descriptive:* A village where everyone is happy and the daily routine flows: children play, women and mothers cook, some fetch water, some go to the fields; a mother cooks for her children, the father takes cattle/goats to be watered, sometimes the mother goes to a distant market with the children. Green, calm, an "ideal village," each household and family member doing their duty.
- *Interpretive:* *If* a family/community each fulfils its assigned role, life is orderly, harmonious, and contented.
- *Diagnostic:* A strong investment in, and idealisation of, a stable, role-bound, harmonious family/community structure — order and belonging are equated with well-being.

**2. Main hero** (✓)
- a) Age: adult ~ b) Sex: diffuse, leaning female (the nurturing mother) ~ c) Vocation: homemaker / agricultural family
- d) Abilities: caretaking, provisioning ~ e) Interests: family welfare, routine ~ f) Traits: dutiful, nurturant, industrious
- g) Body/self-image: not individualised (the "hero" is the collective family) ~ h) Adequacy: adequate within a prescribed role

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**
- a) Behavioural needs: **need for order, security, affiliation/belonging, nurturance**; need for harmony.
- b) Introduced: cattle, fields, water, market, the *intact whole family* → need for stability, sufficiency, and family cohesion.
- c) Omitted: **conflict, hardship, the "pregnant woman/autonomy-vs-family" tension** that this card classically pulls (per Bellak, Picture 2 typically evokes autonomy-vs-compliance and oedipal/sibling themes). Their omission → a **need to avoid/deny conflict** and to keep the family image conflict-free.

**4. Conception of environment (world) as:** **benign, nurturant, abundant, orderly, peaceful** — an idealised "good world."

**5. Figures seen as**

- b) Contemporary figures: other village women (e.g., bhabhi) — cooperative, role-sharing.
- c) Junior figures: children — carefree, playing; benignly regarded.

**6. Significant conflicts:** none expressed overtly — a **conspicuous absence of conflict** (itself meaningful: idealisation/denial).

**7. Nature of anxieties** (✓): essentially none manifest; latent need to ward off disruption of the secure family scene.

**8. Main defenses** (✓)
- (✓) **Idealisation**
- (✓) **Denial** (of conflict/hardship in a scene that usually elicits it)

**9. Adequacy of superego** (✓): not tested by any transgression; values are pro-social and conventional → **appropriate but untested**.

**10. Integration of the ego** (✓)
- a) Hero: adequate
- b) Outcome: **happy, realistic** (though idyllic)
- c) Drive control: high (perhaps over-controlled / conflict-avoidant)
- d) Thought processes: **structured, appropriate, complete**, somewhat **stereotyped** (a conventional "ideal village" tableau)
- Intelligence: average | Maturation: adequate | Organic signs: none

## CARD 3 — Father and son / report-card theme (3BM-equivalent, M.B)

**1. Main theme**
- *Descriptive:* A boy who is weak in studies and has scored poorly is afraid to tell his father. He hesitates ("bataun ya na bataun"), first softens it ("I studied a lot today"), then admits the marks. He fears scolding/beating, but the father responds with understanding — *"koshish karne walon ki haar nahi hoti"* (those who keep trying never lose), telling him that a person falls, rises, falls again, and finally succeeds. The boy becomes emotional, tears come, hugs his father, and vows: *"Papa, I fell behind, but now I will do anything for you."*
- *Interpretive:* *If* one fails and braces for punishment from authority but instead receives understanding, the response is relief, guilt, love, and a surge of reparative motivation.
- *Diagnostic:* Core dynamic of **fear of disapproval/punishment from the father** that is *disconfirmed*; a benevolent, idealised paternal introject; guilt converted into a reparative pledge.

**2. Main hero** (✓)
- a) Age: school-age boy/adolescent ~ b) Sex: male ~ c) Vocation: student
- d) Abilities: academically "weak," but emotionally expressive and capable of resolve ~ e) Interests: gaining father's approval ~ f) Traits: anxious, guilt-prone, sensitive, honest (eventually), reparative
- g) Body/self-image: a "lagging," inadequate self in the achievement domain, hungry for approval ~ h) Adequacy: **initially inadequate (fearful, avoidant), becoming adequate** through honest disclosure and resolve

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**
- a) Behavioural needs: **need for approval/acceptance**, **need for achievement** (to do well for the father), need for **nurturance/forgiveness**, and **need to repair (reparation)**. *Dynamic inference:* the intensity of relief suggests a chronically high need to avoid disapproval.
- b) Introduced: the **understanding, lecturing father**; the *tears*; the *embrace* → need for a forgiving authority and for emotional reconciliation.
- c) Omitted: any **aggression/punishment** that the card's tension anticipates → need to repress/deny paternal aggression (and his own fear of it) by replacing it with kindness.

**4. Conception of environment (world) as:** **demanding but ultimately forgiving and supportive** — authority that instructs rather than punishes.

**5. Figures seen as**
- a) Parental figures: **father — idealised, wise, forgiving, encouraging.** Reaction: fear → relief → love, guilt, gratitude, identification.
- b) Contemporary figures: none.
- c) Junior figures: the boy himself is the junior; self-regarded as wanting/lacking.

**6. Significant conflicts:** failure/inadequacy vs. expectation; **superego (fear of disapproval) vs. need for autonomy/achievement**; honesty vs. concealment.

**7. Nature of anxieties** (✓)
- (✓) **disapproval** (primary)
- (✓) **physical harm/punishment** (anticipated beating — "papa mujhe maarenge ya daantenge")
- (✓) **lack or loss of love** (fear of forfeiting father's regard)

**8. Main defenses** (✓)
- (✓) **Undoing / reparation** (the vow to "do anything for you")
- (✓) **Identification** (with the benevolent father)
- (✓) **Repression/denial** of paternal aggression

**9. Adequacy of superego** (✓)
- (✓) **Appropriate in outcome but internally severe** — the *feared* punishment is harsh and immediate in anticipation (a strict internal superego), yet the *enacted* response is lenient/benevolent. The gap between feared and actual punishment indicates a **superego that is harsher internally than the represented external authority**.
- (✓) **Delayed/initial hesitation** ("bataun ya na bataun") — superego interference shown by pausing/avoidance before disclosure.

**10. Integration of the ego** (✓)
- a) Hero: **adequate** by story's end
- b) Outcome: **happy, realistic** (emotionally credible reconciliation)
- c) Drive control: adequate; affect (tears) is expressed and contained
- d) Thought processes: **structured, appropriate, complete**, with genuine affective elaboration; mildly **conventional** moral message
- Intelligence: average | Maturation: adequate, with strong dependent-affiliative coloring | Organic signs: none

## CARD 4 — Mother and son; poverty and demands (M.B)

**1. Main theme**
- *Descriptive:* A very poor mother; her son insists on toys and clothes "like the other children have," asks why she won't take him to the market. The mother says money is short but promises to take him, comforting that "one day God will look our way." Privately she grieves that she cannot provide. She goes to a wealthy villager and borrows money to buy the things. When the son learns the money was borrowed, he feels guilty, refuses the things — *"Maa, I don't want any of this, I only want your love; if you are here, I have everything; return the money."* Both end emotionally moved, sad outwardly but loving and close inwardly.
- *Interpretive:* *If* one realises that one's demands have driven a loving parent to sacrifice/indebtedness, material wishes are renounced in favour of the relationship, and guilt is resolved through that renunciation.
- *Diagnostic:* A guilt-and-reparation dynamic around material need within a depriving but devoted parent–child bond; **love is consciously elevated above material gratification.**

**2. Main hero** (✓) — *the examinee explicitly nominates the MOTHER as the main character ("she did everything for the child and would go to any length").*
- a) Age: mother ~40–45; son ~19 ~ b) Sex: female (mother) / male (son) ~ c) Vocation: poor/likely farming family, homemaker mother
- d) Abilities: self-sacrifice, resourcefulness (secures a loan) ~ e) Interests: child's happiness ~ f) Traits: devoted, enduring, self-effacing, sorrowful
- g) Body/self-image (via son, the secondary identification figure): a "lacking," guilt-laden self who feels he has *less* than peers ~ h) Adequacy: mother **adequate** (acts to meet the need); son **adequate at resolution** (renounces, repairs)

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**
- a) Behavioural needs: (mother) **nurturance, self-sacrifice**; (son) **need for love over material things**, **need to relieve/not burden the parent**, reparation. *Dynamic inference:* the son's intense renunciation suggests strong **succorance needs warded off by guilt** — he both wants to be given to and feels he must not take.
- b) Introduced: the **wealthy villager / the borrowed money / "God will look our way"** → need for an external rescuer/benefactor and a religious-hope defence against helplessness; the *debt* introduces guilt.
- c) Omitted: a present father/provider → the family is implicitly **father-absent or father-insufficient**, throwing provision onto the mother (heightened maternal dependence).

**4. Conception of environment (world) as:** **depriving and harsh in material terms, but redeemable through love and faith**; help must be sought/borrowed from others.

**5. Figures seen as**
- a) Parental figures: **mother — loving, sacrificing, omnipotent-by-devotion**; reaction: love, guilt, protectiveness toward her.
- b) Contemporary figures: the wealthy villager — a benefactor/creditor (mildly ambivalent: source of help and of indebtedness/shame); peers who "have everything" — objects of envy/comparison.
- c) Junior figures: the son (if mother is hero) — needy, demanding, then contrite.

**6. Significant conflicts:** material desire vs. guilt; dependence/wanting vs. wish not to burden the loved parent; **id-wish (acquisition) vs. superego (guilt)**.

**7. Nature of anxieties** (✓)
- (✓) **lack or loss of love** (the relationship is the prized object)
- (✓) **deprivation** (poverty, "I have less")
- (✓) **disapproval/guilt** (causing the mother distress)

**8. Main defenses** (✓)
- (✓) **Reparation / undoing** (returning the money, renouncing the goods)
- (✓) **Idealisation** (of the mother)
- (✓) **Reaction-formation** (intense rejection of the very things he had demanded)

**9. Adequacy of superego** (✓)
- (✓) **Appropriate, leaning severe** — guilt is strong and prompt once the "transgression" (driving mother into debt) is recognised; the boy punishes himself by renunciation. Healthy moral sense, but self-denying.

**10. Integration of the ego** (✓)
- a) Hero: **adequate** (both figures resolve constructively)
- b) Outcome: **"sad outside, happy inside"** — bittersweet; **realistic** and emotionally mature
- c) Drive control: good — wanting is mastered by relational value
- d) Thought processes: **structured, appropriate, complete**, richly elaborated with dialogue and inner states
- Intelligence: average to above average | Maturation: emotionally mature resolution | Organic signs: none

## CARD 5 — Husband and wife conflict (Card 4-equivalent / marital)

**1. Main theme**
- *Descriptive:* A poor couple quarrels; the wife complains that the husband does nothing, there is no food/grain, no decent clothes — "how long will we live in such poverty? Do something." The husband retorts "what should I do to earn money?", the fight escalates, and at its peak he **grabs/chokes her throat**. The fight then subsides; he realises he was wrong, understands she was speaking about *their* future, apologises ("I grabbed your throat, this will never happen again; I will work and keep the family happy"). The wife says she was thinking only of him. The husband's failure is traced to **falling into bad company / intoxication (daaru, nasha)**; once he leaves the addiction he can work, customers will return, behaviour will improve. Both end happy; wife is the main character because she endured and persuaded. Named Rahul (28) and Sita (25–26).
- *Interpretive:* *If* frustration and felt inadequacy (worsened by intoxication) overwhelm a man, he may act out aggressively toward the one who confronts him — but with insight he recognises the substance as the cause, makes reparation, and reforms.
- *Diagnostic:* **Aggression under frustration with poor impulse control, followed by remorse and reparation; explicit, ego-syntonic insight that intoxication/addiction is the destructive agent.** This addiction theme links directly to Cards 5 and 11.

**2. Main hero** (✓) — *examinee nominates the WIFE (Sita) as main character (she endures, persuades, tolerates).*
- a) Age: wife 25–26; husband 28 ~ b) Sex: female (hero) / male ~ c) Vocation: husband runs/should run a shop; wife homemaker
- d) Abilities: (wife) endurance, foresight, persuasion; (husband) capacity for insight ~ e) Interests: family stability/future ~ f) Traits: (wife) patient, future-oriented, long-suffering; (husband) frustrated, impulsive, then contrite
- g) Body/self-image: the husband's self as a *failed provider* shamed into rage; the wife's self as the steadfast moral centre ~ h) Adequacy: husband **inadequate then partially restored**; wife **adequate**

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**
- a) Behavioural needs: (husband) **aggression** (acute), need to escape felt inadequacy; (wife) **need for security, order, nurturance of the family**, **need to reform the partner**. *Dynamic inference:* the overt choking is a strong behavioural-aggression datum; per Bellak it must be weighed against history, but its vividness is notable.
- b) Introduced: **alcohol/drugs (nasha)** as causal agent; "bad company"; customers/shop → need to externalise the cause of failure onto substances, and a wish for social/occupational rehabilitation.
- c) Omitted: children/extended family are not central → the dyad's survival is the whole stage.

**4. Conception of environment (world) as:** **harsh, depriving, conflict-laden**, where livelihood and respect must be re-earned, and where intoxication corrodes both.

**5. Figures seen as**
- a) Parental figures: none.
- b) Contemporary figures: **spouse** — at first an antagonist/provoker, then a partner to reconcile with; customers/society — withholding until behaviour improves. Reaction: conflict → aggression → remorse → repair.
- c) Junior figures: none.

**6. Significant conflicts:** aggression vs. control; provider-failure/shame vs. self-worth; **addiction/pleasure vs. responsibility**; dependence on the spouse vs. resentment of her confrontation.

**7. Nature of anxieties** (✓)
- (✓) **deprivation** (poverty, no food/clothes)
- (✓) **physical harm** (overt violence enacted)
- (✓) **disapproval/failure** (felt inadequacy as a provider)

**8. Main defenses** (✓)
- (✓) **Acting-out** (the choking) — failure of defence/impulse control
- (✓) **Rationalisation / externalisation** ("it was the nasha")
- (✓) **Undoing / reparation** (apology, vow to reform)
- (✓) **Isolation/insight** (intellectual recognition that substances change a person)

**9. Adequacy of superego** (✓)
- (✓) **Inconsistent / delayed** — superego is **absent at the moment of violence** (no immediate brake) but **re-asserts strongly afterward** as remorse and reparation. This **non-integrated superego (lenient then severe)** is, per the manual, frequently seen in conflicted/neurotic functioning and is clinically important here.

**10. Integration of the ego** (✓)
- a) Hero: **inadequate at the crisis, adequate at resolution**
- b) Outcome: **happy**, but the reconciliation is somewhat **idealised/wish-fulfilling** given the severity of the act (rapid repair after near-strangulation)
- c) Drive control: **poor under stress** (a key finding) with post-hoc recovery
- d) Thought processes: **structured, complete, appropriate**, with cause-and-effect reasoning and a moral conclusion ("nasha is wrong, it changes a person")
- Intelligence: average | Maturation: capacity for insight present but impulse regulation immature | Organic signs: none

## CARD 6 — Landscape / "the world of my dreams"

**1. Main theme**
- *Descriptive:* The narrator says "this is my dream world." Today's world — cities, crowds, vehicles, technology, EVs — runs on, while old things (villages, fields, forests, mountains, rivers, birds, animals) can no longer "play their part." There is pollution, traffic noise, few birds left, vanishing wildlife, villages turning into cities. He wishes for a world with just himself, his partner, his parents (and a future child), surrounded by trees, forest, greenery, animals; land to grow vegetables, enough for the household; total peace, no excess technology or mobiles (everything is "inside the mobile" now). He ends **happy**, with his family, close to nature — "I am where I should be."
- *Interpretive:* *If* the modern, over-stimulating, degraded world is escaped, one can be at peace surrounded by a small loving family and unspoiled nature.
- *Diagnostic:* A **withdrawal/escapist wish** toward a simplified, self-sufficient, family-centred refuge; anti-overstimulation; longing for control, purity, and closeness. **Loss of psychological distance** — told openly as the narrator's own wish (Bellak's flag for personal preoccupation).

**2. Main hero** (✓) — *the narrator himself (explicit "main chahta hoon"; distance is lost).*
- a) Age: young adult (the examinee) ~ b) Sex: male ~ c) Vocation: implicitly a small self-sufficient cultivator/family man
- d) Abilities: capacity to envision, value relationships and nature ~ e) Interests: nature, family, simplicity, peace ~ f) Traits: sensitive, idealistic, peace-seeking, technology-averse, somewhat avoidant
- g) Body/self-image: not a bodily theme; a **self seeking refuge and rightful place** ~ h) Adequacy: adequate *within fantasy* (the solution is imagined, not enacted)

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**
- a) Behavioural needs: **need for peace/quiet (harm- and stimulus-avoidance)**, **need for affiliation/closeness (family, partner)**, need for **autonomy/self-sufficiency**, nurturance of nature. *Dynamic inference:* the strong push to remove technology/crowds suggests **flight from a world experienced as intrusive/overwhelming**.
- b) Introduced: **partner, parents, future child, farmland, animals, greenery** → need for a contained, loving, controllable world.
- c) Omitted: work, money pressure, conflict, other people → **need to exclude stressors and competition** by depopulating the world to the family.

**4. Conception of environment (world) as:** the *real* world = degraded, polluted, noisy, alienating; the *wished* world = serene, natural, nurturing, safe.

**5. Figures seen as**
- a) Parental figures: **parents included in the ideal world** — cherished, kept close (strong attachment; wish to retain them).
- b) Contemporary figures: partner — a desired intimate companion; broader society — excluded/aversive.
- c) Junior figures: a future child — hoped for, part of the idyll.

**6. Significant conflicts:** the self/family vs. an intrusive modern world; engagement vs. **withdrawal**; desire for closeness vs. retreat from the wider social field.

**7. Nature of anxieties** (✓)
- (✓) **being overpowered / overstimulated** by the modern world (helplessness vis-à-vis technology/pollution)
- (✓) latent **fear of loss** (wanting to keep family gathered and safe)

**8. Main defenses** (✓)
- (✓) **Withdrawal / escapism**
- (✓) **Idealisation** (of nature and the simple life)
- (✓) **Reaction-formation/avoidance** toward technology

**9. Adequacy of superego** (✓): not engaged by transgression; values are benign/pro-social → **appropriate, untested**.

**10. Integration of the ego** (✓)
- a) Hero: adequate in fantasy
- b) Outcome: **happy** but **unrealistic / utopian** (a wish rather than a worked-through solution)
- c) Drive control: high (over-controlled; avoids rather than engages conflict)
- d) Thought processes: **structured and appropriate** but **somewhat unstructured/discursive and idealised**; reflective and articulate
- Intelligence: above average (notable ecological/reflective elaboration) | Maturation: idealistic, escapist coloring | Organic signs: none

## CARD 7 — Marital scene; infidelity (4/13MF-equivalent)

**1. Main theme**
- *Descriptive:* The man is with another woman (a girlfriend) while his wife waits at home wondering when he will return. The couple was once happy, but since the other woman entered his life, quarrels began. In one scene he is leaving home saying he may be late 1–2 days; the wife protests that "tomorrow is our special day, you promised we'd celebrate / shop"; she pleads "you didn't used to leave like this, don't go." He is thinking of the other woman, they quarrel, and he storms off to his girlfriend. The wife is left sad. The husband **feels little remorse**. The wife discovers/confronts the affair; he admits it; they fight; he is not very sorry ("if he were, he wouldn't have gone"). It may end in **divorce**.
- *Interpretive:* *If* a partner's desire shifts to someone outside the marriage, the betrayed spouse is left longing and sorrowful while the unfaithful one feels little guilt and the bond may dissolve.
- *Diagnostic:* Theme of **infidelity, betrayal, and abandonment** with a **conspicuously lenient/absent superego in the transgressing figure** — a marked contrast to Cards 3, 4, and the post-hoc remorse of Card 5.

**2. Main hero** (✓) — *split between husband (the actor) and wife (the sufferer); narrative sympathy leans to the wife.*
- a) Age: adult couple ~ b) Sex: male (husband) / female (wife) ~ c) Vocation: unspecified
- d) Abilities: (wife) capacity for attachment/loyalty ~ e) Interests: (husband) the other woman, novelty; (wife) the marriage, the "special day" ~ f) Traits: (husband) self-centred, avoidant of guilt; (wife) loyal, anxious, pleading
- g) Body/self-image: not bodily; a **rejected/unchosen self** (wife) vs. a self pursuing gratification elsewhere (husband) ~ h) Adequacy: husband morally **inadequate**; wife **adequate but powerless**

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**
- a) Behavioural needs: (husband) **sexual/novelty need, autonomy, avoidance of the marital bond**; (wife) **need for love, fidelity, attention, affiliation**. *Dynamic inference:* the careful, sympathetic rendering of the *wife's* longing suggests the examinee identifies with the **abandoned/betrayed position** (fear of being unchosen).
- b) Introduced: **the other woman / "special day" / promise** → themes of triangular jealousy and broken commitment.
- c) Omitted: any reparative or punishing consequence for the husband → his transgression goes largely unpunished (superego leniency).

**4. Conception of environment (world) as:** **untrustworthy and abandoning in intimate relationships**; love is precarious and can be withdrawn.

# PART II — INTEGRATED INTERPRETATION & PSYCHODIAGNOSTIC REPORT

*(Synthesis of the 11 records across Bellak's ten variables, followed by an ego-function appraisal, a dynamic and descriptive formulation, and recommendations. Per the manual, conclusions rest on the **consistency of patterns across stories**, not on isolated signs.)*

## 1. Main Themes (recurrent across the protocol)

Three theme-clusters repeat with high consistency:

1. **Failure / falling-short → guilt → reparation.** A protagonist falls short (studies in Card 3, provision in Cards 4–5, conduct in Card 11), feels guilt, and repairs the bond through renunciation, apology, or a pledge to reform. This guilt-and-repair arc is the **signature dynamic** of the record and culminates, undisguised, in the de-addiction narrative of Card 11.
2. **Conflict within close relationships, usually resolved through understanding.** Parent–child (3, 4, 11) and husband–wife (5, 7, 9) ties are the central stage; most are reconciled (3, 4, 5, 9), a minority are not (7, 8).
3. **An undercurrent of sadness ("udaasi") in nearly every story**, paired with a longing for love, harmony, peace, and escape (Cards 6, 8, 10).

## 2. Main Hero (aggregate)

Across cards the hero is an **emotionally sensitive, guilt-prone, self-doubting young male** (and his proxies), capable of effort, insight, and attachment. **Hero adequacy is variable in a patterned way** (Bellak ties this directly to ego strength): **adequate** when the task is effort-based or repairable through communication (Cards 1, 3, 9) and when the protagonist renounces/repairs (Card 4); **inadequate/overwhelmed** when the theme is abandonment, helplessness, or self-destruction (Cards 7-as-betrayed, 8, and the historical self of 11). Notably, **psychological distance collapses on Cards 6 and 11** (told openly as himself) — by Bellak's criterion, a marker of **strong personal preoccupation** with the contents of those cards (idealised escape; addiction/parents/recovery).

## 3. Main Needs and Drives (aggregate)

## 4. Conception of the Environment (aggregate)

## DYNAMIC FORMULATION

This is the protocol of a **young man organised around a powerful need for parental love and approval**, who experiences himself as a **"fallen," guilt-laden figure** repeatedly rescued by an **idealised father**. His inner world is **split** into an all-good family enclave and a corrupting, depriving outside world. When confronted with frustration, felt inadequacy, or the pull of impulse, his **controls have at times failed** — expressed in fantasy as aggression (Card 5) and, in reality, in **substance dependence and a past suicide attempt** (Card 11).
