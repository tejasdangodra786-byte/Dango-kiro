Part 2 · What Exactly Is CBT?

2.1 Authoritative definition

Cognitive Behaviour Therapy is a structured, time-limited, present-oriented, collaborative psychotherapy that helps a person solve current problems and change unhelpful (inaccurate and/or self-defeating) patterns of thinking and behaviour. Every course of treatment is guided by an individualised cognitive formulation of the person and of the disorder, and change is produced through a blend of cognitive methods (examining and re-evaluating thoughts/beliefs) and behavioural methods (changing what the person does, avoids, or repeats).
Core definition paraphrased from Judith S. Beck, Cognitive Behavior Therapy: Basics and Beyond (2e, Guilford, 2011, Ch. 1), tracing the model to A. T. Beck's 1960s cognitive therapy (Beck, 1964) and the first manual (Beck, Rush, Shaw & Emery, 1979). Guideline/professional definitions (NICE; BABCP) describe CBT as a family of evidence-based talking therapies working on the thought–feeling–behaviour links that maintain distress. Common core across sources: formulation-driven, collaborative, present-focused, targeting maintaining mechanisms. Wording original/paraphrased — no fabricated quotations.
A teaching line such as "CBT helps you notice and change unhelpful thoughts and behaviours" is a simplification for patients, not the formal definition; don't present it as one in a viva.

2.2 CBT in simple language

CBT starts from a deceptively simple observation: it is often not the event itself that upsets us, but the meaning we give the event. Two people face the same setback; one feels crushed, the other shrugs — because they interpreted it differently. CBT works on those interpretations and on the behaviours (especially avoidance) that keep problems alive. Importantly, CBT does not claim all distress comes from "irrational thoughts": sometimes the thought is accurate and the person faces a genuine difficulty, and then CBT uses practical problem-solving, not thought-challenging.

What CBT is NOT (clear this early)

  • Not "positive thinking": CBT seeks balanced, evidence-based thoughts, not forced optimism.
  • Not advice-giving or reassurance: the therapist helps the patient discover, not hands out answers.
  • Not "your thoughts are wrong": it respects that beliefs made sense given the person's history; it tests them, not shames them.
  • Not purely about thoughts: behaviour, body, environment, relationships, culture, learning history and medical factors all matter.

2.5 How cognition and behaviour work together

  • Changing a thought can be insufficient: if avoidance continues, the person never experiences disconfirmation, so the fear belief survives despite "understanding."
  • Experiments beat reassurance: a planned experiment gives first-hand evidence; reassurance is second-hand, fades, and can become a safety behaviour.
  • Action changes belief: doing the feared thing and surviving provides vivid, emotionally credible evidence words cannot.
  • Some approaches change the relationship to thoughts: third-wave methods (mindfulness/ACT-style) focus less on disputing content and more on noticing/defusing/accepting thoughts — useful when disputation stalls.
Panic: a man believes "a racing heart means I'll have a heart attack." Reassurance helps for minutes. A behavioural experiment — deliberately running upstairs to raise his heart rate and seeing nothing catastrophic happen — gives durable, felt evidence that recalibrates the belief.

Part 4 · Core Principles of CBT (in depth)

Each principle: meaning, why it matters, how it's applied, a micro-example, and the common mistake. (Grounded in Beck, 2011, Ch. 1.)

1. Cognitive conceptualisation (the engine)

Meaning: an evolving, individualised map of the patient's thoughts, beliefs, behaviours and their origins. Why: technique choice flows from the map, not the diagnostic label — two people with the same diagnosis may need different methods. Application: the therapist drafts a formulation after assessment and updates it each session as new data arrive. Example: realising a "depressed" patient's inactivity is driven by a belief "if I can't do it perfectly, there's no point" reframes the whole plan toward that assumption. Mistake: running a protocol with no individual conceptualisation.

2. Collaborative empiricism

Meaning: therapist + patient act as a research team testing beliefs against evidence. Why: shared ownership raises engagement and makes change durable; the patient learns a transferable skill. Application: "Let's look at this together — what does the evidence actually show?" Mistake: becoming the expert who "corrects" the patient (breeds resistance).

3. Guided discovery

Meaning: helping the patient reach new perspectives through questions and experiments, not lectures. Why: self-generated insight is far more convincing than being told. Mistake: "leading the witness" — asking questions that merely extract the therapist's opinion.

4. Socratic questioning

Meaning: a sequence of open questions (evidence? alternatives? implications? usefulness?) that broadens a narrow conclusion. Why: widens rigid thinking without confrontation. Mistake: using "Socratic" questions to trap the patient into agreeing.

5. Structured & goal-oriented

Meaning: sessions follow a structure (check-in → agenda → work → summary → homework → feedback) aimed at agreed goals. Why: efficiency and focus in time-limited therapy. Mistake: rigid structure during a crisis session.

6. Present-focused

Meaning: primary focus on current maintaining factors; history is used to understand, not to dwell. Why: maintaining factors are what we can change now. Mistake: endless history-taking with no change work.

7. Individualised formulation · 8. Skills acquisition · 9. Homework

7 — the plan fits this person (mistake: one-size-fits-all). 8 — therapy teaches transferable skills so the patient becomes their own therapist (mistake: fostering dependence). 9 — most change happens between sessions; homework generalises learning to real life (mistake: assigning it with no rationale → non-completion).

10. Behavioural testing · 11. Monitoring · 12. Adaptation · 13. Relapse prevention

10 — beliefs are tested through action/experiments, not only discussion. 11 — routine measures track change. 12 — if the patient isn't improving, revisit the formulation (don't just repeat the technique). 13 — build skills/plans to maintain gains before ending.

14. Autonomy · 15. Alliance · 16. Cultural sensitivity · 17. Evidence-informed

14 — the patient learns to run the methods independently. 15 — warmth, empathy, trust are necessary (if not sufficient). 16 — read beliefs/behaviours within the person's culture, language, circumstances (mistake: labelling culturally-normative beliefs "distortions"). 17 — integrate best research + clinical expertise + patient values.

5.2 Cognitive conceptualisation diagram

Part 6 · The CBT Therapeutic Relationship

CBT depends on a collaborative alliance: empathy, transparency, shared goals/agenda, validation, and genuine teamwork. Below are skilled vs. less-helpful responses.

Patient: "I did the thought record but it felt pointless — nothing changed."

Therapist (skilled): "Thanks for telling me honestly — that's really useful. Let's look at one together and find where it got stuck; sometimes a thought record points us to a deeper belief that needs a different tool."

Why it works: validates, stays collaborative, treats "failure" as data. Less helpful: "You need to do them properly" (blaming, non-collaborative).

Patient: "I don't think talking about thoughts will help someone like me."

Therapist: "That doubt makes sense given what you've been through. Would you treat the next two sessions as an experiment — we test whether this helps you, and you decide?"

Why it works: validates, offers autonomy, models empiricism. Less helpful: "CBT is evidence-based, it will work" (dismissive).

Part 7 · The Complete CBT Session Structure

A typical session follows a predictable, flexible structure (Beck, 2011).

  1. Preparation — review notes, last session, homework set.
  2. Mood/symptom check — a brief rating or measure.
  3. Risk / major changes — screened when indicated.
  4. Bridge from last session — "What stood out from last time?"
  5. Homework review — always (skipping it teaches patients it doesn't matter).
  6. Agenda setting — collaboratively pick 1–2 targets.
  7. Target problem — choose the focus.
  8. Intervention — apply the chosen technique.
  9. Summarise learning — the patient states the take-away.
  10. New homework — agreed, started in-session where possible.
  11. Feedback — "Anything I got wrong or that bothered you today?"
  12. Record & plan next.
Used every session: check-in, homework review, agenda, intervention, summary, new homework, feedback. Flexible/conditional: risk review, formal measures, and which intervention — these depend on the patient and protocol.

Five sample scenarios (fictional, with therapist language)

A. Standard 50-minute session

Check-in (mood 4/10) → bridge ("What stayed with you?") → homework review (thought record done) → agenda ("work stress" + "sleep") → intervention (evaluate hot thought "I'll be fired," build balanced response, set a workplace experiment) → summary ("So what's the take-home?") → homework (experiment + sleep-window plan) → feedback.

B. Short 25-minute session

One target only. "We've limited time, so let's pick the single most useful thing today." Check-in → quick homework review → one technique (set up a behavioural experiment) → summary + homework + feedback.

C. Patient arrives distressed

Structure bends. Validate & contain first ("Let's slow down — you're safe here"), assess risk, use a grounding skill, then collaboratively decide whether the crisis is today's agenda. Do not force the planned technique.

D. Homework not completed

Explore non-judgementally: lack of opportunity? unclear rationale? too hard? avoidance? "No-lose" framing — understanding the barrier is itself useful. Re-grade, start in session, plan reminders.

E. Planned intervention no longer appropriate

If new information changes the picture (fresh stressor, safety concern, formulation was off), re-prioritise transparently: "Given what you've told me, today it's more useful to…", and revisit the formulation.

8.1 Assessment & Monitoring Techniques

Functional analysis (ABC: Antecedent–Behaviour–Consequence)
Definition: systematically mapping what comes immediately before a behaviour (antecedent/trigger), the behaviour itself, and what follows (consequences, short- and long-term).
Rationale: behaviour is maintained by its consequences; the ABC reveals the reinforcement keeping a problem going (usually relief = negative reinforcement).
Indications: almost any behavioural problem — avoidance, compulsions, anger outbursts, self-harm urges, substance use, bingeing.
When NOT / modify: acute crisis or high distress (stabilise first); very abstract patients may need concrete, written episodes.
Pre-assessment: ability to recall specific episodes; literacy for written logs (use verbal if not).
Preparation: ABC worksheet; agree which behaviour to track.
Step-by-step: (1) pick one specific recent episode; (2) elicit the antecedent in detail (where, when, with whom, internal/external); (3) describe the exact behaviour; (4) map immediate consequence (relief?) and later consequence (problem worsens?); (5) repeat over several episodes; (6) identify the maintaining pattern; (7) target the consequence (e.g., block the relief-giving safety behaviour).
Therapist language: "Walk me through one specific time this week — what set it off, exactly what you did, and what happened right after, and then later?"
Trigger: alone at night, intrusive doubt "did I lock the door?" → Behaviour: check the lock 15×. → Immediate consequence: anxiety drops (relief). → Long-term: doubt returns stronger, checking grows. Pattern: checking is negatively reinforced → it maintains OCD.
Common errors: analysing in the abstract instead of specific episodes; missing the long-term consequence; stopping after one episode.
Evaluating outcome: clarity of the maintaining pattern; patient can predict their own cycle.
Worksheet: Functional Analysis / ABC (Part 14). Adaptations: anger (trigger→appraisal→aggression→consequence); substance use (cue→craving→use→relief).
Self-monitoring (mood / thought / activity / symptom logs)
Definition: the patient records target experiences in real time (mood ratings, activities, thoughts, symptoms).
Rationale: gives accurate data (not retrospective guesswork), raises self-awareness, builds the formulation, and provides a baseline against which to measure change.
Indications: depression (activity–mood), anxiety (worry/panic), eating, sleep, substance use. When NOT/modify: in health anxiety or OCD, symptom-monitoring can increase focus on the body/doubt — use selectively and time-limited.
Step-by-step: define the target → agree a simple record (0–10 ratings help) → log in the moment, not from memory → review the pattern together → use the data to plan interventions.
Therapist language: "For the next week, each evening jot what you did each hour and rate your mood 0–10 — we'll look for patterns together."
A week of activity–mood logging shows mood is lowest on days spent entirely at home and higher after any social contact → directly justifies behavioural activation.
Common errors: retrospective filling-in; over-complex forms; not reviewing the data (teaches the patient it was pointless). Rating scales screen/monitor — they are not diagnoses.
Worksheet: Activity & Mood Monitoring; Thought Record; Sleep Diary.

8.2 Cognitive Techniques

Identifying automatic thoughts
Definition: catching the quick, involuntary, situation-specific thoughts/images that pop up and drive emotion — often in "shorthand" ("Oh no!", a mental picture).
Rationale: these thoughts are the most accessible level of cognition and the most directly linked to emotion; you cannot evaluate a thought you haven't captured.
Step-by-step: (1) find a recent moment of strong emotion; (2) ask "What went through your mind just then?"; (3) if it's "shorthand," ask its meaning ("What did 'oh no' mean?"); (4) ask about images ("Any picture in your mind?"); (5) separate thoughts from emotions; (6) rate how much the thought is believed (0–100%).
Therapist language (from the model): "What was going through your mind right then?" · "If that thought were true, what would it mean to/about you?" · "What else went through your mind?"
Patient feels sad in the library. Thought: "They're so inefficient, the system is terrible" (frustration 90%). On enquiry the hotter, more personal thought emerges: "I'll never get my work done — I'm going to fail." That is the thought worth evaluating.
Common errors: accepting an emotion ("I felt bad") as a thought; stopping at a surface thought instead of the "hot" one; jumping to challenge before fully eliciting.
Worksheet: 7-Column Thought Record. Source: Beck (2011), Ch. 9.
Evaluating automatic thoughts (examining the evidence) & thought records
Definition: testing a hot thought against evidence to reach a more balanced (not merely positive) conclusion, usually via a written thought record.
Rationale: distorted thoughts are treated as hypotheses, not facts; examining evidence loosens over-rigid conclusions and relieves emotion.
Step-by-step: (1) select a key/distressing thought; (2) rate belief % and emotion %; (3) list evidence for; (4) list evidence against (the hard part — ask "what would you tell a friend?"); (5) build a balanced alternative that accounts for all the evidence; (6) re-rate belief and emotion; (7) if belief barely moved, look for a deeper belief or switch to a behavioural experiment.
Therapist language: "What's the evidence this is true? What's the evidence it isn't? If a friend had this thought, what would you say to them? What's a more balanced way to see it?"
Hot thought "I'm a complete failure" (belief 85%, sadness 80%). Evidence for: failed one exam. Against: passed many before, colleagues value my work, one exam ≠ whole life. Balanced: "I failed this exam, which is disappointing, but one setback doesn't make me a failure — I can re-sit." Re-rate: belief 30%, sadness 40%.
This is NOT forced positive thinking, arguing, or denying real problems. If the thought is true (a genuine difficulty), stop disputing and switch to problem-solving. Don't rush to a "balanced thought" before the hot thought is truly elicited.
Evaluating outcome: drop in belief/emotion %; patient can run a record alone. Worksheet: 7-Column Thought Record; Evidence For & Against. Source: Beck (2011), Ch. 11–12.
Cognitive restructuring (the umbrella cognitive-change technique)
Definition: the overarching process of identifying, examining and changing unhelpful or inaccurate thoughts, assumptions and beliefs so that thinking becomes more balanced, realistic and useful. It is the "parent" technique that organises many of the specific tools below (thought records, Socratic questioning, evidence-examining, decatastrophising, continuum, reattribution, downward arrow, core-belief work) into one coherent sequence.
Rationale: in the cognitive model, distorted appraisals drive and maintain distressing emotion and behaviour. By treating thoughts as hypotheses to be tested rather than facts to be obeyed, restructuring loosens rigid conclusions, reduces distress, and frees the person to act differently. It is not about replacing "negative" with "positive" — it is about replacing inaccurate/unhelpful with accurate/helpful.
Indications: depression (self-critical/hopeless cognitions), anxiety disorders (threat over-estimation), anger (hostile appraisals), low self-esteem (negative core beliefs), and most presentations with a clear cognitive maintaining factor.
When NOT / modify: when the thought is true (a real problem) — switch to problem-solving; in acute crisis or very high distress (stabilise first); with strongly held delusional beliefs (use the gentler, collaborative CBTp stance, not disputation); early in therapy before the patient can catch thoughts (teach identification first). Do not restructure prematurely — the hot thought must be fully elicited first.
Pre-assessment: the patient can identify automatic thoughts and rate belief/emotion; a shared cognitive formulation exists; the target thought is genuinely distorted/unhelpful (not simply an accurate perception of a real difficulty).
Preparation: a thought record or worksheet; agreement that we are testing the thought, not the person; the patient's own recent, emotionally live example.
Step-by-step: (1) Identify the hot automatic thought in a specific situation; (2) rate belief (0–100%) and emotion (0–100%); (3) categorise the thinking pattern if useful (e.g., all-or-nothing, catastrophising, mind-reading); (4) examine it — evidence for and against, alternative explanations, Socratic questions, decatastrophising; (5) choose a supplementary tool if needed (continuum for all-or-nothing, pie chart for over-responsibility, downward arrow if a deeper belief is driving it); (6) construct a balanced alternative thought that accounts for all the evidence; (7) re-rate belief and emotion; (8) test the new thought in real life via a behavioural experiment and consolidate with a coping card; (9) if belief barely shifts, suspect an underlying core belief and move to belief-level work.
Therapist language: "Let's treat that thought as a hypothesis, not a fact — what's the evidence for it? Against it? If a friend said this to you, what would you tell them? What's a fairer way to see it that fits all the evidence? How much do you believe the new version now?"
Situation: didn't get a reply to a text. Hot thought: "He hates me now" (belief 80%, anxiety 70%, pattern = mind-reading + catastrophising). Evidence for: no reply in 6 hours. Against: he's at work; he replied warmly yesterday; people get busy. Alternative: "He's probably busy; one slow reply doesn't mean he hates me — I'll know more when he answers." Re-rate: belief 25%, anxiety 30%. Experiment: wait without sending an anxious follow-up; observe the outcome.
Common errors: (1) forcing "positive thinking" or arguing the patient into agreement (breeds resistance); (2) restructuring a thought that is actually true instead of problem-solving; (3) jumping to a balanced thought before the hot thought is fully elicited; (4) staying only at the surface when a core belief is driving the thought; (5) stopping at the written conclusion without a real-life experiment to make it stick.
Evaluating outcome: drop in belief/emotion %, the patient can run restructuring independently, reduced frequency/impact of the target thought, and behavioural change that follows the new belief.
Worksheet: Cognitive Restructuring Worksheet (Part 14) — plus the 7-Column Thought Record. Adaptations: simplify to a 3-column (situation–thought–balanced response) version for low literacy or early sessions; visual/continuum formats for all-or-nothing thinking; for psychosis, use collaborative, non-confrontational exploration of alternatives rather than disputation. Source: Beck (2011), Chs. 11–13.

Emotional reasoning"I feel it, so it must be true""I feel worthless, so I am."

Labelling a distortion is a starting point, not the end. Don't use it to dismiss a thought ("that's just catastrophising") — still examine the evidence and build a balanced view, and check the thought isn't actually accurate.
Socratic questioning & guided discovery
Definition: a series of genuine, open questions that help the patient themselves arrive at a broader view, rather than being told.
Rationale: self-generated conclusions are far more convincing and durable than the therapist's assertions.
Step-by-step (the four question types): (1) evidence — "What makes you think that? What points the other way?"; (2) alternatives — "Is there another way to see this?"; (3) implications/decatastrophising — "If it were true, what then? How would you cope?"; (4) usefulness — "What's the effect of thinking this way? Is it helping you?"; then summarise and synthesise.
Common error: "fake Socratic" questions that merely funnel the patient to the therapist's pre-decided answer, or that feel like cross-examination. Questions must be genuine and curious.
Worksheet: works alongside the Thought Record. Source: Beck (2011).
Decatastrophising & probability re-estimation
Definition: examining a feared prediction by estimating the worst, best and most-likely outcomes, plus one's ability to cope if the worst happened.
Rationale: anxiety inflates both the probability and the awfulness of feared outcomes while underestimating coping; this technique recalibrates both.
Step-by-step: name the feared prediction → "What's the worst that could happen? The best? The most realistic?" → "If the worst did happen, how would you cope, and who/what would help?" → re-rate anxiety.
Fear: "If I speak up in the meeting I'll blank and everyone will think I'm stupid." Worst: forget a point. Most likely: pause, glance at notes, continue. Coping: "I'd take a breath and carry on; people forget it in minutes." Anxiety 85% → 45%.
Don't dismiss the feared outcome ("that won't happen") — that's reassurance. Realistically appraise it and build a coping plan.
Worksheet: Decatastrophising.
Continuum · double-standard · responsibility pie · cost–benefit · reattribution
Continuum: for all-or-nothing thinking — place the black-and-white label on a 0–100 scale and locate oneself and others realistically ("If 'total failure' is 100%, where do murderers sit? Where do you really sit?").
Double-standard: apply the (kinder) standard the patient would apply to a friend ("Would you call your friend a failure for this?").
Responsibility pie: for excessive self-blame — list all contributing factors, assign rough %s in a pie, and see the patient's realistic (smaller) share.
Cost–benefit: weigh the advantages and disadvantages of holding a belief or doing a behaviour — often reveals the belief/behaviour costs more than it gives.
Reattribution: generate a fuller, more accurate set of causes for an event instead of a single self-blaming one.
Downward arrow → intermediate & core beliefs; positive data log; coping cards
Downward arrow: starting from an automatic thought, repeatedly ask "If that were true, what would it mean (about you)?" until you reach the underlying assumption and core belief.
Rationale: surface thoughts are fed by deeper beliefs; lasting change often needs to reach them.
"I got a B" → "it means I'm slipping" → "it means I can't keep up" → "it means I'm not good enough" (core belief).
Core-belief change: identify old belief + % → define a new, more adaptive belief → gather evidence for the new belief daily (positive data log) → reframe "evidence" that seemed to support the old one → strengthen with historical review and experiments → carry a coping card (a portable reminder of the balanced conclusion).
Worksheets: Downward Arrow; Core Belief; Positive Data Log; Coping Card. Source: Beck (2011), Ch. 13–14.
Imagery techniques
Definition & use: working with distressing mental images/memories — e.g., transforming or rescripting a recurrent image to a less threatening ending — where the evidence and the treatment model support it. Caution: trauma imagery work requires specific training, a suitable protocol, stabilisation and risk assessment; it is not a casual technique.

8.3 Behavioural Techniques

Behavioural activation & activity scheduling
Definition: systematically planning and re-introducing valued, pleasurable and mastery activities to counter the withdrawal and inactivity of depression.
Rationale: depression → withdrawal → loss of reward/achievement → lower mood → more withdrawal (a vicious cycle). Scheduling activity restores reinforcement and often lifts mood before the thinking changes — so it is frequently the first intervention in depression.
Indications: depression, low mood, apathy, post-illness deconditioning. When NOT/modify: an exhausted or severely depressed patient needs tiny, graded steps — overloading causes failure and more self-criticism.
Pre-assessment: current activity pattern (a daily log), values, barriers, baseline mood.
Step-by-step: (1) monitor activity & mood for a week; (2) identify what's missing (mastery/achievement, pleasure, social, routine); (3) collaboratively schedule a few small activities at set times; (4) rate predicted vs actual mastery/pleasure (0–10); (5) problem-solve barriers; (6) grade up gradually; (7) link activity to mood in review.
Therapist language: "Depression tells you to wait until you feel like it — but with depression, action usually comes before motivation. Shall we pick one small thing to schedule tomorrow and see how your mood responds?"
"Raj" schedules a 15-minute morning walk (predicted pleasure 2/10). Actual: 5/10 — "better than I expected." This disconfirms "nothing will help" and builds momentum for more activity.
Common errors: starting too big; scheduling only pleasure (ignore mastery); not reviewing predicted-vs-actual (that's where the learning is). Evaluating: activity count ↑, mood ratings ↑, "nothing helps" belief ↓. Worksheet: Activity & Mood Monitoring; Behavioural Activation Plan; Graded Task. Source: Beck (2011), Ch. 6.
Graded exposure (in-vivo, imaginal, interoceptive) & ERP
Definition: systematically and repeatedly approaching a feared situation, memory, or bodily sensation — in a planned, graded way, while dropping avoidance and safety behaviours — so new, safer learning can occur. In-vivo = real situations; imaginal = feared images/memories; interoceptive = feared body sensations; ERP (exposure & response prevention) = facing OCD triggers while not performing the compulsion.
Rationale: fear is maintained by avoidance (negative reinforcement) and safety behaviours that block disconfirmation. Approaching the cue lets the person learn that the feared outcome doesn't occur (inhibitory learning) and that anxiety is tolerable and falls over time (habituation).
Indications: phobias, panic/agoraphobia, social anxiety, OCD (as ERP), PTSD (specialised imaginal). When NOT/modify: never coerce; adapt for safety and the formulation; trauma reliving needs training/stabilisation; some medical conditions limit interoceptive tasks (take medical advice).
Pre-assessment: clear formulation of the feared outcome; list of avoidances and safety behaviours; medical clearance if relevant; a strong rationale the patient accepts.
Step-by-step: (1) build a fear hierarchy (situations rated 0–100 SUDs); (2) agree the specific belief/prediction to test; (3) start at a manageable step; (4) enter the situation and stay until learning occurs (anxiety drops or the prediction is disconfirmed); (5) drop safety behaviours deliberately; (6) repeat, varied and frequent; (7) review what was learned; (8) climb the hierarchy.
Therapist language: "The goal isn't to white-knuckle it — it's to learn something new. What do you predict will happen? Let's test it, and drop the water bottle this time so you find out it was never what kept you safe."
Dog phobia hierarchy: look at dog photos (20) → watch a video (35) → stand 10 m from a leashed dog (55) → same room (70) → stroke a calm dog (90). The patient learns the feared outcome (being bitten / unbearable panic) doesn't occur; fear falls.
Common errors: letting the patient keep safety behaviours (they credit the crutch, not reality → no learning); exposures too brief or too rare; jumping too high on the hierarchy; using it as reassurance. Never force or trick the patient.
Evaluating: SUDs fall across repetitions; belief/prediction % drops; avoidance ↓, functioning ↑. Worksheets: Fear Hierarchy; Exposure Planning & Outcome; Safety-Behaviour Map; OCD ERP. Adaptations: panic → interoceptive; OCD → ERP; PTSD → imaginal reliving (specialist).
Behavioural experiments
Definition: a planned, real-world test of a specific belief/prediction, designed collaboratively, with the outcome recorded and the belief updated.
Rationale: experiential learning changes beliefs more powerfully and durably than verbal discussion; the patient discovers the evidence.
Step-by-step: (1) state the belief + prediction and rate belief %; (2) design a concrete test (what, when, where, with whom); (3) specify what result would support vs. refute the belief; (4) agree which safety behaviours to drop; (5) run it; (6) record the actual outcome; (7) draw the conclusion and re-rate the belief.
Distinct from exposure & BA: an experiment tests a belief (any emotion/prediction); exposure creates new fear learning; activation restores reinforcement. They overlap (an exposure can be framed as an experiment) but have different primary aims.
Belief: "If I ask a question in class, people will laugh" (80%). Experiment: ask one genuine question today, watch reactions, don't over-rehearse (dropped safety behaviour). Outcome: no one laughed; two people nodded. Re-rate: 25%.
Common errors: vague prediction (nothing to confirm/refute); keeping safety behaviours; not recording the actual result. Worksheet: Behavioural Experiment Record.
Problem-solving · assertiveness · relaxation/breathing · stimulus control · reducing safety behaviours · sleep & pacing
Structured problem-solving: for real, solvable difficulties — define the problem precisely → brainstorm many solutions → weigh pros/cons → choose and plan one → act → review. (Use when the worrying thought is true.)
Assertiveness & social skills: role-play saying no, making requests, giving/receiving criticism; reduces passive/aggressive patterns.
Relaxation & breathing retraining: for general over-arousal. Caution: in panic, slow breathing used to "prevent" catastrophe becomes a safety behaviour that blocks learning — use for general arousal, not as a crutch during feared sensations.
Stimulus control: change the cues that trigger a behaviour (e.g., bed only for sleep in CBT-I; remove alcohol from the house).
Reducing safety behaviours & avoidance: deliberately dropping the subtle "just-in-case" acts so reality can disconfirm the fear.
Sleep interventions & activity pacing: stimulus control, sleep restriction, consistent rise time (insomnia); graded, time-based pacing (chronic fatigue/pain) so activity isn't "boom-and-bust."

Core-belief modification; schema-level work within CBT; imagery rescripting (where evidence/model support it); experiments targeting deeply held beliefs; relapse-signature work; problem-solving under uncertainty; rehearsing difficult situations; testing predictions in real life. Clearly separate standard CBT methods from techniques that originate in related therapies (schema therapy, ACT, DBT) — name the source and work within your competence and supervision.

Motivational enhancement within CBT (explore ambivalence, link change to the patient's own values); collaborative goal-setting; graded commitment (start with the smallest viable step); strengths-based reinforcement; reviewing homework barriers without blame; using feedback and progress data to adjust the plan.

Part 10 · CBT for Different Disorders (Full Template)

Each disorder has its own maintaining model — the same protocol does not fit all. Template per disorder: Presentation · CBT model · Assessment · Formulation (fictional) · Goals (short/intermediate/long) · Techniques · Session plan · Limitations.

1. Major Depressive Disorder
Presentation: persistent low mood/anhedonia, fatigue, sleep/appetite change, concentration problems, guilt/worthlessness, hopelessness; Beck's cognitive triad — negative view of self, world, future. Always screen suicide risk.
CBT model (Beck): negative automatic thoughts + withdrawal/inactivity → loss of reward & mastery → lower mood → more negative thinking & withdrawal (vicious cycle); rumination deepens it.
Assessment: activity–mood log, cognitive triad, rumination, hopelessness, sleep; risk; monitor with PHQ-9/BDI (monitoring, not diagnosis).
Formulation — "Raj," 34: post-redundancy; core belief "I'm a failure"; withdrawn, ruminating; protective — supportive partner, motivation. Cycle: inactivity + self-criticism → low mood → more inactivity.
Goals: short — 1 valued activity/day, start activity log; intermediate — evaluate self-critical thoughts, re-engage socially; long — mood in normal range, return to work, functional recovery; relapse-prevention — early-warning plan.
Techniques: behavioural activation first, activity scheduling, thought records on self-critical cognitions, problem-solving, core-belief work later, relapse prevention.
Session plan: S1–2 assess/formulate/psychoeducate + BA rationale; S3–5 activity scheduling + graded mastery/pleasure; S6–10 cognitive restructuring of triad thoughts; S11–14 core beliefs + relapse prevention.
Limitations: severe depression may need an activation-led/low-intensity start, medication, or crisis care first; persistent hopelessness/suicidality needs active risk management and possibly higher-intensity input.
2. Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD)
Presentation: excessive, hard-to-control worry across many domains; restlessness, muscle tension, poor sleep, irritability, fatigue.
CBT model: chronic worry maintained by (a) positive beliefs about worry ("worrying keeps me prepared/safe"), (b) negative beliefs ("my worry is uncontrollable/harmful"), (c) intolerance of uncertainty, and (d) avoidance + reassurance-seeking + checking.
Techniques: worry-type sorting (practical→problem-solving; hypothetical→let-go/postponement); worry postponement; evaluate meta-beliefs via experiments (e.g., a "worry-free day" to test "if I don't worry, something bad will happen"); build uncertainty tolerance; drop reassurance/checking. Session plan: assess worry process → psychoeducate the model → worry sorting & postponement → meta-belief experiments → uncertainty-tolerance tasks → relapse prevention.
Limitations: relaxation alone is weak for GAD — target the worry process, not just arousal.
3. Panic Disorder
Presentation: recurrent unexpected panic attacks + persistent fear of further attacks and/or their meaning; often avoidance.
CBT model (Clark, 1986): benign bodily sensations (palpitations, dizziness) are catastrophically misinterpreted ("heart attack," "going mad," "I'll faint") → escalating fear → more sensations → "proof" of catastrophe → vicious cycle; safety behaviours (sitting down, carrying water) prevent disconfirmation.
Techniques: psychoeducation on the cycle; interoceptive exposure (induce feared sensations deliberately — e.g., over-breathing, spinning, stairs); behavioural experiments to test the catastrophe; drop safety behaviours; reduce situational avoidance. Session plan: S1–2 assess/formulate; S3–4 psychoeducation + myth-busting; S5–7 interoceptive exposure + experiments; S8–10 drop safety behaviours + in-vivo; S11–14 consolidate + relapse plan.
Limitations: rule out genuine cardiac/medical causes first; take medical advice before interoceptive tasks if there's a relevant condition.
4. Agoraphobia
Model: avoidance of situations where escape or help feels difficult (crowds, transport, queues), maintained by anticipatory anxiety + safety behaviours. Techniques: graded in-vivo exposure hierarchy, dropping safety behaviours, behavioural experiments; usually combined with panic treatment. Limitation: may need accompanied early steps, then faded support.
5. Social Anxiety Disorder
CBT model (Clark & Wells, 1995): in feared social situations attention turns inward (self-focused attention), the person builds a distorted negative self-image ("I look like I'm shaking/boring"), uses safety behaviours (rehearsing, avoiding eye contact), and engages in anticipatory worry + post-event rumination ("the post-mortem").
Techniques: shift attention externally; drop safety behaviours; video/observer feedback experiments (reality vs. feared self-image); address pre/post-event processing; graded exposure to feared social tasks. Session plan: assess → formulate the self-focused-attention model → external-focus practice → drop safety behaviours in experiments → video feedback → tackle post-event rumination → relapse prevention.
6. Specific Phobias
Model: conditioned fear + avoidance preventing new learning; often inflated probability/cost of harm. Techniques: graded in-vivo exposure (often rapid, sometimes single-session for circumscribed phobias), behavioural experiments, dropping avoidance; modelling. Limitation: blood-injection-injury phobia needs applied tension (to counter fainting), not relaxation.
7. Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder (OCD)
CBT model (Salkovskis): normal intrusive thoughts are misappraised as signalling serious responsibility/threat ("thinking it means I want it / I must prevent harm") → distress → compulsions/neutralising/reassurance → short-term relief (negative reinforcement) → intrusions return and strengthen.
Repository source: David A. Clark, CBT for OCD (scanned PDF — see Part 24). Techniques: ERP (exposure to triggers + prevention of compulsions), re-appraising inflated responsibility, behavioural experiments (e.g., "if I don't check, does harm occur?"), surveys of "normal" intrusions. Worksheets: OCD cycle; ERP planning. Session plan: assess obsessions/compulsions → formulate appraisal-&-responsibility model → build ERP hierarchy → graded ERP with no neutralising → responsibility experiments → relapse prevention.
Limitations/errors: never give reassurance (it's a compulsion); mental compulsions/rumination are easy to miss; start ERP low on the hierarchy; comorbid depression may need addressing first.
8. Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)
CBT model (Ehlers & Clark, 2000): a sense of current threat arising from (a) a poorly elaborated, fragmented trauma memory that is easily triggered, and (b) excessively negative appraisals ("I'm permanently damaged," "nowhere is safe"), maintained by avoidance, rumination, and safety behaviours.
Techniques (trauma-focused CBT): reclaiming-your-life activities; imaginal reliving / narrative with updating of hotspots; stimulus discrimination (then vs now); site visits; cognitive restructuring of appraisals. Session plan: stabilise & psychoeducate → reclaim-life → memory work (reliving/updating) → discrimination of triggers → appraisal work → relapse prevention.
Limitations: requires specific training, supervision and a suitable protocol; assess risk and dissociation; stabilise first; not a technique for the untrained.
9–10. Health Anxiety / Illness Anxiety
Model: catastrophic misinterpretation of normal bodily signs as evidence of serious illness, maintained by body-checking, reassurance-seeking (doctors, family, internet), symptom-monitoring and avoidance. Techniques: reduce checking/reassurance/online-searching; behavioural experiments; re-attribute sensations to benign causes; attention retraining. Worksheet: Health Anxiety monitoring & experiment.
11. Insomnia (CBT-I)
Model: conditioned arousal (bed = wakefulness/worry) + maladaptive habits (irregular schedule, long time awake in bed, daytime naps) + unhelpful beliefs ("I must get 8 hours or I can't function"). Techniques: stimulus control (bed only for sleep; leave if awake), sleep restriction/compression, consistent rise time, sleep-hygiene, cognitive work on sleep beliefs. Worksheet: Sleep Diary.
12. Eating Disorders (CBT-E)
Model (Fairburn, CBT-E): over-evaluation of shape, weight and their control drives dietary restraint → binge–purge cycles, body-checking/avoidance, and mood-driven eating. Techniques: regular eating pattern, self-monitoring, addressing the over-evaluation of shape/weight, behavioural experiments, body-image work. Limitation: medical monitoring is essential; low weight/medical instability or severe cases may need specialist/day-patient/inpatient care.
13. Substance Use & Relapse Prevention
Model: cues (people/places/moods) → cravings + permissive beliefs ("just one won't hurt") → use → reinforcement (relief/reward). Techniques: functional analysis of use, identifying high-risk situations, craving-surfing/coping, refusal skills, challenging permissive beliefs, a relapse-prevention plan; motivational work on ambivalence. Worksheet: Substance trigger & craving record. Limitation: integrate with medical detox/pharmacotherapy and social support where needed.
14. Psychosis

Covered in depth in Part 11 (repository priority source: Hagen, Turkington, Berge & Gråwe).

15. Bipolar Disorder
CBT as an adjunct to medication: psychoeducation, mood monitoring, identifying personal prodromes/early-warning signs, regulating routines and sleep (social-rhythm ideas), action plans for early intervention, and tackling depressive cognitions. Limitation: CBT does not replace pharmacological management; strongest evidence is for relapse prevention and adjunctive use. Caution with activation during emerging mania.
16–22. Chronic pain · Anger · BDD · Hoarding · Somatic presentations · Personality-related difficulties
  • Chronic pain: pain→fear-avoidance→deconditioning→more pain/disability cycle; graded activity, pacing, cognitive work on pain/catastrophising beliefs, acceptance strategies.
  • Anger: trigger → appraisal ("that's disrespect/injustice") → arousal → aggression → consequences; techniques: trigger/appraisal analysis, cognitive reappraisal, arousal reduction, assertiveness, planned time-out.
  • Body Dysmorphic Disorder: appraisal of a perceived defect + mirror-checking/comparing/avoidance/camouflaging; ERP-style experiments, attention retraining, reducing checking; caution re: reassurance and cosmetic procedures.
  • Hoarding: beliefs about possessions ("I might need it," "it's part of me") + distress on discarding + avoidance of decisions; graded sorting/discarding experiments, decision-making skills.
  • Somatic symptom presentations: symptom focus + checking + illness beliefs; re-attribution, reduce checking, graded activity; rule out medical causes, work with the physician.
  • Personality-related difficulties: schema-level and longer-term CBT; often need DBT/schema-therapy elements (emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness) — name the source and work within competence/supervision.
Across all disorders: do not assume one protocol fits all; match technique to mechanism; attend to comorbidity, medical factors, culture and risk; when progress stalls, revisit the formulation before blaming "non-response."

Part 11 · CBT for Psychosis (CBTp)

Priority repository source: Hagen, Turkington, Berge & Gråwe, CBT for Psychosis: A Symptom-based Approach. Supplemented by current guidance (NICE recommends CBTp in psychosis/schizophrenia) and the normalising tradition (Turkington et al.). Established recommendations are distinguished from areas of clinical judgement.

Rationale: CBTp does not try to argue someone out of their experiences. It targets the distress and life-disruption linked to unusual experiences by building a collaborative, normalising understanding and gently testing threat-related appraisals.

Core stance & methods (in depth)

  • Engagement first — warmth, patience, the person's own goals; go at their pace; many sessions may be about building trust.
  • Collaborative, shared formulation of how triggers, experiences, appraisals, emotions and coping interact.
  • Normalising — placing unusual experiences on a continuum with ordinary experience (stress, sleep loss, sensory deprivation can produce similar phenomena), to reduce fear, shame and isolation — where clinically appropriate.
  • Assess the appraisal, not just the experience — distress depends on meaning, conviction, preoccupation, and the perceived power/threat of voices or beliefs.
  • Work with threat beliefs & safety behaviours; explore alternative explanations collaboratively (never imposed).
  • Coping strategies for voices — focusing/refocusing, scheduling/activity, reducing isolation, reducing substance use, responding differently to the voice.
  • Carefully-formulated behavioural experiments, adapted to safety and engagement (e.g., gently testing a voice's predicted "power").
  • Negative symptoms & activity — graded activity, addressing defeatist beliefs ("there's no point"), when indicated.
  • Relapse prevention and integration with psychiatric/multidisciplinary care and medication.
Fictional case — "Sam," 24: hears a critical voice he believes is an all-powerful entity that will harm him if disobeyed; stays home, avoids eye contact (safety behaviours). Shared formulation: sleep loss + isolation → more voice activity → threat appraisal → avoidance → more isolation. Work: normalising, sleep/activity, gently testing the voice's "power" with small safe experiments, exploring alternative explanations — alongside his psychiatric care.

Asking respectfully — sample dialogue

Therapist: "You mentioned hearing a voice. I'd really like to understand your experience — would it be okay to ask a little? There are no wrong answers."

Patient: "You'll think I'm mad."

Therapist: "Not at all — lots of people have experiences like this, especially under stress. I'm interested in what it's like for you and what the voice seems to mean. When is it strongest?"

Why appropriate: collaborative, normalising, explores meaning/context — it does not argue about whether the voice is "real."

Phase plan: (1) engagement & assessment → (2) collaborative formulation & normalising → (3) working with appraisals, coping, safety behaviours, experiments → (4) negative symptoms/activity & relapse prevention. Adaptations: shorter sessions, concrete language, flexible pacing for cognitive difficulties, fluctuating symptoms and limited engagement.

CBTp complements — never replaces — psychiatric care. It can reduce distress and some symptom-related outcomes; effect sizes and targets need clinical judgement and should not be over-stated.

3. Five Ps Formulation

Purpose: Organise the case into Presenting, Predisposing, Precipitating, Perpetuating, Protective factors.
How to use — step by step:
  1. Presenting: current problems.
  2. Predisposing: older vulnerabilities (upbringing, temperament).
  3. Precipitating: what triggered this episode.

Part 16 · Special Clinical Skills

"I know it's irrational, but I still feel afraid."

Therapist: "That makes complete sense — knowing something and feeling it are different. Feelings change through experience, not logic. Shall we design a small experiment so your body can learn what your head already suspects?"

Why: validates the intellectual-vs-emotional belief gap; points to experiential change.

Patient asks for reassurance

Therapist: "I notice the urge to reassure you. Reassurance helps for a few minutes then wears off, and it can keep the worry going. Could we instead work out how you could answer that doubt?"

Why: names the process, avoids feeding the reassurance cycle (vital in OCD/health anxiety), builds autonomy.

Missed homework · low motivation · silence · tangents · disagreement
  • Missed homework: explore the barrier ("no-lose"); re-grade; start in session.
  • Low motivation: explore ambivalence; link tasks to the patient's own values/goals.
  • Silence: allow it, then gently check ("I noticed it went quiet — what's happening for you?").
  • Tangents: validate, then collaboratively return to the agenda.
  • Rupture/disagreement: name it openly, take responsibility, adjust — repaired ruptures strengthen the alliance.
Therapist pitfalls to avoid

Premature cognitive restructuring (before the hot thought is elicited); confrontation vs collaboration; unexamined therapist assumptions; mechanical worksheet use; prioritising technique over the relationship.

Part 17 · Homework & Between-Session Practice

Most CBT change happens between sessions (Beck, 2011, Ch. 17). Good homework is collaboratively chosen, tied to the formulation and goals, appropriately graded, and reviewed every session.

  • Agree a clear rationale ("how will this help your goal?").
  • Predict barriers; problem-solve them in advance; start the task in-session.
  • Review without blame; distinguish lack of opportunity from avoidance.
  • Measure learning, not perfect performance ("no-lose" framing).

  • Reliable change and clinically significant change help interpret scores — but have limits and don't capture the whole person.
  • Monitor deterioration and engagement; recognise non-response early.
  • When stuck: revise the formulation; consider alternative diagnoses, comorbidity, environmental barriers, or unsuitability of the approach.
Illustrative progress (fictional): PHQ-9 across sessions 1→8: 21, 20, 18, 17, 14, 12, 10, 8 — a steady, clinically meaningful decline supporting continuation.
Rating scales support clinical judgement; they never replace a comprehensive assessment.

Part 19 · Ending CBT & Relapse Prevention

Prepare for ending from early on (Beck, 2011, Ch. 18): review goals, compare baseline vs current functioning, consolidate skills, build a maintenance plan.

  • Identify early-warning signs; anticipate future stressors.
  • Plan responses to setbacks; distinguish a lapse (temporary slip) from a relapse (full return).
  • Create a self-therapy plan; agree follow-up; discuss remaining difficulties honestly.
Fictional: "Raj" ends with a one-page plan — warning signs (withdrawing, waking at 4am, "what's the point"); action (resume activity schedule, use thought records, phone a friend, book a review if signs persist >2 weeks).

Part 20 · Limitations, Ethics & Adaptations

  • Limitations: effective for many but not all; response varies; the alliance matters as much as technique; manualised protocols can fit poorly for complex/comorbid cases.
  • Adaptations: culture, language/literacy, neurodevelopmental and cognitive needs; severe symptoms may need stabilisation, medication or multidisciplinary care first.
  • Ethics: informed consent, confidentiality and its limits, accurate documentation, risk management, working within competence, regular supervision.
  • Pitfalls: rigid protocol adherence, mechanical worksheet use, over-claiming efficacy, ignoring the formulation.
CBT is not a universal solution. Present it honestly as an evidence-based approach with boundaries, embedded in comprehensive, multidisciplinary care.

Part 22 · CBT Viva & Exam Preparation

Why is CBT not simply positive thinking?

CBT tests thoughts against evidence to reach balanced (not merely positive) conclusions, and changes the behaviour that maintains problems. It validates real difficulties and uses problem-solving when a thought is true.

Cognitive restructuring vs reassurance?

Restructuring helps the patient evaluate evidence themselves (a durable skill); reassurance supplies an external answer that relieves briefly, fades, and can become a maintaining safety behaviour.

Behavioural experiment vs exposure?

An experiment tests a specific belief (any emotion/prediction); exposure creates new fear learning by approaching feared cues and dropping avoidance. They overlap but have different primary aims.

Why may insight fail to change avoidance?

Avoidance is maintained by negative reinforcement (immediate relief). Insight doesn't deliver the corrective experience; only approaching the situation updates the emotional learning.

Formulation vs diagnosis?

Diagnosis names a syndrome (a category); formulation is an individualised explanation of why this person has these difficulties and what maintains them — it drives treatment.

When homework isn't completed?

Explore the barrier non-judgementally (opportunity vs avoidance vs unclear rationale vs too hard), treat it as useful data ("no-lose"), re-grade, and start the task in session.

Approaching unusual beliefs in CBT for psychosis?

Collaboratively and respectfully — normalising, exploring meaning/appraisal/distress and coping, gently testing threat beliefs; never arguing about whether the experience is "real."

How do you know an intervention targets a maintaining mechanism?

It maps directly onto a factor in the formulation that keeps the problem going (exposure for avoidance; ERP for compulsions; BA for lost reinforcement) — not just onto the symptom label.

Key theorists & models?

A. Beck (cognitive therapy, triad); Ellis (REBT/ABC); Clark (panic); Clark & Wells (social anxiety); Salkovskis / D. A. Clark (OCD); Ehlers & Clark (PTSD); Fairburn (CBT-E); Meichenbaum (SIT); third-wave: Hayes (ACT), Segal/Williams/Teasdale (MBCT), Linehan (DBT), Wells (MCT).

A common misconception to correct?

That CBT ignores emotions, the past, or the relationship. It uses the past to understand, targets emotion via thought and behaviour change, and depends on a strong collaborative alliance.
