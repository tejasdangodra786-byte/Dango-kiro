Part 2 · What Exactly Is CBT?

2.1 An authoritative definition

Cognitive Behaviour Therapy is a structured, time-limited, present-oriented psychotherapy that helps people solve current problems and change unhelpful (inaccurate and/or unhelpful) patterns of thinking and behaviour. Treatment is based on an individualised cognitive formulation of the person and of their disorder.
Paraphrased from Judith S. Beck, Cognitive Behavior Therapy: Basics and Beyond (2nd ed., Guilford Press, 2011), Ch. 1, which traces the approach to A. T. Beck's early 1960s "cognitive therapy" (Beck, 1964) and the first treatment manual (Beck, Rush, Shaw & Emery, 1979). Wording is original/paraphrased, not a verbatim quotation. A complementary definition is offered by clinical guidelines (e.g., NICE) and professional bodies (e.g., BABCP), which describe CBT as a family of evidence-based talking therapies addressing the links between thoughts, feelings and behaviour. Where sources differ in emphasis, the common core is: a formulation-driven, collaborative therapy targeting the thinking–behaviour links that maintain distress.
A simplified teaching explanation ("CBT helps you notice and change unhelpful thoughts and behaviours") is useful for patients but is not the formal definition and should not be cited as one.

2.2 CBT in simple language

  • What CBT is: a practical, collaborative therapy that works out what keeps a problem going now and helps the person change those maintaining patterns through new thinking and new action.
  • What CBT is not: it is not "positive thinking," not simply giving advice or reassurance, not telling people their thoughts are "wrong," and not a claim that every problem is caused by irrational thinking.
  • Why "cognitive-behavioural": it deliberately works on both the cognitive side (thoughts, beliefs, images, interpretations) and the behavioural side (what the person does, avoids, or repeats) — because in real life these feed each other.
TermPlain meaning
CognitiveTo do with thinking — the thoughts, images, interpretations, predictions and beliefs a person holds.
BehaviouralTo do with doing — actions, habits, avoidance, and the way consequences shape future behaviour.
TherapyA structured, professional, collaborative process aimed at reducing distress and improving functioning.
InterventionA specific planned technique (e.g., a thought record, a behavioural experiment) used to change a maintaining process.

How the pieces interact: a situation is interpreted by the person; that interpretation shapes emotion and bodily sensation, which shape behaviour, which produces consequences that feed back and strengthen (or weaken) the original interpretation. Crucially, CBT does not assume thoughts are the only cause: the therapist must weigh the person's environment, learning history, relationships, culture, medical factors and real-life adversity. Sometimes the thought is accurate and the problem is a genuine life difficulty requiring practical problem-solving, not "restructuring."

2.4 The behavioural model

CBT's behavioural roots explain how problems persist even when a person "knows better." Key mechanisms:

MechanismWhat it meansClinical relevance
Classical conditioningA neutral cue becomes paired with a response (e.g., a place paired with panic)Explains how cues trigger fear/craving
Operant conditioningBehaviour is shaped by its consequencesReinforcement/punishment maintain habits
Positive reinforcementA behaviour increases because something rewarding followse.g., checking relieves doubt → checking increases
Negative reinforcementA behaviour increases because it removes something aversiveThe engine of avoidance: escaping fear feels good → avoidance grows
PunishmentA consequence that reduces a behaviourOften unreliable clinically; can increase avoidance/shame
Avoidance & escapeStaying away from, or leaving, a feared situationPrevents disconfirmation → fear persists
Safety behavioursSubtle actions to "prevent disaster" (gripping, rehearsing, carrying water)Person credits the safety behaviour, not reality → belief never updates
Behavioural activationScheduling rewarding/meaningful activityBreaks the withdrawal–low mood loop in depression
Exposure & inhibitory learningApproaching the feared cue so new, safer learning competes with the old fearCore change mechanism in anxiety; modern view = new learning, not just "wearing out"
HabituationFear response naturally declines with sustained contactOne mechanism within exposure (not the whole story)
Generalisation / discriminationLearning spreads to similar cues / is narrowed to specific cuesExplains spread of fear and its targeted reduction
Maintenance cycleShort-term relief sustains the long-term problemThe central "vicious cycle" CBT interrupts
The behavioural engine of most anxiety is negative reinforcement: avoidance and safety behaviours bring immediate relief (reward), so they strengthen — while blocking the corrective experience that would update the fear. This is why insight alone rarely cures avoidance.

2.5 How cognition and behaviour work together

  • Why changing a thought can be insufficient: if avoidance continues, the person never experiences disconfirmation, so the fear belief survives despite verbal "understanding."
  • Why behavioural experiments beat reassurance: a planned experiment lets the person discover the outcome first-hand; reassurance is second-hand and fades, often becoming a safety behaviour itself.
  • Why action changes belief: doing the feared thing and surviving provides vivid, emotionally credible evidence that talk cannot.
  • Why some approaches change the relationship to thoughts: third-wave methods (e.g., mindfulness/ACT-influenced) focus less on disputing thought content and more on changing how the person relates to thoughts (noticing, defusing, accepting) — useful when content-disputation stalls.
Fictional: A man with panic believes "my racing heart means I'll have a heart attack." Verbal reassurance helps for minutes. A behavioural experiment — deliberately running up stairs to raise his heart rate and observing that no catastrophe occurs — provides durable, felt evidence that recalibrates the belief.

Part 3 · History, Development & Major CBT Approaches

CBT is best understood in three "waves," each addressing a limitation of the one before. Do not treat them as interchangeable or claim every modern therapy is "just CBT."

3.2 Distinguishing the approaches

ApproachPrimary targetSignature method
Behaviour therapyObservable behaviour & conditioningExposure, reinforcement, skills
Cognitive therapy (Beck)Content of thoughts/beliefs & their accuracyThought evaluation, guided discovery
REBT (Ellis)Irrational beliefs ("musts/shoulds")Vigorous disputation (ABCDE)

REBT disputes beliefs more forcefully; Beckian CT favours collaborative guided discovery. Third-wave therapies often do not dispute thought content at all. Blurring these distinctions is a common viva error.

Part 4 · Core Principles of CBT

Each principle below is given with its meaning, rationale, a brief application, and a common mistake. (Grounded in Beck, 2011, Ch. 1 "basic principles," and general CBT practice.)

1. Cognitive conceptualisation

Meaning: an evolving map of the person's thoughts, beliefs, behaviours and their origins. Rationale: technique choice flows from the map, not from the diagnosis alone. Application: the therapist updates the formulation every session. Mistake: applying a protocol without an individual conceptualisation.

2. Collaborative empiricism

Meaning: therapist and patient work as a team of investigators testing beliefs against evidence. Rationale: shared ownership increases engagement and durability. Application: "Let's look at this together — what does the evidence say?" Mistake: the therapist becoming the expert who "corrects" the patient.

3. Guided discovery

Meaning: helping the patient reach new perspectives through questions rather than lecturing. Rationale: self-generated insight is more convincing. Mistake: leading questions that merely extract the therapist's opinion.

4. Socratic questioning

Meaning: a series of open questions that expand the patient's view (evidence, alternatives, implications, usefulness). Mistake: "Socratic" questioning used to trap or argue the patient into agreement.

5. Structured & goal-oriented treatment

Meaning: sessions follow a structure (check-in, agenda, work, summary, homework, feedback) toward agreed goals. Rationale: efficiency and focus. Mistake: rigidly forcing structure when a patient is in crisis.

6. Present-focused work

Meaning: primary focus on current maintaining factors; history is used to understand, not to dwell. Mistake: endless history-taking with no change work.

7. Individualised case formulation

Meaning: the plan fits this person. Mistake: one-size-fits-all worksheets.

8. Skills acquisition

Meaning: therapy teaches transferable skills (thought evaluation, problem-solving, exposure) so the patient becomes their own therapist. Mistake: fostering dependence on the therapist.

9. Between-session practice (homework)

Meaning: most change happens between sessions. Rationale: generalisation to real life. Mistake: assigning homework without a collaborative rationale → non-completion.

10. Behavioural testing of beliefs

Meaning: beliefs are tested through action/experiments, not just discussion. Mistake: relying on verbal disputation alone.

11. Monitoring & measuring progress

Meaning: routine outcome measures track change. Mistake: treating scores as the whole picture (see Part 18).

12. Treatment adaptation based on response

Meaning: if the patient isn't improving, revisit the formulation. Mistake: repeating an ineffective technique.

13. Relapse prevention

Meaning: building skills and plans to maintain gains. Mistake: ending abruptly with no maintenance plan.

14. Patient autonomy & self-therapy

Meaning: the patient learns to run the methods independently. Mistake: therapist does all the "work."

15. Therapeutic alliance

Meaning: warmth, empathy and trust are necessary (if not sufficient) conditions. Mistake: prioritising technique over relationship.

16. Cultural & contextual sensitivity

Meaning: beliefs and behaviours are read within the person's culture, language, and circumstances. Mistake: labelling culturally normative beliefs as "distortions."

17. Evidence-informed practice

Meaning: integrating best research, clinical expertise, and patient values/preferences. Mistake: following a manual against clear evidence it isn't working.

5.2 Cognitive conceptualisation diagram

The diagram links early experiences → core beliefs → rules/assumptions → to the "here-and-now" maintaining cycle of situation–thought–emotion–behaviour. Every box and arrow is explained beneath it. (Structure follows Beck, 2011, Ch. 3 & 13–14.)

  • Early experiences: formative events that seeded beliefs (not a cause to be "treated," but context).
  • Core beliefs: global, absolute ideas ("I'm unlovable," "the world is dangerous").
  • Rules/assumptions: conditional strategies protecting the core belief ("If I'm perfect, I won't be rejected").
  • Compensatory strategies: habitual coping (perfectionism, avoidance, over-pleasing).
  • Maintaining cycle: the here-and-now situation→thought→emotion→behaviour loop where change work concentrates.
Not every box applies to every case. Early CBT often works only at the maintaining-cycle level; deeper belief work is added when needed. Do not force developmental content where it isn't clinically useful.

Part 6 · The CBT Therapeutic Relationship

CBT depends on a collaborative alliance: empathy, transparency, shared goals/agenda, validation, and genuine teamwork ("collaborative empiricism"). Below, dialogues show skilled vs. less-helpful responses.

Patient: "I did the thought record but it felt pointless — nothing changed."

Therapist (skilled): "Thanks for being honest — that's really useful feedback. Let's look at one together and see where it got stuck; sometimes a thought record points us to a belief that needs a different approach."

Why appropriate: validates, stays collaborative, treats "failure" as data. Less helpful: "You need to keep doing them properly" (blaming, non-collaborative).

Patient: "I don't think talking about my thoughts will help someone like me."

Therapist: "That makes sense given what you've been through. Would you be willing to treat the first couple of sessions as an experiment — we test whether this helps you specifically, and you decide?"

Why appropriate: validates doubt, offers autonomy, models empiricism. Less helpful: "CBT is evidence-based, it will work" (dismissive of the person's experience).

Part 7 · The Complete CBT Session Structure

A typical CBT session follows a predictable structure (Beck, 2011, Ch. 4–8). Structure aids efficiency but is flexible — it bends to the patient's needs and the protocol.

  1. Preparation — review notes, last session, homework set.
  2. Mood/symptom check — brief measure or rating.
  3. Risk / major changes — screened when indicated.
  4. Bridge from last session — "What stood out from last time?"
  5. Homework review — always review what was set (non-review teaches patients it doesn't matter).
  6. Agenda setting — collaboratively pick 1–2 targets.
  7. Target problem — select the focus.
  8. Intervention — apply the chosen technique.
  9. Summarise learning — patient states the take-away.
  10. New homework — collaboratively agreed, started in-session where possible.
  11. Feedback — "Anything I got wrong or that bothered you today?"
  12. Record & plan next.
Commonly used every session: check-in, homework review, agenda, intervention, summary, new homework, feedback. Flexible/conditional: risk review, formal measures, and which intervention — these depend on the patient and the protocol.

Five sample scenarios (fictional)

A. Standard 50-minute session

Check-in (mood 4/10) → bridge → homework review (thought record completed) → agenda: "work stress" + "sleep" → intervention: evaluate the hot thought "I'll be fired," generate balanced response → summary → homework: behavioural experiment at work; sleep-window plan → feedback.

B. Shorter (25-minute) session

Tighter agenda: one target only. Check-in → quick homework review → single technique (e.g., set up one behavioural experiment) → summary + homework + feedback. Therapist language: "We've limited time, so let's pick the one thing most useful today."

C. Patient arrives distressed

Structure bends: validate and contain first ("Let's slow down — you're safe here"), assess risk, use a grounding/affect-regulation skill, and collaboratively decide whether to address the crisis as the agenda. Do not force the planned technique.

D. Homework not completed

Explore non-judgementally: was it lack of opportunity, unclear rationale, too hard, or avoidance? "No-lose" framing — understanding the barrier is useful. Re-grade the task, start it in-session, problem-solve reminders.

E. Planned intervention not appropriate today

If new information changes the picture (e.g., a fresh stressor, safety concern, or the formulation was off), the therapist transparently re-prioritises: "Given what you've told me, I think today it's more useful to…", and revisits the formulation.

Part 8 · All Major CBT Techniques

Organised by clinical purpose. Each technique: definition, rationale, indications, cautions, steps, example language, a fictional example, common errors, and the matching worksheet (see Part 14).

8.1 Assessment & monitoring techniques

Functional analysis / ABC analysis (Antecedent–Behaviour–Consequence)

Definition: mapping what comes before a behaviour (A), the behaviour (B), and what follows (C). Rationale: reveals the reinforcement maintaining the behaviour. Indications: almost any behavioural problem. Steps: record several real episodes → identify the pattern → target the maintaining consequence. Example language: "Walk me through one specific time — what set it off, what you did, and what happened right after." Error: analysing in the abstract instead of specific episodes. Worksheet: Functional analysis / ABC.

Self-monitoring (mood, thoughts, activity, symptoms)

Definition: the patient records target experiences in real time. Rationale: accurate data, raises awareness, builds the formulation, gives a baseline. Caution: monitoring can increase focus on symptoms (e.g., in health anxiety) — use judiciously. Error: retrospective guessing instead of in-the-moment logging. Rating scales screen/monitor — they are not diagnoses.

8.2 Cognitive techniques

Identifying & evaluating automatic thoughts (thought records)

Definition: catching the quick, situation-specific thoughts that drive emotion, then examining them. Steps: situation → emotion (rate %) → automatic thought (rate belief %) → evidence for/against → balanced alternative → re-rate. Example language: "What went through your mind just then? If that thought is true, what would it mean?" Error: jumping to "balanced thoughts" before genuinely eliciting the hot thought. (Beck, 2011, Ch. 9–12.)

Socratic questioning & guided discovery

Open questions that broaden perspective: evidence? alternative explanations? what would you tell a friend? how useful is this thought? Error: using questions to argue the patient into the "right" answer.

Decatastrophising & probability re-estimation

"What's the worst / best / most likely? If the worst happened, how would you cope?" Re-estimating both the probability and one's coping ability. Error: dismissing the feared outcome rather than realistically appraising it and coping.

Examining the evidence, alternatives, continuum, double-standard, responsibility pie, cost–benefit, reattribution

A toolkit for testing distorted conclusions: list evidence for/against; generate alternatives; place an "all-or-nothing" judgement on a continuum; apply the standard you'd apply to a friend (double-standard); divide responsibility in a pie chart; weigh the costs/benefits of a belief or behaviour; reattribute causes more accurately.

Downward arrow → intermediate & core beliefs; positive data log; coping cards

The downward-arrow ("If that were true, what would it mean about you?") reaches assumptions and core beliefs. New, healthier beliefs are strengthened with a positive data log (daily evidence for the new belief) and portable coping cards. (Beck, 2011, Ch. 13–14.)

Imagery techniques

Working with distressing images/memories (e.g., transforming or rescripting a recurrent image) when the evidence and model support it. Caution: trauma imagery work requires appropriate training and a suitable protocol.

Cognitive restructuring is not forced positive thinking, arguing, or invalidating real problems. When a thought is true (a genuine difficulty), switch to problem-solving, not disputation.

8.3 Behavioural techniques

Behavioural activation & activity scheduling

For: depression. Rationale: withdrawal removes reinforcement → lower mood → more withdrawal; scheduling valued/mastery/pleasure activity reverses the loop. Steps: monitor activity–mood → schedule graded activities → review. Error: overloading an exhausted patient; start small and graded. (Beck, 2011, Ch. 6.)

Graded exposure (in-vivo, imaginal, interoceptive) & ERP

For: anxiety, phobias, OCD (as ERP), PTSD (specialised). Rationale: approaching the feared cue while dropping avoidance/safety behaviours enables new, safer learning. Steps: build a fear hierarchy → agree predictions → expose, stay until learning occurs, drop safety behaviours → review what was learned. Caution: select and adapt per disorder, formulation, safety, and protocol; never coerce.

Behavioural experiments

Definition: a planned real-world test of a specific belief ("If I blush, people will laugh"). Steps: state belief + prediction (rate %) → design the test → run it → record the actual outcome → update the belief. Distinct from exposure: an experiment's aim is to test a belief (any emotion); exposure's aim is new fear learning; activation's aim is restoring reinforcement.

Problem-solving, assertiveness, social skills, relaxation/breathing, stimulus control, response prevention, reducing safety behaviours, activity pacing, sleep interventions

Skill-building and behaviour-change methods matched to the maintaining mechanism. Caution: relaxation/breathing can become a safety behaviour in panic if used to "prevent" catastrophe — use for general arousal, not as a crutch that blocks disconfirmation.

8.4 Advanced interventions

Core-belief modification, schema-level work within CBT, imagery rescripting (where evidence/model support it), experiments targeting deep beliefs, relapse-signature work, and testing predictions in real life. Clearly separate standard CBT methods from techniques originating in related therapies (schema therapy, ACT, DBT); name the source and use within your competence.

8.5 Engagement & adherence techniques

Motivational enhancement within CBT, collaborative goal-setting, graded commitment, strengths-based reinforcement, exploring ambivalence, reviewing homework barriers, and using feedback/progress data to adjust the plan.

Select interventions by the maintaining mechanism in the formulation — never from a symptom label alone. Combine with evidence, patient preference, safety, and progress data.

Part 10 · CBT for Different Disorders

Each disorder has its own maintaining model — the same protocol does not fit all. For the major disorders the full template is given; related presentations are summarised with their distinctive model and foci. Models are attributed to their originators where identifiable.

1. Major Depressive Disorder

Presentation: persistent low mood/anhedonia, negative view of self–world–future (Beck's cognitive triad), withdrawal, rumination, sleep/appetite change, hopelessness; screen suicide risk.

CBT model (Beck): negative automatic thoughts + inactivity → loss of reinforcement → worse mood → more withdrawal/rumination (maintaining loop).

Assessment: activity–mood monitoring, cognitive triad, rumination, hopelessness, risk; measures such as PHQ-9/BDI (monitoring, not diagnosis).

Formulation: "Raj," 34, post-redundancy; core belief "I'm a failure"; withdrawn, rumination; protective: supportive partner, motivation. Maintaining cycle: inactivity + self-critical thoughts → low mood → more inactivity.

Goals: short — resume 1 valued activity/day, start activity log; intermediate — evaluate self-critical thoughts, re-engage socially; long — mood within normal range, return to work; relapse-prevention — early-warning plan.

Techniques: behavioural activation first (often before cognitive work), activity scheduling, thought records on self-critical cognitions, problem-solving, core-belief work later, relapse prevention.

Brief plan: S1–2 assessment/formulation/psychoeducation + BA rationale; S3–5 activity scheduling + graded mastery/pleasure; S6–10 cognitive restructuring of triad thoughts; S11–14 core beliefs + relapse prevention. Limitations: severe depression may need activation-led/lower-intensity start, medication, or crisis care first.

2. Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

Model: chronic worry maintained by beliefs about worry (positive: "worry keeps me prepared"; negative: "worry is uncontrollable/harmful"), intolerance of uncertainty, and avoidance/reassurance. (Metacognitive & intolerance-of-uncertainty models.)

Techniques: sort worries into practical (→ problem-solving) vs hypothetical (→ let-go / uncertainty tolerance); worry postponement; evaluate meta-beliefs; drop reassurance-seeking/checking. Worksheet: worry monitoring; worry problem-solving vs hypothetical. Caution: relaxation alone is weak for GAD; target the worry process.

3. Panic Disorder

Model (Clark, 1986): catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily sensations ("palpitations = heart attack") → escalating fear → more sensations (vicious cycle); safety behaviours prevent disconfirmation.

Techniques: psychoeducation on the panic cycle; interoceptive exposure (induce feared sensations); behavioural experiments to test the catastrophe; drop safety behaviours; reduce avoidance. Worksheet: panic cycle.

4. Agoraphobia

Model: avoidance of situations where escape/help feels hard, maintained by anticipatory anxiety + safety behaviours. Techniques: graded in-vivo exposure hierarchy, dropping safety behaviours, behavioural experiments; often combined with panic treatment.

5. Social Anxiety Disorder

Model (Clark & Wells, 1995): in feared social situations, attention shifts inward (self-focused attention), the person constructs a distorted negative self-image, uses safety behaviours, and engages in anticipatory/post-event processing. Techniques: shift attention externally; drop safety behaviours; video/observer feedback experiments; address pre/post-event rumination. Worksheet: self-focused attention & safety-behaviour record.

6. Specific Phobias

Model: conditioned fear + avoidance preventing new learning. Techniques: graded in-vivo exposure (often rapid/one-session for circumscribed phobias), behavioural experiments, dropping avoidance.

7. Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Model (Salkovskis): normal intrusive thoughts are appraised as signalling responsibility/threat → distress → compulsions/neutralising → short-term relief (negative reinforcement) → intrusions persist. Repository source: David A. Clark, CBT for OCD (scanned; see note in Part 24). Techniques: ERP (exposure to triggers + prevention of compulsions), appraising responsibility, behavioural experiments. Worksheets: obsession–compulsion cycle; ERP planning. Caution: reassurance is a compulsion — don't provide it.

8. Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

Model (Ehlers & Clark, 2000): a sense of current threat from poorly elaborated trauma memory + negative appraisals + maintaining strategies (avoidance, rumination, safety behaviours). Techniques (trauma-focused CBT): reclaiming-your-life activities, imaginal reliving/updating the memory, stimulus discrimination, site visits, cognitive restructuring of appraisals. Caution: requires specific training and a suitable protocol; stabilise and assess risk first.

9–10. Health Anxiety / Illness Anxiety

Model: catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily signs as serious illness, maintained by body-checking, reassurance-seeking, symptom-monitoring, and avoidance. Techniques: reduce checking/reassurance; behavioural experiments; re-attribute sensations; limit online symptom-searching. Worksheet: health anxiety monitoring & experiment.

11. Insomnia (CBT-I)

Model: conditioned arousal + maladaptive habits (irregular schedule, time-in-bed awake) + unhelpful beliefs about sleep. Techniques: stimulus control, sleep restriction/compression, sleep hygiene, cognitive work on sleep beliefs. Worksheet: sleep diary.

12. Eating Disorders

Model (CBT-E, Fairburn): over-evaluation of shape/weight/control drives dietary restraint, binge–purge cycles, checking/avoidance. Techniques: regular eating, self-monitoring, addressing over-evaluation, behavioural experiments. Caution: medical monitoring essential; some presentations need specialist/inpatient care.

13. Substance Use & Relapse Prevention

Model: cues → cravings/permissive beliefs → use → reinforcement. Techniques: functional analysis of use, identifying high-risk situations, coping with cravings, refusal skills, relapse-prevention plan; motivational work on ambivalence. Worksheet: substance trigger & craving record.

14. Psychosis & schizophrenia-spectrum

Covered in depth in Part 11 (repository priority source: Hagen, Turkington, Berge & Gråwe).

15. Bipolar Disorder

CBT as adjunct to medication: psychoeducation, mood monitoring, identifying prodromes/early-warning signs, regulating routines/sleep, relapse-prevention action plans. Caution: CBT does not replace pharmacological management; evidence is strongest for relapse prevention and adjunctive use.

16–22. Chronic pain · Anger · Body Dysmorphic Disorder · Hoarding · Somatic symptom presentations · Personality-related difficulties
  • Chronic pain: pain–activity–mood cycle; pacing, graded activity, cognitive work on pain beliefs, acceptance where helpful.
  • Anger: trigger → appraisal ("disrespect") → arousal → aggression; techniques: trigger analysis, cognitive reappraisal, arousal reduction, assertiveness, time-out.
  • BDD: appraisal of perceived defect + mirror-checking/avoidance/comparing; ERP-style experiments, attention retraining; caution re: reassurance and cosmetic procedures.
  • Hoarding: beliefs about possessions + distress on discarding + avoidance; graded sorting/discarding experiments, decision-making skills.
  • Somatic symptom presentations: symptom focus + checking + illness beliefs; re-attribution, reduce checking, activity. Rule out medical causes.
  • Personality-related difficulties: schema-level and longer-term CBT approaches; often needs DBT/schema-therapy elements — name the source and work within competence.

Part 11 · CBT for Psychosis (CBTp)

Priority repository source: Hagen, Turkington, Berge & Gråwe, CBT for Psychosis: A Symptom-based Approach. Supplemented by current clinical guidance (e.g., NICE recommends CBTp for people with psychosis/schizophrenia) and the normalising tradition associated with Turkington and colleagues. Statements below distinguish established recommendations from areas requiring clinical judgement.

Rationale: CBTp does not aim to "argue someone out of" beliefs or experiences. It targets the distress and disruption linked to unusual experiences by building a collaborative, normalising understanding and testing threat-related appraisals gently.

Core stance & methods

  • Engagement first — warmth, time, the person's own goals; go at their pace.
  • Collaborative formulation of how experiences, appraisals, emotions and coping interact.
  • Normalising — placing unusual experiences on a continuum with ordinary experience (e.g., under stress/sleep loss) to reduce fear and shame, where appropriate.
  • Assess the appraisal, not just the experience — distress depends on meaning, conviction, preoccupation, and perceived power/threat of voices or beliefs.
  • Work with threat beliefs & safety behaviours; explore alternative explanations collaboratively.
  • Coping strategies for voices (e.g., focusing/refocusing, scheduling, reducing isolation).
  • Carefully-formulated behavioural experiments, adapted to safety and engagement.
  • Negative symptoms/activity — graded activity, addressing defeatist beliefs, when indicated.
  • Relapse prevention and integration with psychiatric/multidisciplinary care and medication.
Fictional case: "Sam," 24, hears a critical voice he believes is an all-powerful entity that will harm him if disobeyed; he stays home and avoids eye contact (safety behaviours). Collaborative formulation links sleep loss + isolation → more voice activity → threat appraisal → avoidance → isolation. Work: normalising, sleep/activity, gently testing the voice's "power" through small, safe behavioural experiments, alternative explanations — alongside his psychiatric care.

Asking respectfully — sample dialogue

Therapist: "You mentioned hearing a voice. I'd really like to understand your experience — would it be okay to ask a bit about it? There are no wrong answers."

Patient: "You'll think I'm mad."

Therapist: "Not at all — lots of people have experiences like this, especially under stress. I'm interested in what it's like for you and what the voice seems to mean. When is it strongest?"

Why appropriate: collaborative, normalising, explores meaning/context — it does not argue about whether the voice is "real."

Phase plan: Phase 1 engagement & assessment → Phase 2 collaborative formulation & normalising → Phase 3 working with appraisals, coping, safety behaviours, experiments → Phase 4 negative symptoms/activity & relapse prevention. Adapt for cognitive difficulties, fluctuating symptoms, and limited engagement (shorter sessions, concrete language, flexible pacing).

CBTp complements — never replaces — psychiatric care. Evidence supports reducing distress and some symptom-related outcomes; magnitude of effect and targets require clinical judgement and should not be over-stated.

Part 16 · Special Clinical Skills

"I know it's irrational, but I still feel afraid."

Therapist: "That makes complete sense — knowing something logically and feeling it are different. Feelings change through experience, not just logic. Shall we design a small experiment so your body can learn what your head already suspects?"

Why: validates the gap between intellectual and emotional belief; points to behavioural (experiential) change rather than more disputation.

Patient asks for reassurance

Therapist: "I notice the urge for me to reassure you. Reassurance tends to help for a few minutes then wears off, and it can keep the worry going. Could we instead look at how you could answer that doubt?"

Why: names the process, avoids feeding the reassurance cycle (crucial in OCD/health anxiety), builds autonomy.

Homework not completed · low motivation · silence · tangents · disagreement
  • Missed homework: explore the barrier non-judgementally ("no-lose"); re-grade; start in session.
  • Low motivation: explore ambivalence, link tasks to the patient's own values/goals.
  • Silence: allow it, then gently check ("I notice it went quiet — what's happening for you?").
  • Tangents: validate, then collaboratively return to the agenda.
  • Disagreement/rupture: name it openly, take responsibility, adjust — ruptures repaired well can strengthen the alliance.
Therapist pitfalls to avoid

Premature cognitive restructuring (before the hot thought is elicited); confrontation instead of collaboration; unexamined therapist assumptions; using worksheets mechanically; prioritising technique over the relationship.

Part 17 · Homework & Between-Session Practice

Most CBT change happens between sessions (Beck, 2011, Ch. 17). Good homework is collaboratively chosen, tied to the formulation and goals, appropriately graded, and reviewed every session.

  • Agree a clear rationale ("how will this help your goal?").
  • Predict barriers; problem-solve them in advance; start the task in-session.
  • Review without blame; distinguish lack of opportunity from avoidance.
  • Measure learning, not perfect performance ("no-lose" framing).

Part 18 · Measurement-Based Care & Treatment Evaluation

  • Baseline → repeated measurement of symptoms, function, and patient-defined goals; routine session feedback.
  • Reliable change and clinically significant change help interpret scores — but have limits and don't capture the whole person.
  • Monitor deterioration and engagement; recognise non-response early.
  • When stuck: revise the formulation; consider alternative diagnoses, comorbidity, environmental barriers, or unsuitability of the approach.

Part 19 · Ending CBT & Relapse Prevention

Prepare for ending from early on (Beck, 2011, Ch. 18): review goals, compare baseline vs current functioning, consolidate skills, and build a maintenance plan.

  • Identify early-warning signs and anticipate future stressors.
  • Plan responses to setbacks; distinguish a lapse (temporary slip) from a relapse (full return).
  • Create a self-therapy plan and agree follow-up; discuss remaining difficulties honestly.
Fictional: "Raj" ends therapy with a one-page plan: warning signs (withdrawing, waking at 4am, "what's the point" thoughts); action (resume activity schedule, use thought records, phone a friend, book a review if signs persist >2 weeks).

Part 20 · Limitations, Ethics & Adaptations

  • Limitations: CBT is effective for many but not all patients/disorders; response varies; the alliance matters as much as technique; manualised protocols can fit poorly for complex/comorbid presentations.
  • Adaptations: culture, language/literacy, neurodevelopmental and cognitive needs; severe symptoms may require stabilisation, medication, or multidisciplinary care first.
  • Ethics: informed consent, confidentiality and its limits, accurate documentation, risk management, working within competence, and regular supervision.
  • Pitfalls: rigid protocol adherence, mechanical worksheet use, over-claiming efficacy, ignoring the formulation.
CBT is not a universal solution. Present it honestly as an evidence-based approach with boundaries, embedded in comprehensive, multidisciplinary care.

Part 22 · CBT Viva & Exam Preparation

Why is CBT not simply positive thinking?

CBT tests thoughts against evidence to reach balanced (not merely positive) conclusions, and crucially changes behaviour that maintains problems. It validates real difficulties and uses problem-solving when a thought is true.

Difference between cognitive restructuring and reassurance?

Restructuring helps the patient evaluate evidence themselves, building a durable skill; reassurance supplies an external answer that relieves anxiety briefly, fades, and can become a maintaining safety behaviour.

How does a behavioural experiment differ from exposure?

An experiment is designed to test a specific belief (any emotion, any prediction); exposure is designed to create new fear learning by approaching feared cues and dropping avoidance. They overlap but have different primary aims.

Why may insight fail to change avoidance?

Avoidance is maintained by negative reinforcement (immediate relief). Intellectual insight doesn't deliver the corrective experience; only approaching the situation updates the emotional learning.

How is formulation different from diagnosis?

Diagnosis names a syndrome (a category); formulation is an individualised explanation of why this person has these difficulties and what maintains them — it drives treatment.

How should a therapist respond when homework isn't completed?

Explore the barrier non-judgementally (opportunity vs avoidance vs unclear rationale vs too hard), treat it as useful data ("no-lose"), re-grade, and start the task in session.

How should a therapist approach unusual beliefs in CBT for psychosis?

Collaboratively and respectfully — normalising, exploring meaning/appraisal/distress and coping, and testing threat beliefs gently; never arguing about whether the experience is "real."

How do you know an intervention targets a maintaining mechanism?

It maps directly onto a factor in the formulation that keeps the problem going (e.g., exposure for avoidance; ERP for compulsions; BA for lost reinforcement) — not just onto the symptom label.

Name the key theorists and their models.

A. Beck (cognitive therapy, cognitive triad); Ellis (REBT/ABC); Clark (panic); Clark & Wells (social anxiety); Salkovskis / D. A. Clark (OCD); Ehlers & Clark (PTSD); Fairburn (CBT-E); Meichenbaum (SIT); third-wave: Hayes (ACT), Segal/Williams/Teasdale (MBCT), Linehan (DBT), Wells (MCT).

Common misconception to correct?

That CBT ignores emotions, the past, or the relationship. It uses the past to understand, targets emotion via thought and behaviour change, and depends on a strong collaborative alliance.
