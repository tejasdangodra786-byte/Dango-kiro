The F10–F19 Block — Overview

The block F10–F19 in ICD-10 is titled "Mental and behavioural disorders due to psychoactive substance use". It contains a wide variety of disorders that differ in severity and clinical form but that are all attributable to the use of one or more psychoactive substances (chemicals that act on the brain to change mood, thinking, perception, or behaviour), whether or not medically prescribed. ICD-10

The corresponding ICD-11 grouping is "Disorders due to substance use" (part of "Disorders due to substance use or addictive behaviours"), coded in the 6C4 range. Both systems separate this block from the organic/neurocognitive block (F00–F09) purely for practical convenience — substance-caused brain disorders would logically belong with the organic disorders but are kept together here so that all substance-related conditions sit in one place. ICD-11

Why substances get their own block: keeping every substance-related disorder (from a single episode of intoxication through to dependence, withdrawal, substance-induced psychosis, and substance-related dementia) in one block makes the classification easier to use and allows the same set of clinical syndromes to be applied consistently across all substances.

How the Block is Coded — Two-Axis Structure

The genius of this block is a two-part ("two-axis") code. The third character identifies the substance; the fourth (and fifth) characters identify the clinical syndrome. This lets you describe, for example, "alcohol dependence" or "opioid withdrawal" or "cannabis-induced psychotic disorder" using the same syndrome menu for every drug. ICD-10

Axis 1 — the substance (3rd character)

F10 alcohol; F11 opioids; F12 cannabinoids; F13 sedatives/hypnotics; F14 cocaine; F15 other stimulants (incl. caffeine); F16 hallucinogens; F17 tobacco; F18 volatile solvents; F19 multiple drug use / other.

Axis 2 — the clinical syndrome (4th/5th character, "F1x.x")

.0 acute intoxication; .1 harmful use; .2 dependence syndrome; .3 withdrawal state; .4 withdrawal state with delirium; .5 psychotic disorder; .6 amnesic syndrome; .7 residual and late-onset psychotic disorder; .8 other mental and behavioural disorders; .9 unspecified.

Thus F10.2 = alcohol dependence syndrome; F11.3 = opioid withdrawal state; F12.5 = cannabinoid-induced psychotic disorder. The syndrome definitions below are written once and apply, with substance-specific detail, across the whole block.

ICD-11 ICD-11 uses the same idea but with alphanumeric stems: each substance has a code (e.g. 6C40 alcohol, 6C43 opioids), and each carries sub-codes for episode of harmful use, harmful pattern of use, dependence, intoxication, and withdrawal, plus separate codes for substance-induced disorders (delirium, psychotic disorder, mood disorder, and so on) in the 6C4x.x and 6E6x ranges.

The Shared Clinical Syndromes (apply to every substance)

Because the fourth/fifth character carries the syndrome, the same clinical concepts recur for every drug. The modules below define each syndrome once, giving the identifying features, the ICD-10 diagnostic guidelines, and the ICD-11 essential features. Substance-specific detail (what each syndrome looks like for alcohol, opioids, and so on) is then given in the substance sections that follow.

Acute Intoxication (F1x.0)

Definition and identifying features

Acute intoxication (the transient set of effects that appear soon after taking a substance and wear off as it is cleared from the body) is a transient condition following the administration of a psychoactive substance, resulting in disturbances of consciousness, cognition, perception, affect (mood), behaviour, or other psychophysiological functions and responses. It is closely related to dose and is time-limited, resolving as the substance is eliminated (unless tissue damage or another complication has occurred). ICD-10

Identifying points

  • The picture is substance-specific (e.g. disinhibition and slurred speech with alcohol; euphoria and pinpoint pupils with opioids; overactivity and dilated pupils with stimulants).
  • Intensity is dose-related and effects lessen with time.
  • Pathological intoxication (a sudden, marked, aggressive or violent reaction to an amount of alcohol that would not intoxicate most people) is a specific qualifier for alcohol.

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines

Acute intoxication should be diagnosed only when intoxication is not associated with more persistent substance-related problems. There must be clear evidence of recent use of a psychoactive substance at a dose sufficient to produce intoxication; symptoms and signs of intoxication compatible with the known actions of that substance; and findings not accounted for by a medical disorder unrelated to substance use, or better explained by another mental/behavioural disorder. Sub-codes: .00 uncomplicated; .01 with trauma/bodily injury; .02 with other medical complications; .03 with delirium; .04 with perceptual distortions; .05 with coma; .06 with convulsions; .07 pathological intoxication (alcohol).

ICD-11 Essential features

A clinically significant, transient condition that develops during or shortly after the consumption of the substance, characterised by disturbances in consciousness, cognition, perception, affect, behaviour, or coordination that are caused by the known pharmacological effects of the substance and their intensity is closely related to the amount consumed. The features resolve as the substance is cleared. Severity is specified (mild, moderate, severe), and complications (e.g. with delirium, with perceptual disturbances) are noted. Intoxication is not diagnosed if the symptoms are better accounted for by another condition.

Mnemonic — the 4 rules of intoxication: "DR TD"

Dose-related (intensity tracks how much was taken) · Recent use (clear evidence of recent intake) · Transient (wears off as the drug clears) · Drug-specific picture (matches that substance's known effects).
Complications add-on — "TIP-CCC": Trauma · Injury/medical · Perceptual distortions · Coma · Convulsions · (pathological) — the ICD-10 fifth-character complications.

Harmful Use (F1x.1)

Definition and identifying features

Harmful use (a pattern of substance use that is actually damaging health, but without meeting the criteria for dependence) is a pattern of psychoactive substance use that is causing damage to health. The damage may be physical (e.g. hepatitis from injecting) or mental (e.g. depressive episodes secondary to heavy drinking). ICD-10

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines

  • There must be clear evidence that the substance use was responsible for (or substantially contributed to) actual physical or psychological harm to the user.
  • The nature of the harm should be clearly identifiable.
  • The pattern of use has persisted for at least one month or has occurred repeatedly within a 12-month period.
  • The diagnosis is not made if dependence syndrome, a psychotic disorder, or another specific form of substance-related disorder is present.

Note: socially disapproved use, or mere intoxication, is not in itself "harmful use"; actual demonstrable harm is required.

ICD-11 Essential features (single episode / harmful pattern)

ICD-11 divides this into a single episode of harmful use and a harmful pattern of use (specified as episodic or continuous). Harmful use is diagnosed when substance use has caused clinically significant harm to the person's physical or mental health, OR to the health of others (e.g. injury caused while intoxicated) — the explicit inclusion of harm to others is a change from ICD-10. It is diagnosed only in the absence of dependence, and the harmful pattern requires a period of use (generally at least one month if episodic, or continuous over at least one month).

Mnemonic — "HARM"

Health damage that is real and identifiable (physical or mental) · Absence of dependence (if dependent, code dependence instead) · Repeated / lasting pattern (about a month, or repeated over a year) · May affect others too (ICD-11 also counts harm to other people).
Remember the difference: harmful use = "actual damage, no dependence"; dependence = "the substance is now in charge".

Dependence Syndrome (F1x.2) — the core concept

Definition and identifying features

The dependence syndrome (a cluster of behavioural, cognitive and physiological features that develop after repeated use, in which the substance takes on a much higher priority than other behaviours that once had greater value) is the central concept of the block. A defining characteristic is a strong desire to take the substance, and it is typically accompanied by tolerance and a physical withdrawal state. ICD-10

Two hallmark physiological features to identify

  • Tolerance (needing progressively larger doses to get the same effect that a smaller dose once produced).
  • Withdrawal (a characteristic set of unpleasant symptoms that appear when the substance is stopped or reduced, relieved by taking the substance again).
  • Plus behavioural markers: craving (a strong desire or compulsion to use), loss of control over use, neglect of other interests, and continued use despite clear harm.

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines (three or more in the past year)

A definite diagnosis of dependence should usually be made only if three or more of the following have been present together at some time during the previous year:

  1. A strong desire or sense of compulsion to take the substance;
  2. Difficulties in controlling substance-taking behaviour in terms of its onset, termination, or levels of use;
  3. A physiological withdrawal state when substance use has ceased or been reduced, evidenced by the characteristic withdrawal syndrome, or use of the same (or a closely related) substance to relieve or avoid withdrawal symptoms;
  4. Evidence of tolerance, such that increased doses are required to achieve effects originally produced by lower doses;
  5. Progressive neglect of alternative pleasures or interests because of substance use, and increased time spent obtaining, using, or recovering from the substance;
  6. Persisting with substance use despite clear evidence of harmful consequences.

ICD-11 Essential features (streamlined three-feature model)

Dependence is a disorder of regulation of substance use arising from repeated or continuous use. The central feature is a strong internal drive to use the substance, manifested by two or more of the following, usually evident over at least 12 months (or over at least one month if use is continuous/daily):

  1. Impaired control over substance use (onset, frequency, intensity, duration, termination, context);
  2. Increasing precedence of substance use over other aspects of life (health, daily activities, responsibilities), such that use continues or escalates despite harm or negative consequences;
  3. Physiological features indicating neuroadaptation (the brain's adjustment to repeated substance exposure): tolerance; withdrawal symptoms after stopping or reducing; or repeated use to prevent or relieve withdrawal.

Remission is specified (early full, sustained partial, sustained full) and pattern of use (continuous, episodic).

Mnemonic for the ICD-10 six criteria — "CANT SToP" (need 3 or more in a year)

Compulsion / strong desire (craving) · Attention neglected (neglect of other pleasures and interests) · No control (difficulty controlling use) · Tolerance · Stopping causes withdrawal (physiological withdrawal state) · Persists despite harm.
(Letters: C, A, N, T, S-to, P — "CAN'T STOP" captures the loss-of-control theme of dependence.)

Mnemonic for the ICD-11 three features — "CPP" (need 2 or more)

Control impaired · Precedence (use takes priority over life) · Physiological features (tolerance / withdrawal).
Quick recall: ICD-10 = 3 of 6 (CAN'T STOP); ICD-11 = 2 of 3 (CPP).

Withdrawal State (F1x.3)

Definition and identifying features

The withdrawal state (the group of symptoms that appears when a substance is stopped or reduced after prolonged and/or heavy use) is a group of symptoms of variable clustering and severity occurring on absolute or relative withdrawal of a substance after persistent use. Onset and course are time-limited and related to the type of substance and the dose being used immediately before cessation. A key rule: the withdrawal syndrome is typically the opposite of the intoxication effects of the same drug (e.g. alcohol is a depressant, so its withdrawal causes over-excitation — tremor, anxiety, seizures). ICD-10

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines

  • Clear evidence of recent cessation or reduction of substance use after repeated, usually prolonged and/or high-dose, use.
  • Symptoms and signs are compatible with the known features of a withdrawal state from the particular substance.
  • Symptoms are not accounted for by a medical disorder unrelated to substance use, and not better explained by another mental/behavioural disorder.

Sub-codes: .30 uncomplicated; .31 with convulsions.

ICD-11 Essential features

A clinically significant cluster of symptoms, behaviours, and/or physiological features, varying in degree of severity and duration, that occurs on cessation or reduction of use in individuals who have developed dependence or have used the substance for a prolonged period or in large amounts. The presentation is consistent with the known withdrawal features of the specific substance and is not better accounted for by another disorder. Severity and complications (e.g. with perceptual disturbances, with seizures) are specified.

Mnemonic — "STOP = withdrawal": "STOP"

Stopped or reduced after heavy/prolonged use · Time-limited (onset and course depend on the drug's half-life) · Opposite of the drug's own effect (depressant drugs → over-excited withdrawal; stimulants → "crashed" withdrawal) · Pattern matches that specific substance.
The golden rule to remember: withdrawal is the mirror-image of intoxication — a downer drug gives an "up" withdrawal, an upper drug gives a "down" withdrawal.

Withdrawal State with Delirium (F1x.4)

A withdrawal state (as above) complicated by delirium (an acute, fluctuating confusional state with clouded consciousness, disorientation, and hallucinations). The prototype is delirium tremens (the severe, potentially fatal alcohol-withdrawal delirium), discussed in the alcohol section. Sub-codes: .40 without convulsions; .41 with convulsions. ICD-10 In ICD-11 this is captured as substance-induced (withdrawal) delirium.

Clinical alert: withdrawal delirium (especially from alcohol or sedatives) is a medical emergency with significant mortality if untreated.

Mnemonic for delirium tremens — "DTs = Disoriented, Tremulous, Sympathetic-overdrive"

Disorientation + clouded consciousness · Tremor + Terrifying visual/tactile hallucinations (classically insects — formication) · s = Sympathetic overdrive (Sweating, fast heart, fever, high blood pressure).
Only depressants do this: the two withdrawals that cause seizures + delirium are Alcohol and Sedatives (remember "A&S = Alcohol & Sedatives are Serious").

Psychotic Disorder (F1x.5)

A substance-induced psychotic disorder (hallucinations and/or delusions caused by the substance, occurring during or shortly after use) is a cluster of psychotic phenomena that occur during or immediately after substance use (typically within 2 weeks), characterised by hallucinations (often auditory but perceptions of any modality), delusions/ideas of reference, psychomotor disturbances, and abnormal affect. Consciousness is essentially clear, though some clouding may occur. The symptoms typically resolve at least partially within a month and fully within about six months — this time-limited course helps distinguish it from a primary psychotic disorder such as schizophrenia. ICD-10

ICD-10 Sub-codes

.50 schizophrenia-like; .51 predominantly delusional; .52 predominantly hallucinatory; .53 predominantly polymorphic; .54 predominantly depressive symptoms; .55 predominantly manic symptoms; .56 mixed.

Identifying clue

Psychotic symptoms in clear consciousness, arising in close relation to heavy substance use (classically alcoholic hallucinosis, or cannabis/stimulant/hallucinogen psychosis), that resolve as the drug clears — a temporal link that primary schizophrenia does not show.

Mnemonic — "CLEAR"

Clear consciousness (not clouded, unlike delirium) · Linked in time to substance use (within ~2 weeks) · Early resolution (partly within a month, fully within ~6 months) · All modalities of hallucination possible (often auditory) · Reverses as the drug clears.
Contrast to remember: substance psychosis CLEARs; schizophrenia lingers.

Amnesic Syndrome (F1x.6)

A substance-induced amnesic syndrome (a chronic, prominent impairment of memory caused by the substance, with other cognition relatively spared) is characterised by chronic prominent impairment of recent and remote memory, with preserved immediate recall, and impaired ability to learn new material. Confabulation (unintentionally filling memory gaps with false recollections, without intent to deceive) may be marked. The classic example is the alcohol-related amnesic (Korsakoff) syndrome due to thiamine deficiency, described in the alcohol section. The general criteria correspond to the organic amnesic syndrome (see F04 in the F00–F09 handbook). ICD-10

Mnemonic — "MICA"

Memory (recent and remote) badly impaired · Immediate recall preserved · Confabulation (making up stories to fill gaps) · Alert with intact general intellect.
Cause reflex: alcohol → thiamine deficiency → Korsakoff amnesia. Treat with thiamine.

Residual and Late-Onset Psychotic Disorder (F1x.7)

Disorders in which substance-induced changes in cognition, affect, personality, or behaviour persist beyond the period during which a direct substance-related effect might reasonably be expected. ICD-10 Sub-codes:

  • .70 Flashbacks (brief, spontaneous re-experiencing of drug effects — e.g. visual disturbances — long after the drug was taken, classically with hallucinogens).
  • .71 Personality or behaviour disorder.
  • .72 Residual affective disorder (persisting mood change).
  • .73 Dementia (persisting global cognitive decline, e.g. alcohol-related dementia).
  • .74 Other persisting cognitive impairment.
  • .75 Late-onset psychotic disorder.
Exam point: this category answers the question "what conditions can persist or appear after the acute drug effect and withdrawal are over?" — flashbacks, persisting personality/mood change, substance-related dementia, and late psychosis.

Mnemonic for the residual sub-types — "FP-MD-CL"

Flashbacks (.70) · Personality/behaviour change (.71) · Mood (residual affective) disorder (.72) · Dementia (.73) · Cognitive impairment, other persisting (.74) · Late-onset psychotic disorder (.75).
One-line recall: "things left behind after the drug is gone."

F10 · Disorders due to Alcohol Use → ICD-11 6C40

ICD-10 F10 (with .0–.9 syndromes)
ICD-11 6C40 (episode of harmful use, harmful pattern, dependence, intoxication, withdrawal)
Drug type Central nervous system depressant
Exam weight Highest — most-tested substance

1. Introduction and mechanism

Alcohol (ethanol) is a central nervous system depressant (a substance that slows down brain activity). It enhances the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid — the brain's main "calming" chemical) and blocks the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate at NMDA receptors (the brain's main "stimulating" chemical). With chronic use the brain adapts by reducing GABA activity and increasing glutamate activity; when alcohol is suddenly stopped, this leaves the brain in a state of over-excitation — which explains why alcohol withdrawal produces tremor, agitation, and seizures (the opposite of alcohol's depressant effect).

2. Epidemiology

  • Alcohol is among the most widely used psychoactive substances worldwide and a leading contributor to the global burden of disease, injury, and premature death. Other evidence
  • Alcohol use disorders are considerably more common in men than women, though the gap is narrowing.
  • Strong associations with accidents, violence, liver disease, and numerous cancers; alcohol dependence frequently coexists with depression and anxiety disorders. Kaplan & Sadock

3. Identifying symptoms by clinical syndrome

Acute intoxication (F10.0)

Disinhibition, euphoria then sedation, slurred speech (unclear, drawn-out speech), unsteady gait (ataxia — loss of coordination), nystagmus (jerking eye movements), impaired attention and judgement; in severe cases stupor and coma. Pathological intoxication = extreme aggression after a small amount.

Withdrawal state (F10.3) — identify the timeline

Time after last drinkFeatures
6–12 hoursTremor ("the shakes"), sweating, anxiety, nausea, insomnia, tachycardia (fast heart rate), hypertension
12–24 hoursAlcoholic hallucinosis (hallucinations — often visual or tactile — with an otherwise clear mind)

4. Associated conditions and complications (what happens with heavy use and on withdrawal)

Key associated conditions — high-yield for exams

  • Delirium tremens (DTs) (severe alcohol-withdrawal delirium, typically 48–72 hours after stopping): clouding of consciousness, disorientation, vivid visual and tactile hallucinations (classically formication — the sensation of insects crawling on the skin), marked tremor, agitation, fever, sweating, and autonomic overactivity (fast heart rate, high blood pressure). It is a medical emergency with appreciable mortality if untreated.
  • Wernicke encephalopathy (an acute, reversible brain disorder from thiamine / vitamin B1 deficiency): the triad of confusion + ataxia + ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus (abnormal eye movements). A medical emergency requiring urgent thiamine.
  • Korsakoff syndrome (the chronic, often irreversible amnesic state that follows untreated Wernicke): profound inability to form new memories (anterograde amnesia) with confabulation (unintentional filling of memory gaps). Together these form Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.
  • Alcoholic hallucinosis: persistent hallucinations (often auditory) in clear consciousness during or after heavy drinking — distinct from DTs (no clouding of consciousness).
  • Alcohol-related dementia and other persisting cognitive impairment (F10.73/.74).
  • Morbid jealousy (Othello syndrome) — delusional belief of a partner's infidelity, associated with chronic alcohol dependence.
  • Physical: liver disease (fatty liver, hepatitis, cirrhosis), pancreatitis, gastritis, peripheral neuropathy, cardiomyopathy, and fetal alcohol syndrome (birth defects and developmental problems in a baby exposed to alcohol in the womb).
  • Psychiatric: depression, anxiety, and elevated suicide risk.

5. Diagnostic criteria (from ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10

Diagnosed using the shared syndrome guidelines applied to alcohol: F10.0 acute intoxication, F10.1 harmful use, F10.2 dependence syndrome (three or more dependence features in the past year), F10.3 withdrawal state, F10.4 withdrawal state with delirium (delirium tremens), F10.5 psychotic disorder (alcoholic hallucinosis), F10.6 amnesic syndrome (Korsakoff).

ICD-11

6C40 with sub-codes: 6C40.0 episode of harmful use; 6C40.1 harmful pattern (episodic/continuous); 6C40.2 alcohol dependence (two or more of impaired control, precedence, physiological features); 6C40.3 alcohol intoxication (severity specified); 6C40.4 alcohol withdrawal (with sub-specifiers including uncomplicated, with perceptual disturbances, with seizures, with delirium). Alcohol-induced psychotic, mood, and other disorders are separately coded.

6. Easy way to remember

Memory aids

Withdrawal timeline — "6-12-24-48": 6 h shakes, 12 h see things (hallucinosis), 24–48 h fits (seizures), 48–72 h DTs.

Wernicke triad — "CAN": Confusion, Ataxia, Nystagmus (eye signs).

Complications of chronic alcohol — "WK HALT": Wernicke, Korsakoff, Hallucinosis, A = alcoholic dementia, Liver disease/cirrhosis, T = morbid jealousy (Othello). Treatment reflex: give thiamine before glucose (glucose alone can trigger Wernicke).

7. Treatment

Pharmacological Kaplan & Sadock

  • Withdrawal / detoxification: benzodiazepines (sedative drugs that are the first-line treatment for alcohol withdrawal and delirium tremens), e.g. chlordiazepoxide or diazepam, on a reducing schedule; parenteral thiamine to prevent Wernicke.
  • Relapse prevention: disulfiram (a drug that causes an unpleasant reaction if alcohol is taken, acting as a deterrent); acamprosate and naltrexone (anti-craving medications that reduce the urge to drink).

Psychological

  • Motivational interviewing (a collaborative counselling style that strengthens the person's own motivation to change); cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) (therapy that identifies and changes the thoughts and situations that trigger drinking); relapse-prevention training.
  • Brief interventions; Alcoholics Anonymous and other mutual-support groups; family and social interventions.

F11 · Disorders due to Opioid Use → ICD-11 6C43

ICD-10 F11
ICD-11 6C43
Drug type CNS depressant (analgesic)
Examples Heroin, morphine, codeine, methadone, tramadol, prescription painkillers

1. Introduction and mechanism

Opioids (drugs derived from or acting like opium, used medically as strong painkillers) act on opioid receptors (the brain and body's natural pain- and pleasure-signalling docking sites), producing pain relief, euphoria, and sedation, and slowing breathing. Tolerance and physical dependence develop rapidly.

2. Epidemiology

A major cause of drug-related death because of overdose; high rates of injecting-related infection; strong link with dependence and criminal-justice involvement. Kaplan & Sadock

3. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Intoxication: euphoria then drowsiness ("nodding"), pinpoint pupils (miosis) (abnormally small pupils — a classic opioid sign), slurred speech, reduced consciousness; in overdose, the lethal triad of coma + pinpoint pupils + respiratory depression (dangerously slow, shallow breathing).
  • Withdrawal: not usually life-threatening but very distressing — "flu-like" picture: dilated pupils, yawning, runny nose and eyes (lacrimation/rhinorrhoea), sweating, gooseflesh (piloerection — hence "cold turkey"), muscle and bone aches, abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, vomiting, and intense craving.

Associated conditions and complications

  • Overdose from respiratory depression — the leading cause of death; reversed by naloxone (an emergency antidote that blocks opioid receptors).
  • Injecting complications: HIV, hepatitis B and C, infective endocarditis (heart-valve infection), abscesses, deep-vein thrombosis.
  • Neonatal abstinence syndrome in babies of dependent mothers.

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Shared syndromes applied to opioids (F11.0–.9): intoxication, harmful use, dependence, withdrawal (F11.3), withdrawal with delirium, etc.

ICD-11

6C43 with episode of harmful use, harmful pattern, dependence, intoxication, and withdrawal sub-codes.

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

Overdose triad — "PPP": Pinpoint pupils, Poorly breathing (respiratory depression), Plummeting consciousness (coma). Antidote = naloxone.

Withdrawal — "WATERY": Watering eyes/nose (lacrimation, rhinorrhoea), Aches (muscle/bone), Tremor + Tachycardia, Enlarged pupils (dilated), Runs (diarrhoea) + cramps, Yawning + gooseflesh. Not fatal, but very distressing ("cold turkey").

Remember: opioid intoxication and withdrawal are mirror images (pinpoint vs dilated pupils).

7. Treatment

  • Overdose: naloxone (opioid antagonist) — life-saving.
  • Withdrawal / maintenance: opioid substitution therapy (replacing an illicit opioid with a prescribed, longer-acting one under supervision) with methadone or buprenorphine; symptomatic relief with lofexidine/clonidine.
  • Relapse prevention: naltrexone (opioid blocker); psychosocial support, CBT, contingency management, needle-exchange and harm-reduction services. Kaplan & Sadock

F12 · Disorders due to Cannabinoid Use → ICD-11 6C41 (cannabis) / 6C42 (synthetic)

ICD-10 F12
ICD-11 6C41 cannabis; 6C42 synthetic cannabinoids
Drug type Cannabinoid (mixed effects)
Examples Marijuana, hashish; synthetic "spice"

1–2. Introduction, mechanism, epidemiology

Cannabis acts on cannabinoid (CB1) receptors (brain docking sites that regulate mood, perception and appetite); the main active compound is THC (tetrahydrocannabinol). One of the most widely used illicit substances, especially among young people.

3. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Intoxication: euphoria and relaxation, altered time perception, increased appetite ("the munchies"), conjunctival injection (red eyes), dry mouth, tachycardia, impaired coordination and short-term memory; anxiety or paranoia at high doses.
  • Withdrawal (recognised in ICD-11 and increasingly in practice): irritability, anxiety, sleep disturbance and vivid dreams, reduced appetite, restlessness, low mood — milder than alcohol/opioid withdrawal.

Associated conditions

  • Cannabis-induced psychosis (F12.5) and a recognised association with later schizophrenia, particularly with heavy use in adolescence and with high-potency products.
  • Amotivational syndrome (a state of apathy, reduced drive and poor goal-directed activity linked to chronic heavy use).
  • Synthetic cannabinoids ("spice") can cause severe agitation, psychosis, and medical emergencies.

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Shared syndromes applied to cannabinoids (F12.0–.9).

ICD-11

6C41 (cannabis) and separately 6C42 (synthetic cannabinoids), each with harmful use, dependence, intoxication and withdrawal.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — intoxication "RED-CAT"

Red eyes (conjunctival injection) · Euphoria/relaxation · Dry mouth · Cravings for food ("the munchies") · Altered time perception · Tachycardia (fast heart). High doses → anxiety/paranoia.

Associations: "spice" (synthetic) is far more dangerous; heavy adolescent use raises later schizophrenia risk; chronic use → amotivational syndrome.

Treatment: largely psychological — motivational interviewing, CBT, and contingency management; treat associated anxiety or psychosis; no established specific pharmacotherapy for dependence. Kaplan & Sadock

F13 · Disorders due to Sedative or Hypnotic Use → ICD-11 6C44

ICD-10 F13
ICD-11 6C44
Drug type CNS depressant
Examples Benzodiazepines, "Z-drugs", barbiturates

1–2. Introduction and mechanism

Sedatives and hypnotics (drugs that calm and induce sleep), especially benzodiazepines (e.g. diazepam, lorazepam — anti-anxiety/sleep drugs), enhance GABA activity. Like alcohol, they are CNS depressants, so their withdrawal is dangerously over-excitatory. Dependence often develops iatrogenically (as a result of medical prescribing).

3. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Intoxication: resembles alcohol without the smell — sedation, slurred speech, ataxia, poor coordination, impaired memory, disinhibition.
  • Withdrawal: anxiety, insomnia, tremor, sweating, perceptual disturbances, and — importantly — seizures and delirium, as with alcohol.

Associated conditions

  • Withdrawal seizures and delirium (potentially fatal) — sedative withdrawal must be tapered slowly.
  • Dangerous synergy with alcohol and opioids (combined respiratory depression and overdose risk).
  • Anterograde amnesia and falls (especially in the elderly).

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Shared syndromes applied to sedatives/hypnotics (F13.0–.9).

ICD-11

6C44 with harmful use, dependence, intoxication and withdrawal sub-codes.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — "alcohol in a pill" + danger word "SWAY"

Think of sedatives as "alcohol in pill form" — same depressant intoxication, same dangerous withdrawal. Withdrawal danger — "SWAY": Seizures, Withdrawal delirium, Anxiety/agitation + tremor, Y = don't stop abruptly (must taper). Deadly combo: sedatives + alcohol + opioids = additive respiratory depression.

Treatment: gradual dose reduction (slow taper, often switching to a long-acting benzodiazepine); overdose antidote is flumazenil (used cautiously); psychological support and treatment of underlying anxiety/insomnia. Kaplan & Sadock

F14 · Disorders due to Cocaine Use → ICD-11 6C45

ICD-10 F14
ICD-11 6C45
Drug type CNS stimulant
Examples Cocaine powder, crack cocaine

1–2. Introduction and mechanism

Cocaine is a powerful CNS stimulant (a substance that speeds up brain activity) that blocks reuptake of dopamine (the brain's reward/pleasure chemical), noradrenaline and serotonin, causing intense but short-lived euphoria. Strong psychological dependence develops quickly.

3. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Intoxication: euphoria, increased energy and alertness, grandiosity, dilated pupils (mydriasis), tachycardia, hypertension, reduced appetite; at high doses agitation, paranoia, and formication ("cocaine bugs" — the sensation of insects crawling under the skin).
  • Withdrawal ("the crash"): the opposite of intoxication — intense low mood, fatigue, hypersomnia, increased appetite, vivid unpleasant dreams, and strong craving; high suicide risk during the crash.

Associated conditions

  • Cardiovascular emergencies: myocardial infarction (heart attack), arrhythmias, stroke, and hypertensive crisis — even in young users.
  • Cocaine-induced psychosis with persecutory delusions and formication.
  • Nasal septum perforation (snorting); seizures; sudden death.

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Shared syndromes applied to cocaine (F14.0–.9).

ICD-11

6C45 with harmful use, dependence, intoxication and withdrawal sub-codes.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — intoxication "HIGH", withdrawal "CRASH"

Intoxication "HIGH": Heart racing + hypertension, I = big pupils (dilated), Grandiosity/euphoria, Hallucinations of bugs (formication) + paranoia at high dose.

Withdrawal "CRASH": Craving, R = fatigue/rest (hypersomnia), Appetite up, Sad mood (dysphoria, high suicide risk), Horrible vivid dreams.

Danger: heart attack/arrhythmia/stroke even in the young; cocaine psychosis; nasal septum perforation.

Treatment: no established specific pharmacotherapy; mainstay is psychological — CBT, contingency management, and relapse prevention; treat cardiovascular complications and cocaine-induced psychosis medically. Kaplan & Sadock

F15 · Disorders due to Other Stimulants, including Caffeine → ICD-11 6C46 / 6C47 / 6C48

ICD-10 F15
ICD-11 6C46 stimulants (amfetamines/methamfetamine); 6C47 synthetic cathinones; 6C48 caffeine
Drug type CNS stimulant
Examples Amfetamine, methamfetamine, methylphenidate, "bath salts", caffeine

1–2. Introduction and mechanism

Amfetamine-type stimulants increase release and block reuptake of dopamine and noradrenaline, producing effects similar to cocaine but longer-lasting. ICD-11 gives synthetic cathinones ("bath salts", 6C47) and caffeine (6C48) their own stems.

3. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Intoxication: euphoria, hyperalertness, grandiosity, reduced need for sleep, dilated pupils, tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia; prolonged binges cause paranoia and stimulant psychosis that closely mimics paranoid schizophrenia.
  • Withdrawal: like the cocaine crash — fatigue, hypersomnia, increased appetite, dysphoria, and craving.
  • Caffeine: intoxication = restlessness, insomnia, palpitations, diuresis; withdrawal = headache, fatigue, low mood, poor concentration.

Associated conditions

  • Amfetamine/methamfetamine psychosis (paranoid, with hallucinations and formication) — a classic model of drug-induced schizophrenia-like psychosis.
  • Cardiovascular events, hyperthermia, seizures; dental decay ("meth mouth"); weight loss; aggression.

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Shared syndromes applied to other stimulants including caffeine (F15.0–.9).

ICD-11

6C46 (stimulants), 6C47 (synthetic cathinones), and 6C48 (caffeine — including caffeine intoxication and caffeine withdrawal), each with the standard syndrome sub-codes.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — same "HIGH / CRASH" as cocaine, plus "PARANOID binge"

Amfetamines act like a longer-lasting cocaine, so use the same "HIGH" (intoxication) and "CRASH" (withdrawal) mnemonics. Signature: stimulant psychosis looks like paranoid schizophrenia but follows a binge and clears with the drug. Caffeine mini-mnemonic — intoxication "RIP": Restless, Insomnia, Palpitations; withdrawal = headache + fatigue + low mood.

Treatment: supportive management of intoxication and psychosis (benzodiazepines for agitation, short-term antipsychotics for psychosis); psychological treatments (CBT, contingency management) for dependence; treat cardiovascular and hyperthermic complications. Kaplan & Sadock

F16 · Disorders due to Hallucinogen Use → ICD-11 6C49

ICD-10 F16
ICD-11 6C49
Drug type Hallucinogen (perception-altering)
Examples LSD, psilocybin ("magic mushrooms"), mescaline

1–2. Introduction and mechanism

Hallucinogens (drugs that profoundly alter perception, thought and mood, often producing hallucinations) act largely on serotonin receptors (a brain chemical involved in mood and perception). They produce marked perceptual changes with relatively little physical dependence.

3. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Intoxication ("a trip"): vivid visual illusions and hallucinations, synaesthesia (a blending of the senses — e.g. "seeing" sounds), altered sense of time and self, intensified emotions, dilated pupils; a frightening experience is a "bad trip" with panic and paranoia.
  • No characteristic physical withdrawal syndrome — a key distinguishing feature; tolerance develops rapidly but physical dependence is minimal.

Associated conditions

  • Flashbacks / hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (spontaneous, brief re-experiencing of drug-like perceptual disturbances long after the drug was taken) — coded under residual disorders (F16.70).
  • Accidents and injuries from impaired judgement during intoxication; precipitation or worsening of psychosis in vulnerable people.

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Shared syndromes applied to hallucinogens (F16.0–.9), including flashbacks under F16.70.

ICD-11

6C49 with harmful use, dependence and intoxication; note the characteristic absence of a physical withdrawal syndrome.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — "TRIP"

Time and self perception altered · R = vivid hallucinations + synaesthesia (senses blend) · I = dilated pupils (and intense emotion) · P = no Physical withdrawal (key point) but flashbacks may occur later.
One-line recall: "big perceptual trip, no physical withdrawal, flashbacks after."

Treatment: a "bad trip" is managed by reassurance in a calm environment ("talking down") and a benzodiazepine if needed; treat persistent psychosis; psychological support for problematic use. Kaplan & Sadock

F17 · Disorders due to Tobacco / Nicotine Use → ICD-11 6C4A

ICD-10 F17 (tobacco)
ICD-11 6C4A (nicotine)
Drug type Stimulant (nicotine)
Examples Cigarettes, chewing tobacco, e-cigarettes

1–2. Introduction and mechanism

Nicotine (the addictive stimulant in tobacco) acts on nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and the dopamine reward pathway. Dependence is very common and physically driven.

3. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Intoxication is rarely a clinical issue; the main disorder is dependence with strong craving.
  • Withdrawal: irritability, anxiety, restlessness, difficulty concentrating, increased appetite and weight gain, low mood, and intense craving — beginning within hours of the last cigarette.

Associated conditions

  • Major cause of preventable death: cardiovascular disease, chronic lung disease, and many cancers (lung, mouth, throat, bladder).
  • High comorbidity with other mental disorders (very high smoking rates in schizophrenia).

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Shared syndromes applied to tobacco (F17.0–.9), dependence and withdrawal being the main entities.

ICD-11

6C4A (nicotine) with harmful use, dependence, and withdrawal.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — withdrawal "CRAImB"

Craving · Restlessness · Anxiety · Irritability · m = poor concentration (mind foggy) · B = bigger appetite / weight gain + low mood.
Key point: for tobacco/nicotine the clinical disorder is dependence and withdrawal, not intoxication.

Treatment: nicotine replacement therapy (patches, gum, lozenges that supply nicotine without tobacco smoke); varenicline and bupropion (anti-craving medications); behavioural support, brief advice, and quit-lines. Kaplan & Sadock

F18 · Disorders due to Volatile Solvent / Inhalant Use → ICD-11 6C4B

ICD-10 F18
ICD-11 6C4B (volatile inhalants)
Drug type CNS depressant (inhaled)
Examples Glue, petrol, paint thinners, aerosols, butane

1–2. Introduction, mechanism, epidemiology

Volatile solvents / inhalants (everyday chemicals whose fumes are breathed in to get intoxicated) are CNS depressants. Use is most common among children and adolescents because these substances are cheap and easily available.

3. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Intoxication: rapid, short-lived alcohol-like intoxication — euphoria, dizziness, slurred speech, ataxia, and sometimes hallucinations; a chemical smell on breath/clothing and a peri-oral rash ("glue-sniffer's rash") are giveaway signs.

Associated conditions

  • "Sudden sniffing death" (fatal heart rhythm disturbance during or right after inhalation).
  • Damage to brain, liver, kidney, bone marrow, and peripheral nerves with chronic use; cognitive impairment.

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Shared syndromes applied to volatile solvents (F18.0–.9).

ICD-11

6C4B with harmful use, dependence, intoxication and withdrawal.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — "SNIFF"

Sudden sniffing death (fatal arrhythmia) is the great danger · N = young (children/adolescents) · Intoxication is alcohol-like but very brief · F = chemical smell + facial "glue-sniffer's" rash · F = failure of organs (brain, liver, kidney, marrow) with chronic use.

Treatment: largely psychosocial and educational; family and social intervention; treat cognitive and organ complications; no specific pharmacotherapy. Kaplan & Sadock

F19 · Multiple Drug Use and Use of Other Psychoactive Substances → ICD-11 6C4C–6C4G

ICD-10 F19
ICD-11 6C4C MDMA/related; 6C4D dissociatives; 6C4E other; 6C4F multiple; 6C4G unknown
Drug type Mixed
Examples Polydrug use; MDMA/ecstasy; ketamine, PCP

1. Introduction

Used when a disorder arises from the use of multiple substances together (so that no single drug predominates), or from substances not covered by F10–F18. ICD-11 has created distinct new stems for several previously "other" substances.

2–3. Key members and identifying features

Identifying clues

  • Polydrug use (F19): a chaotic pattern in which several substances are taken, often to enhance or counteract each other's effects; complicates both intoxication and withdrawal pictures.
  • MDMA / ecstasy (6C4C) (an "entactogen" stimulant-hallucinogen used at parties): euphoria, emotional closeness, increased energy; risks of hyperthermia (dangerous overheating), hyponatraemia (dangerously low blood sodium from drinking too much water), and serotonin toxicity.
  • Dissociative drugs (6C4D) such as ketamine and PCP (drugs producing a sense of detachment from the body and surroundings): dissociation, hallucinations, analgesia, nystagmus; PCP is associated with agitation and violence.

Associated conditions

  • Overdose risk is amplified by combining depressants (alcohol + opioids + benzodiazepines).
  • MDMA: hyperthermia, water intoxication/hyponatraemia, liver damage; ketamine: bladder damage ("ketamine cystitis"); PCP: violent behaviour and psychosis.

4–5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

F19 used for multiple drug use and other/unspecified psychoactive substances, applying the shared syndromes.

ICD-11

Distinct stems: 6C4C MDMA and related drugs; 6C4D dissociative drugs including ketamine and PCP; 6C4E other specified; 6C4F multiple specified substances; 6C4G unknown or unspecified.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — "COCKTAIL"

F19 = a "COCKTAIL" of drugs (no single one predominates), or the newer substances with their own hazards: MDMA → remember "HHH" (Hyperthermia, Hyponatraemia, Happy/empathic mood); ketamine/PCP → dissociation + nystagmus (PCP = violence, ketamine = bladder damage).

Treatment: supportive and symptom-directed for intoxication/complications (cooling and fluids for MDMA hyperthermia, benzodiazepines for agitation); psychosocial treatment of the underlying pattern of use. Kaplan & Sadock

Two golden rules to carry into the exam

Rule 1 — "Withdrawal is the mirror of intoxication"

Depressants (alcohol, opioids, sedatives, inhalants): calm intoxication → over-excited withdrawal. Stimulants (cocaine, amfetamines, nicotine): "up" intoxication → "crashed" withdrawal. Opioid pupils flip: pinpoint (intoxication) ↔ dilated (withdrawal).

Rule 2 — "Which withdrawals can kill?"

Alcohol and sedatives (the two GABA depressants) — seizures and delirium; a medical emergency. Opioid withdrawal is miserable but rarely fatal.
