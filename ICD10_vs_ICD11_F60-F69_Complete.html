The F60–F69 block: what holds it together

ICD-10 gathers under “disorders of adult personality and behaviour” a broad set of conditions in which the problem lies not in a discrete illness episode but in enduring patterns of the person's character, impulses, identity or sexual behaviour. The block runs from F60 to F69.

The territories are: F60 specific personality disorders (with nine subtypes); F61 mixed and other personality disorders; F62 enduring personality change not due to brain damage (acquired after catastrophe or after psychiatric illness); F63 habit and impulse disorders (gambling, fire-setting, stealing, hair-pulling); F64 gender identity disorders; F65 disorders of sexual preference (the paraphilias); F66 psychological and behavioural disorders associated with sexual development and orientation; F68 other disorders of adult personality/behaviour (symptom elaboration, factitious disorder); and F69 unspecified.

Terms in plain language. Personality disorder = a long-standing, pervasive, inflexible pattern of relating and behaving that deviates from cultural expectation and causes distress or dysfunction; ego-syntonic = the person sees the trait as part of themselves and not a problem (typical of personality disorder); ego-dystonic = the person experiences the symptom as alien/unwanted; anankastic = obsessional/perfectionistic; dissocial = antisocial; paraphilia = a disorder of sexual preference; egodystonic here also names an old ICD-10 category; trait = a stable dimension of personality.

ICD-10 categorical vs ICD-11 dimensional — the biggest change in the classification

Personality disorders are where ICD-11 made its most radical break with ICD-10. This is the single most examinable point in the block, so state it plainly:

ICD-10 — categorical “types”

  • Personality disorder is diagnosed by matching the person to one of nine named subtypes (paranoid, schizoid, dissocial, emotionally unstable, histrionic, anankastic, anxious, dependent, plus “other”).
  • Each subtype has its own list of descriptive criteria; a person either “is” or “is not” that type.
  • Problem in practice: heavy overlap, most patients meet several types, and “mixed” (F61) is over-used.

ICD-11 — one diagnosis, graded by severity + traits

  • There is a single diagnosis: Personality Disorder (6D10), first graded by severity — 6D10.0 mild, 6D10.1 moderate, 6D10.2 severe (plus “personality difficulty” as a sub-threshold Z-code).
  • It is then described by prominent trait domains (6D11): 6D11.0 Negative affectivity, 6D11.1 Detachment, 6D11.2 Dissociality, 6D11.3 Disinhibition, 6D11.4 Anankastia, and a 6D11.5 Borderline pattern qualifier.
  • The nine categorical subtypes are abolished. Severity (how much dysfunction) matters more than the label.

The one-line exam answer

ICD-10 classifies personality disorder into nine categorical subtypes; ICD-11 replaces them with a single dimensional diagnosis graded by severity (mild/moderate/severe) and described by five trait domains plus a borderline pattern qualifier. The shift is from “which type?” to “how severe, and which traits?” — because severity predicts outcome better than the old categories, which overlapped too much.

ICD-11 also relocated several F60–F69 conditions out of this block entirely (mirroring what it did to sleep and sexual disorders in F50–F59):

  • Pathological gambling (F63.0) → 6C50 Gambling disorder, now grouped with Disorders due to addictive behaviours (alongside the brand-new 6C51 Gaming disorder), not with impulse disorders.
  • Trichotillomania (F63.3) → 6B25.0, now a body-focused repetitive behaviour within Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders (with the new 6B25.1 excoriation / skin-picking disorder).
  • Gender identity disorders (F64) → recast as Gender incongruence and moved to a chapter on conditions related to sexual health — deliberately depsychiatrised (no longer a mental disorder).
  • Egodystonic sexual orientation (F66.1) is deleted: sexual orientation is not a disorder.
  • Pyromania, kleptomania, intermittent explosive disorder, compulsive sexual behaviour → a coherent new grouping, Impulse control disorders (6C70–6C73).
  • Paraphilic disorders are retained but reframed around risk/harm to others or to self rather than mere non-normative arousal.

F60 · Specific personality disorders — the general criteria

Group: F60 is the personality-disorder group within F60–F69. In ICD-11 the whole of F60 collapses into the single dimensional diagnosis 6D10 Personality disorder graded by severity and described by trait domains (6D11).

A specific personality disorder is a severe disturbance in the characterological constitution and behavioural tendencies of the individual, usually involving several areas of the personality, and nearly always associated with considerable personal and social disruption. It tends to appear in late childhood or adolescence and continues into adulthood; the diagnosis is therefore unlikely to be appropriate before age 16–17.

ICD-10 general diagnostic guidelines (apply to all F60 subtypes)

Conditions not directly attributable to gross brain damage or disease, or to another psychiatric disorder, meeting the following:

  • (a) markedly disharmonious attitudes and behaviour, usually involving several areas of functioning (affectivity, arousal, impulse control, ways of perceiving and thinking, and style of relating to others);
  • (b) the abnormal behaviour pattern is enduring, of long standing, and not limited to episodes of mental illness;
  • (c) the abnormal pattern is pervasive and clearly maladaptive to a broad range of personal and social situations;
  • (d) the manifestations always appear during childhood or adolescence and continue into adulthood;
  • (e) the disorder leads to considerable personal distress, but this may become apparent only late in its course;
  • (f) the disorder is usually, but not always, associated with significant problems in occupational and social performance.

Cyclothymia and schizotypal disorder were formerly classed here but are now placed elsewhere (F34 and F21 respectively).

ICD-11 approach (6D10 + 6D11)

ICD-11 requires an enduring disturbance in how a person experiences and thinks about themselves, others and the world, manifest in maladaptive patterns of cognition, emotional experience/expression and behaviour, sustained over time (≥2 years) and across situations, causing significant distress or impairment in relationships, work or other areas. Then:

  • Grade severity: 6D10.0 mild, 6D10.1 moderate, 6D10.2 severe (based on extent of dysfunction, risk to self/others, and pervasiveness).
  • Add trait qualifiers (6D11): negative affectivity, detachment, dissociality, disinhibition, anankastia, and/or a borderline pattern.

ICD-11 also permits “personality difficulty” as a sub-diagnostic descriptor (a QE-range code) for problems that do not reach disorder threshold.

Etiology (with psychological theories)

Kaplan & Sadock Personality disorders arise from an interaction of genetic/temperamental vulnerability (heritable traits, emotional reactivity), early adversity (abuse, neglect, invalidating environments, insecure attachment), and developmental/social factors.

Psychological theories

  • Psychodynamic / object-relations (Kernberg). Other evidence Immature defences such as splitting (seeing people as all-good or all-bad) and projective identification produce the unstable relationships and identity diffusion central to borderline organisation.
  • Attachment theory (Bowlby). Disorganised/insecure early attachment shapes later patterns of fearful dependence, avoidance of intimacy, or hostile mistrust.
  • Biosocial theory (Linehan). Borderline patterns arise from a biological tendency to emotional dysregulation meeting a chronically invalidating environment — the rationale for dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT).
  • Cognitive theory (Beck & Young). Early maladaptive schemas (e.g. abandonment, mistrust, defectiveness) and dysfunctional core beliefs drive the rigid interpersonal patterns; basis for schema therapy.
  • Trait / dimensional models (Five-Factor). Personality pathology is the extreme of normal trait dimensions — the empirical foundation of the ICD-11 trait model.

Epidemiology

  • Personality disorders (any) affect roughly 6–10% of the general population; higher in clinical and forensic settings.
  • Borderline pattern is prevalent in clinical samples and more often diagnosed in women; dissocial/antisocial is more common in men and in forensic populations.
  • Onset by adolescence/early adulthood; some patterns (especially borderline, antisocial) tend to attenuate with age.

Common comorbidities

High rates of coexisting mood, anxiety, substance-use, eating and psychotic disorders. Personality disorder worsens the course and treatment response of these Axis-I conditions. Self-harm and suicide risk is markedly raised, especially with the borderline pattern.

Remember the F60 “PD” core — “PERSON”

  • P — Pervasive across situations
  • E — Enduring / early onset (adolescence)
  • R — Relationships and functioning impaired
  • S — Stable and inflexible pattern
  • O — Own distress (may appear late) / ego-syntonic traits
  • N — Not due to another disorder or brain disease

Treatment (applies across the personality disorders)

  • Psychological (mainstay): structured, longer-term psychotherapies — DBT, mentalization-based treatment (MBT), schema therapy, transference-focused therapy, and structured clinical management, especially for the borderline pattern.
  • Pharmacological (adjunctive only): no drug is licensed to “treat” personality disorder; medication targets comorbidity and specific symptom domains (e.g. mood instability, transient psychotic-like symptoms, impulsivity). Avoid polypharmacy and long-term sedatives.
  • General principles: consistency, clear boundaries, treating comorbid disorders, managing crises and self-harm risk, and a long-term therapeutic relationship.
Differential diagnosis (general)

From enduring personality change (F62) — acquired in adult life after catastrophe/illness, not developmental; from organic personality disorder (F07.0) — due to brain disease/injury; from mood, anxiety and psychotic disorders whose symptoms are episodic rather than lifelong traits; and from normal personality variation / accentuation of traits (Z73.1) that does not cause the required distress or dysfunction.

F60.0 · Paranoid personality disorder

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the suspicious/mistrustful pattern. ICD-11 maps this mainly onto the Detachment and Negative affectivity trait domains.

ICD-10 (F60.0) — personality disorder characterised by:

  • (a) excessive sensitivity to setbacks and rebuffs;
  • (b) tendency to bear grudges persistently (refusal to forgive insults, injuries or slights);
  • (c) suspiciousness and a pervasive tendency to distort experience by misconstruing neutral or friendly actions of others as hostile or contemptuous;
  • (d) a combative and tenacious sense of personal rights out of keeping with the actual situation;
  • (e) recurrent unjustified suspicions regarding the sexual fidelity of a spouse or partner;
  • (f) tendency to excessive self-importance (persistent self-referential attitude);
  • (g) preoccupation with unsubstantiated “conspiratorial” explanations of events.
Includes: expansive paranoid, fanatic, querulant and sensitive paranoid personality. Excludes: delusional disorder (F22.-); schizophrenia (F20.-).

ICD-11

No separate code: diagnosed as Personality disorder (6D10) at the appropriate severity, with prominent Detachment (6D11.1) and Negative affectivity (6D11.0) traits (mistrust, suspiciousness, hostility).

Differential diagnosis

From delusional disorder (F22) and paranoid schizophrenia (F20) — there the beliefs reach delusional intensity and there may be hallucinations; in paranoid PD the suspiciousness is an over-valued trait, not a fixed delusion. From schizotypal disorder (F21). From reactive suspiciousness in the context of substance use or a mood/psychotic episode.

Remember

“Suspicious, sensitive, holds grudges, self-important, conspiratorial” — trait-level mistrust that never quite becomes delusional.

F60.1 · Schizoid personality disorder

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the emotionally detached, solitary pattern. ICD-11 maps this onto the Detachment trait domain.

ICD-10 (F60.1) — meeting the following:

  • (a) few, if any, activities provide pleasure;
  • (b) emotional coldness, detachment or flattened affect;
  • (c) limited capacity to express warm, tender feelings or anger towards others;
  • (d) apparent indifference to praise or criticism;
  • (e) little interest in sexual experiences with another person (allowing for age);
  • (f) almost invariable preference for solitary activities;
  • (g) excessive preoccupation with fantasy and introspection;
  • (h) lack of close friends or confiding relationships (or only one), and no desire for such;
  • (i) marked insensitivity to prevailing social norms and conventions.
Excludes: Asperger's syndrome (F84.5); delusional disorder (F22.0); schizoid disorder of childhood (F84.5); schizophrenia (F20.-); schizotypal disorder (F21).

ICD-11

Diagnosed as Personality disorder (6D10) with prominent Detachment (6D11.1) traits (social withdrawal, emotional coldness, reduced capacity for close relationships and for experiencing pleasure).

Differential diagnosis

From autism spectrum / Asperger's (F84.5) — onset in early childhood with communication and stereotyped-interest features; from schizotypal disorder (F21) — which adds odd beliefs/perceptual distortions; from the negative symptoms of schizophrenia (F20); from avoidant PD (F60.6) — where the person desires closeness but avoids it through fear, unlike the schizoid person who lacks the desire.

Remember — schizoid vs avoidant

Schizoid: alone and content (no desire for closeness). Avoidant: alone and lonely (wants closeness but fears rejection).

F60.2 · Dissocial (antisocial) personality disorder

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the callous, norm-violating pattern. ICD-11 maps this onto the Dissociality (and often Disinhibition) trait domains.

Usually comes to attention because of a gross disparity between behaviour and prevailing social norms.

ICD-10 (F60.2) — characterised by:

  • (a) callous unconcern for the feelings of others;
  • (b) gross and persistent irresponsibility and disregard for social norms, rules and obligations;
  • (c) incapacity to maintain enduring relationships, though with no difficulty in establishing them;
  • (d) very low tolerance to frustration and a low threshold for discharge of aggression, including violence;
  • (e) incapacity to experience guilt or to profit from experience, particularly punishment;
  • (f) marked proneness to blame others, or to offer plausible rationalisations, for the behaviour bringing the person into conflict with society.

Persistent irritability may be associated. Conduct disorder in childhood/adolescence, though not always present, supports the diagnosis.

Includes: amoral, antisocial, asocial, psychopathic and sociopathic personality (disorder). Excludes: conduct disorders (F91.-); emotionally unstable PD (F60.3).

ICD-11

Diagnosed as Personality disorder (6D10) with prominent Dissociality (6D11.2) (self-centredness, lack of empathy, callousness, exploitativeness) commonly combined with Disinhibition (6D11.3) (impulsivity, irresponsibility, risk-taking).

Differential diagnosis

From conduct disorder (F91) — the childhood/adolescent precursor; from emotionally unstable PD (F60.3) — where aggression is driven by affective instability rather than callous exploitation; from offending behaviour occurring only in mania or substance intoxication/use; from criminality without personality disorder (motivated, situational).

Remember — “CORRUPT”

Callous · Obligations ignored · Remorseless (no guilt) · Relationships not sustained · Unlawful/norm-breaking · Planning poor (impulsive, low frustration tolerance) · Tends to blame others.

F60.3 · Emotionally unstable personality disorder (EUPD)

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the impulsive/affectively unstable pattern; the only F60 subtype with its own coded sub-subtypes. ICD-11 retains its essence as the Borderline pattern qualifier (6D11.5).

A personality disorder with a marked tendency to act impulsively without considering consequences, together with affective instability. The ability to plan ahead may be minimal, and outbursts of intense anger may lead to violence or “behavioural explosions”, easily precipitated when impulsive acts are criticised or thwarted. Two variants share this theme of impulsiveness and lack of self-control.

F60.30 Impulsive type

ICD-10 (F60.30)

The predominant characteristics are emotional instability and lack of impulse control. Outbursts of violence or threatening behaviour are common, particularly in response to criticism by others.

Includes: explosive and aggressive personality (disorder). Excludes: dissocial PD (F60.2).
Differential diagnosis

From dissocial PD (F60.2) — aggression here is reactive/affective, not coldly exploitative; from intermittent explosive disorder (F63.8) — episodic aggression without the broader personality pattern; from mania and substance-related aggression.

F60.31 Borderline type

ICD-10 (F60.31)

Several characteristics of emotional instability are present; in addition:

  • the person's own self-image, aims and internal preferences (including sexual) are often unclear or disturbed;
  • usually chronic feelings of emptiness;
  • a liability to become involved in intense and unstable relationships, which may cause repeated emotional crises;
  • excessive efforts to avoid abandonment and a series of suicidal threats or acts of self-harm (which may occur without obvious precipitants).
Includes: borderline personality (disorder).

ICD-11

Captured by the Borderline pattern qualifier (6D11.5) added to a Personality disorder diagnosis: instability of self-image, affect and relationships; frantic efforts to avoid abandonment; impulsivity; recurrent self-harm/suicidal behaviour; chronic emptiness; and transient dissociative or paranoid states under stress.

Unique / high-risk features

Recurrent self-harm and suicidal behaviour, frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment, splitting (idealisation/devaluation of others), chronic emptiness, and stress-related transient paranoid or dissociative experiences. The highest-risk personality pattern for completed suicide.

Differential diagnosis

From bipolar disorder — mood shifts in EUPD are rapid, reactive and interpersonal, not sustained days-long episodes; from histrionic PD (F60.4); from PTSD / complex PTSD (6B41) after chronic trauma; from depression with self-harm.

Remember borderline — “AM SUICIDE”

Abandonment fears · Mood instability · Self-image disturbance · Unstable intense relationships · Impulsivity · Chronic emptiness · Identity/dissociative-paranoid under stress · Deliberate self-harm · Explosive anger.

Treatment

  • Psychological (first-line): DBT, MBT, schema therapy, or structured clinical management; crisis and safety planning.
  • Pharmacological: adjunctive only, targeting specific symptoms (mood instability, transient psychotic-like symptoms); avoid long-term benzodiazepines.

F60.4 · Histrionic personality disorder

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the attention-seeking, dramatic pattern. ICD-11 maps this partly onto Negative affectivity and Dissociality (attention-seeking) traits.

ICD-10 (F60.4) — characterised by:

  • (a) self-dramatisation, theatricality, exaggerated expression of emotions;
  • (b) suggestibility (easily influenced by others or by circumstances);
  • (c) shallow and labile affect;
  • (d) continual seeking for excitement and activities in which the person is the centre of attention;
  • (e) inappropriate seductiveness in appearance or behaviour;
  • (f) over-concern with physical attractiveness.

Associated: egocentricity, self-indulgence, continuous longing for appreciation, easily hurt feelings, and persistent manipulative behaviour.

Includes: hysterical and psychoinfantile personality (disorder).

ICD-11

Diagnosed as Personality disorder (6D10); attention-seeking, dramatic and manipulative features are represented within the trait qualifiers rather than as a named subtype.

Differential diagnosis

From borderline pattern (F60.31) — which adds self-harm, emptiness and abandonment terror; from narcissistic presentations (coded F60.8 in ICD-10); from normal expressiveness within cultural norms; from mania (episodic, with other manic features).

Remember — “PRAISE”

Provocative/seductive · Relationships seen as more intimate than they are / suggestible · Attention (must be centre of it) · Influenced easily · Shallow, shifting emotions · Exaggerated, theatrical expression.

F60.5 · Anankastic (obsessive-compulsive) personality disorder

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the perfectionistic, controlling pattern. ICD-11 maps this directly onto the Anankastia trait domain.

ICD-10 (F60.5) — characterised by:

  • (a) feelings of excessive doubt and caution;
  • (b) preoccupation with details, rules, lists, order, organisation or schedule;
  • (c) perfectionism that interferes with task completion;
  • (d) excessive conscientiousness and scrupulousness, and undue preoccupation with productivity to the exclusion of pleasure and relationships;
  • (e) excessive pedantry and adherence to social conventions;
  • (f) rigidity and stubbornness;
  • (g) unreasonable insistence that others submit to exactly his or her way of doing things, or unreasonable reluctance to let others do things;
  • (h) intrusion of insistent and unwelcome thoughts or impulses.
Includes: compulsive and obsessional personality; obsessive-compulsive personality disorder. Excludes: obsessive-compulsive disorder (F42.-).

ICD-11

Diagnosed as Personality disorder (6D10) with prominent Anankastia (6D11.4) traits (perfectionism, rigid control of behaviour, need for order, over-conscientiousness).

Differential diagnosis

Crucially from obsessive-compulsive disorder (F42) — OCD has ego-dystonic obsessions and compulsions (unwanted, resisted rituals), whereas anankastic PD is an ego-syntonic perfectionistic character style the person sees as correct. Also from normal conscientiousness and from autism-related rigidity.

Remember — “LAW FIRMS” (and the OCD contrast)

Lists/order · Adherence to rules · Won't delegate · Frugal/rigid · Inflexible morally · Reluctant to discard · Miserly control · Stubborn perfectionism. Key: anankastic PD is ego-syntonic; OCD is ego-dystonic.

F60.6 · Anxious (avoidant) personality disorder

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the socially inhibited, rejection-sensitive pattern. ICD-11 maps this onto Negative affectivity and Detachment traits.

ICD-10 (F60.6) — characterised by:

  • (a) persistent and pervasive feelings of tension and apprehension;
  • (b) belief that one is socially inept, personally unappealing, or inferior to others;
  • (c) excessive preoccupation with being criticised or rejected in social situations;
  • (d) unwillingness to become involved with people unless certain of being liked;
  • (e) restrictions in lifestyle because of a need to have physical security;
  • (f) avoidance of social or occupational activities involving significant interpersonal contact, for fear of criticism, disapproval or rejection.

Associated: hypersensitivity to rejection and criticism.

ICD-11

Diagnosed as Personality disorder (6D10) with prominent Negative affectivity (6D11.0) (anxiousness, low self-worth) and Detachment (6D11.1) (social withdrawal) traits.

Differential diagnosis

From social anxiety disorder (F40.1) — overlapping but situational/episodic rather than a lifelong pervasive character; from schizoid PD (F60.1) — schizoid people lack the desire for closeness, avoidant people want it but fear rejection; from dependent PD (F60.7).

Remember

“Wants closeness, fears rejection, feels inferior, avoids contact” — the lonely opposite of the content-to-be-alone schizoid.

F60.7 · Dependent personality disorder

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the clinging, submissive, reassurance-seeking pattern. ICD-11 maps this onto the Negative affectivity (dependency) trait.

ICD-10 (F60.7) — characterised by:

  • (a) encouraging or allowing others to make most of one's important life decisions;
  • (b) subordination of one's own needs to those of others on whom one is dependent, and undue compliance with their wishes;
  • (c) unwillingness to make even reasonable demands on the people one depends on;
  • (d) feeling uncomfortable or helpless when alone, because of exaggerated fears of inability to care for oneself;
  • (e) preoccupation with fears of being abandoned by a person with whom one has a close relationship, and of being left to care for oneself;
  • (f) limited capacity to make everyday decisions without an excessive amount of advice and reassurance from others.

Associated: perceiving oneself as helpless, incompetent and lacking stamina.

Includes: asthenic, inadequate, passive and self-defeating personality (disorder).

ICD-11

Diagnosed as Personality disorder (6D10) with prominent Negative affectivity (6D11.0) traits, especially dependency, submissiveness and separation insecurity.

Differential diagnosis

From avoidant PD (F60.6) — both fear being alone, but the dependent person seeks others to take over, while the avoidant person withdraws through fear of criticism; from borderline pattern (abandonment fear there is more turbulent, with self-harm); from dependency that is culturally normative or arises during a depressive episode.

Remember — “RELIANCE”

Reassurance needed for decisions · Excessive lengths to obtain support · Lacks initiative · Inability to be alone (feels helpless) · Abandonment preoccupation · Needs others to take responsibility · Compliant/submissive · Easily hurt by disapproval.

F60.8 / F60.9 · Other and unspecified specific personality disorders

Group: F60 Specific personality disorders → the “other” and residual categories.

ICD-10

  • F60.8 Other specific personality disorders — a personality disorder fitting none of F60.0–F60.7. Includes: eccentric, “haltlose” type, immature, narcissistic, passive-aggressive and psychoneurotic personality (disorder). (Note: ICD-10 has no separate narcissistic PD code — it sits here.)
  • F60.9 Personality disorder, unspecified. Includes: character neurosis NOS; pathological personality NOS.

ICD-11

All of these are handled by 6D10 Personality disorder at the appropriate severity with trait qualifiers — e.g. narcissistic features fall largely under Dissociality (6D11.2) (grandiosity, attention-seeking).

Differential diagnosis

Use F60.8/F60.9 only after the eight specific patterns have been excluded; distinguish from enduring personality change (F62), organic personality disorder (F07.0), and accentuation of normal traits (Z73.1).

F61 · Mixed and other personality disorders

Group: F61 sits alongside F60 for personality problems that do not fit a single specific pattern. ICD-11's dimensional model makes a separate “mixed” code unnecessary.

ICD-10 (F61)

  • F61.0 Mixed personality disorders — features of several of the F60 disorders but without a predominant set of symptoms that would allow a more specific diagnosis.
  • F61.1 Troublesome personality changes — not classifiable in F60 or F62 and regarded as secondary to a main diagnosis of a coexisting affective or anxiety disorder. Excludes: accentuation of personality traits (Z73.1).

This category is often more difficult to diagnose than the specific F60 patterns.

ICD-11

No equivalent needed: because ICD-11 describes personality disorder by severity + as many trait domains as apply, a person with mixed features simply receives 6D10 with several trait qualifiers. This is one of the clearest practical advantages of the dimensional model.

Differential diagnosis

From a specific F60 disorder (where one pattern predominates), from accentuation of normal traits (Z73.1), and from personality change that is acquired (F62) or organic (F07.0).

Remember

“Several patterns, no clear winner → F61” — the very problem ICD-11's trait model was designed to dissolve.

F62 · Enduring personality changes, not attributable to brain damage and disease

Group: F62 covers acquired personality change — developing in an adult with no previous personality disorder, after catastrophe or after severe psychiatric illness. This distinguishes it from developmental personality disorder (F60) and from organic personality change (F07.0).

ICD-10 (F62) general requirements

Disorders of adult personality and behaviour developing following catastrophic or excessive prolonged stress, or following a severe psychiatric illness, in people with no previous personality disorder. Diagnose only when there is evidence of a definite and enduring change in the person's pattern of perceiving, relating to, or thinking about the environment and self. The change must be significant, associated with inflexible and maladaptive behaviour not present before, must not be a manifestation of another mental disorder or a residual symptom of an antecedent disorder, and must not be due to brain damage/disease (use F07.0 instead).

Excludes: personality and behavioural disorder due to brain disease, damage and dysfunction (F07.-).

ICD-11

ICD-11 has no direct equivalent; the concept is largely absorbed elsewhere. Enduring change after prolonged, inescapable trauma is now captured chiefly by Complex PTSD (6B41) — which explicitly includes lasting disturbances in self-organisation, affect regulation and relationships — or by a personality-disorder diagnosis where the criteria are met.

F62.0 Enduring personality change after catastrophic experience

ICD-10 (F62.0)

Follows the experience of catastrophic stress so extreme that vulnerability of the person need not be considered (e.g. concentration-camp experiences, torture, disaster, prolonged captivity with imminent threat of death). Features include a hostile or mistrustful attitude to the world, social withdrawal, feelings of emptiness or hopelessness, a chronic feeling of being “on edge” as if constantly threatened, and estrangement. PTSD (F43.1) may precede it; this change may be a chronic, irreversible sequel. Present for at least 2 years.

Differential diagnosis

From PTSD (F43.1) — which is a syndrome of re-experiencing/avoidance/hyperarousal rather than an enduring personality shift (though it may precede F62.0); from a pre-existing personality disorder unmasked by stress; from depressive disorder. In ICD-11, most such presentations are coded as complex PTSD (6B41).

F62.1 Enduring personality change after psychiatric illness

ICD-10 (F62.1)

Personality change attributable to the traumatic experience of suffering from a severe psychiatric illness. The change cannot be explained by a previous personality disorder and should be distinguished from residual schizophrenia. Features include excessive dependence on others, demandingness, conviction of being changed or stigmatised by the illness, reduced ability to form and maintain close relationships, social isolation, passivity, reduced interests, and persistent complaints of being ill. Present for at least 2 years.

Excludes: residual schizophrenia (F20.5).
Differential diagnosis

From residual schizophrenia (F20.5) and the negative-symptom aftermath of psychosis; from a relapse of the original disorder; from a pre-existing personality disorder; from organic personality change (F07.0).

Remember F62 — “changed after”

Personality that changed after a catastrophe (F62.0) or after a severe illness (F62.1), lasting ≥2 years, in someone previously without a PD, and not from brain disease (that is F07.0). ICD-11 largely folds F62.0 into complex PTSD (6B41).

F63 · Habit and impulse disorders

Group: F63 covers repeated acts with no clear rational motive, driven by an impulse the person cannot control, harming the person or others. In ICD-11 this group is split up: some become Impulse control disorders (6C70–6C73), gambling becomes an addictive-behaviour disorder (6C50), and trichotillomania becomes an OCD-related body-focused behaviour (6B25.0).

The shared pattern: repeated actions with no clear rational motive, generally harming the person's own and others' interests, with reported mounting tension before the act and a sense of relief or gratification during/after it.

Psychological understanding

Other evidence These behaviours are conceptualised on a spectrum between impulsivity (acting to gain arousal/gratification) and compulsivity (acting to relieve tension/anxiety). ICD-11 reorganised the group accordingly — gambling toward the addiction model (reward-driven), trichotillomania toward the OCD-related model (tension-relief).

F63.0 Pathological gambling

ICD-10 (F63.0)

Frequent, repeated episodes of gambling that dominate the person's life to the detriment of social, occupational, material and family values and commitments. There is an intense urge to gamble, difficult to control, with preoccupation with ideas and images of gambling; these urges often increase at times of stress. The essential feature is persistently repeated gambling that continues and often increases despite adverse social consequences (impoverishment, impaired family relationships, disruption of personal life). ICD-10 notes the term “compulsive gambling” is less appropriate as the behaviour is not compulsive in the technical sense.

Includes: compulsive gambling.

ICD-11 (6C50)

Gambling disorder, now grouped under Disorders due to addictive behaviours (with online/offline qualifiers) — recognising it as a behavioural addiction with impaired control, increasing priority over other interests, and continuation despite harm. The new sibling diagnosis 6C51 Gaming disorder was added here.

Differential diagnosis (from ICD-10)

From social/habitual gambling and betting (Z72.6) — done for excitement or money, curbed when losses mount; from excessive gambling by manic patients (F30); from gambling by dissocial personalities (F60.2) — part of a wider persistent disturbance of social behaviour.

Remember

“Can't stop despite ruin” — the marker is continuation despite mounting harm. ICD-11 reclassifies it as a behavioural addiction (6C50), next to gaming disorder (6C51).

F63.1 Pathological fire-setting (pyromania)

ICD-10 (F63.1) — essential features:

  • (a) repeated fire-setting without any obvious motive (such as monetary gain, revenge, or political extremism);
  • (b) an intense interest in watching fires burn;
  • (c) reported feelings of increasing tension before the act, and intense excitement immediately after it.

Often with abnormal interest in fire-engines, fire-fighting equipment, and calling out the fire service.

ICD-11 (6C70)

Pyromania, within the new Impulse control disorders grouping: recurrent failure to control strong impulses to set fires, with tension beforehand and gratification/relief afterwards, not better explained by another motive or disorder.

Differential diagnosis (from ICD-10)

From deliberate fire-setting with an obvious motive (Z03.2); from fire-setting by a young person with conduct disorder (F91.1) (other disordered behaviour present); by an adult with dissocial PD (F60.2); in schizophrenia (F20) (in response to delusions/command hallucinations); and in organic states (F00–F09) or intoxication (confusion, accidental).

Remember

“Fire for its own sake” — fascination + tension-then-thrill, no external motive (money, revenge, politics rule it out).

F63.2 Pathological stealing (kleptomania)

ICD-10 (F63.2)

Repeated failure to resist impulses to steal objects that are not acquired for personal use or monetary gain — the objects may be discarded, given away or hoarded. There is an increasing sense of tension before, and a sense of gratification during and immediately after, the act. Some effort at concealment is usually made.

ICD-11 (6C71)

Kleptomania, within Impulse control disorders: recurrent failure to control impulses to steal objects not needed for use or value, with the characteristic pre-act tension and post-act relief/gratification.

Differential diagnosis

From ordinary shoplifting/theft (goods wanted for use or gain, planned); from stealing in dissocial PD (F60.2) or conduct disorder (F91); from theft during a manic episode; from stealing driven by organic disorder or poverty.

Remember

“Steals things it doesn't even want” — no gain motive, driven by the tension-relief cycle.

F63.3 Trichotillomania

ICD-10 (F63.3)

Noticeable hair loss due to a recurrent failure to resist impulses to pull out hairs. The hair-pulling is usually preceded by mounting tension and followed by a sense of relief or gratification. This diagnosis should not be made if there is a pre-existing skin inflammation, or if the hair-pulling is in response to a delusion or a hallucination.

Excludes: stereotyped movement disorder with hair-plucking (F98.4).

ICD-11 (6B25.0)

Trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder) is reclassified as a body-focused repetitive behaviour within Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders — not with the impulse disorders. Its new sibling is 6B25.1 Excoriation (skin-picking) disorder.

Differential diagnosis

From stereotyped movement disorder with hair-plucking (F98.4); from hair-pulling secondary to a delusion or hallucination (do not code here); from dermatological causes of hair loss; from OCD (where pulling would be a ritual to neutralise an obsession).

Remember

“Pull → relief” — and note ICD-11 moved it to the OCD-related body-focused group with skin-picking.

Comorbidities across F63

Depression and anxiety are common; gambling disorder strongly co-occurs with substance use and mood disorders; trichotillomania and skin-picking co-occur with OCD-spectrum and anxiety disorders.

Treatment across F63

  • Gambling: CBT, motivational work, self-exclusion/financial measures, gambling-specific support; treat comorbid mood/substance disorders.
  • Pyromania/kleptomania: CBT (impulse-control strategies, covert sensitisation); an SSRI may help, especially with comorbidity.
  • Trichotillomania: habit-reversal training (the best-evidenced behavioural treatment); an SSRI or N-acetylcysteine may be tried.

F64 · Gender identity disorders

Group: F64 covers persistent distress/incongruence between experienced gender and assigned sex. A major ICD-11 change: this was removed from the mental-disorders chapter and recast as Gender incongruence under conditions related to sexual health — a deliberate depsychiatrisation.

Framing note. ICD-11 renamed and relocated these categories specifically to reduce stigma: being transgender is no longer classified as a mental disorder. The retained category (gender incongruence) exists chiefly to enable access to health care, not to label a mental illness.

F64.0 Transsexualism

ICD-10 (F64.0)

A desire to live and be accepted as a member of the opposite sex, usually accompanied by a sense of discomfort with, or inappropriateness of, one's anatomic sex, and a wish to have hormonal treatment and surgery to make one's body as congruent as possible with the preferred sex. For diagnosis, the transsexual identity should have been present persistently for at least 2 years, and must not be a symptom of another mental disorder (such as schizophrenia) or associated with any intersex, genetic or sex-chromosome abnormality.

ICD-11

Recast as Gender incongruence of adolescence and adulthood in the sexual-health chapter: a marked and persistent incongruence between experienced gender and assigned sex. Notably, distress is not required and it is no longer a mental disorder.

Differential diagnosis

From dual-role transvestism (F64.1) (no desire for permanent change); from fetishistic transvestism (F65.1) (cross-dressing for sexual arousal); from transient identity questioning; from an identity disturbance occurring only within schizophrenia or an intersex/chromosomal condition (excluded).

F64.1 Dual-role transvestism

ICD-10 (F64.1)

The wearing of clothes of the opposite sex for part of the individual's existence, to enjoy the temporary experience of membership of the opposite sex, but without any desire for a more permanent sex change or surgical reassignment, and without sexual excitement accompanying the cross-dressing (which distinguishes it from fetishistic transvestism, F65.1).

Includes: gender identity disorder of adolescence/adulthood, non-transsexual type. Excludes: fetishistic transvestism (F65.1).
Differential diagnosis

From fetishistic transvestism (F65.1) — there cross-dressing produces sexual arousal; from transsexualism (F64.0) — there a permanent change is desired.

F64.2 Gender identity disorder of childhood

ICD-10 (F64.2)

Usually first manifest in early childhood (always well before puberty), characterised by persistent and intense distress about assigned sex, together with a desire to be (or insistence that one is) of the other sex, and preoccupation with the dress and/or activities of the other sex and/or repudiation of one's own sex. It requires a profound disturbance of the normal sense of maleness/femaleness — mere “tomboyishness” in girls or “girlish” behaviour in boys is not sufficient. The diagnosis cannot be made once the individual has reached puberty.

ICD-11

Recast as Gender incongruence of childhood in the sexual-health chapter.

Differential diagnosis

From normal gender non-conformity (much commoner; not a disorder); from an identity disturbance within another disorder. Note ICD-11 removes it from the mental-disorders classification.

Remember F64

“Persistent incongruence, ≥2 years for transsexualism, arousal-free cross-dressing = dual-role” — and the big change: ICD-11 renames the block gender incongruence and moves it out of mental disorders.

F65 · Disorders of sexual preference (the paraphilias)

Group: F65 covers persistent, intense atypical patterns of sexual arousal. ICD-11 reframes these as Paraphilic disorders, diagnosed only when the arousal pattern involves non-consenting others or substantial risk of harm/distress — private, consensual atypical interests are no longer disorders.

The key ICD-10 vs ICD-11 shift. ICD-10 diagnoses on the basis of the preference pattern itself being the main/necessary source of arousal. ICD-11 requires action on the urges with a non-consenting person, or marked distress/impairment or risk. So consensual, non-harmful sadomasochism or fetishism is not a disorder in ICD-11.

F65.0 Fetishism  |  F65.1 Fetishistic transvestism

ICD-10

  • F65.0 Fetishism: reliance on a non-living object as a stimulus for sexual arousal and gratification (e.g. clothing, rubber, leather). Diagnosed only if the fetish is the most important source of stimulation or is essential for satisfactory sexual response; fantasies alone are not a disorder. Almost exclusively in males.
  • F65.1 Fetishistic transvestism: wearing clothes of the opposite sex principally to obtain sexual excitement, creating the appearance of the opposite sex. Distinguished from dual-role transvestism (F64.1) by the clear association with sexual arousal and the desire to remove the clothing once orgasm occurs. Includes: transvestic fetishism.
Differential diagnosis

Fetishism from ordinary use of objects to enhance normal sex (only a disorder if the fetish is essential/dominant). Fetishistic transvestism from dual-role transvestism (F64.1) (no arousal) and from transsexualism (F64.0) (desire for permanent change).

F65.2 Exhibitionism  |  F65.3 Voyeurism

ICD-10

  • F65.2 Exhibitionism: a recurrent or persistent tendency to expose the genitalia to strangers (usually of the opposite sex) or to people in public places, without inviting or intending closer contact, usually with sexual excitement and often followed by masturbation. Almost entirely limited to heterosexual males exposing to females. Urges are typically difficult to control and ego-alien.
  • F65.3 Voyeurism: a recurrent or persistent tendency to look at people engaging in sexual or intimate behaviour (e.g. undressing) without their knowledge, leading to sexual excitement and masturbation.

ICD-11

Exhibitionistic disorder and Voyeuristic disorder are retained as paraphilic disorders because they inherently involve non-consenting people.

Differential diagnosis

From exposure/observation occurring within mania, intoxication, dementia or psychosis; from consensual activity; from public urination or nudity without sexual intent.

F65.4 Paedophilia

ICD-10 (F65.4)

A sexual preference for children, usually of prepubertal or early pubertal age. An isolated incident (especially by an adolescent) does not establish the persistent or predominant tendency required for the diagnosis. Rarely identified in women.

ICD-11

Pedophilic disorder is retained as a paraphilic disorder (involves non-consenting minors).

Differential diagnosis

From isolated offending without a persistent preference; from offending within an organic disorder or dissocial PD (F60.2); from contact with sexually mature adolescents (socially disapproved but not necessarily paedophilia).

F65.5 Sadomasochism  |  F65.6 Multiple  |  F65.8 Other (incl. frotteurism)

ICD-10

  • F65.5 Sadomasochism: a preference for sexual activity involving bondage or the infliction of pain or humiliation. Masochism = preferring to receive; sadism = preferring to provide. Coded only if sadomasochistic activity is the most important source of stimulation or is necessary for gratification. Includes: masochism, sadism.
  • F65.6 Multiple disorders of sexual preference: more than one paraphilia in one person with none predominating; the commonest combination is fetishism, transvestism and sadomasochism.
  • F65.8 Other disorders of sexual preference: e.g. making obscene telephone calls, frotteurism (rubbing against people in crowds for stimulation), sexual activity with animals, use of strangulation/anoxia to intensify arousal, and preference for a partner with a particular anatomical abnormality.

ICD-11

Retains Frotteuristic disorder and Coercive sexual sadism disorder (non-consenting others), plus “other paraphilic disorder involving non-consenting individuals” and “involving solitary behaviour or consenting individuals” only where there is marked distress or risk. Consensual sadomasochism is not a disorder.

Differential diagnosis

Consensual, non-harmful practices (not a disorder in ICD-11); mild sadomasochistic elements enhancing otherwise normal sex; behaviour better explained by another disorder. Distinguish sexual sadism from non-erotic cruelty/anger.

Remember F65 — the ICD-11 test

“Consent + no harm = not a disorder (ICD-11).” The paraphilic disorders retained are essentially those involving non-consenting people (exhibitionistic, voyeuristic, pedophilic, frotteuristic, coercive sadism) or marked distress/risk.

Treatment (F65)

  • Psychological: CBT (cognitive restructuring, relapse prevention, victim empathy), especially where there is risk to others.
  • Pharmacological: SSRIs to reduce urges; anti-androgen/hormonal treatments in high-risk cases with specialist oversight.

F66 · Psychological and behavioural disorders associated with sexual development and orientation

Group: F66 concerns difficulties around sexual development, orientation and relationships. ICD-11 deleted the orientation-related categories, because sexual orientation is not a disorder.

ICD-10 (F66)

ICD-10 stressed that sexual orientation alone is not a disorder. The subtypes were:

  • F66.0 Sexual maturation disorder: the person suffers from uncertainty about gender identity or sexual orientation, causing anxiety or depression.
  • F66.1 Egodystonic sexual orientation: the gender identity or sexual orientation is not in doubt, but the person wishes it were different because of associated psychological and behavioural disorders, and may seek treatment to change it.
  • F66.2 Sexual relationship disorder: the gender identity or sexual preference is responsible for difficulties in forming or maintaining a relationship with a sexual partner.

A fifth character indicated association with heterosexuality, homosexuality, bisexuality, or other/prepubertal.

ICD-11

Egodystonic sexual orientation (F66.1) and sexual maturation disorder are removed. ICD-11 rejects any classification that frames a sexual orientation as pathological. Any genuine associated distress is coded through the relevant mood/anxiety diagnosis instead.

Differential diagnosis

From a primary depressive or anxiety disorder (which should be coded directly); from gender incongruence (F64/ICD-11 sexual-health chapter). Note that in ICD-11 these F66 orientation categories no longer exist.

Remember

“Orientation is never the disorder” — ICD-10 already said so, and ICD-11 removed the egodystonic-orientation category entirely.

F68 · Other disorders of adult personality and behaviour

Group: F68 gathers disorders of illness behaviour — producing or exaggerating symptoms. In ICD-11, factitious disorder gains its own codes (6D50/6D51).

ICD-10 (F68)

  • F68.0 Elaboration of physical symptoms for psychological reasons: physical symptoms compatible with, and originally due to, a confirmed physical disorder become exaggerated or prolonged owing to the psychological state of the patient (seeking attention, or dissatisfaction).
  • F68.1 Factitious disorder: intentional production or feigning of symptoms or disabilities, either physical or psychological. The person feigns symptoms without an obvious external motive (to adopt the sick role). This distinguishes it from malingering (Z76.5), where the motivation is obvious and external (avoiding danger, criminal sentencing, or money).

ICD-11

6D50 Factitious disorder imposed on self and 6D51 Factitious disorder imposed on another (the latter formerly “by proxy”) are now explicit diagnoses.

Differential diagnosis

From malingering (Z76.5) — intentional feigning for an obvious external gain (not a mental disorder); from somatoform / bodily distress disorder — symptoms are not intentionally produced; from a genuine physical disorder. In factitious disorder the deception is intentional but the motive is internal (the sick role).

Remember — the illness-behaviour trio

Factitious: symptoms intentional, motive internal (sick role). Malingering: symptoms intentional, motive external/obvious. Somatoform/bodily distress: symptoms not intentional.

F69 · Unspecified disorder of adult personality and behaviour

Group: F69 is the residual code closing the F60–F69 block.

ICD-10 (F69)

The residual category, used when a disorder of adult personality and behaviour is present but cannot be assigned to F60–F68.

ICD-11

No single equivalent; each ICD-11 destination grouping has its own “other specified” and “unspecified” residual codes.

As a purely residual, unspecified category, F69 has no meaningful stand-alone differential diagnosis; the task is to identify a more specific category wherever possible.
