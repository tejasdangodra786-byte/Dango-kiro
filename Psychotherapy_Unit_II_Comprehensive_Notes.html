How to read these notes

These are concise, bullet-style revision notes for Unit II of the Psychotherapy paper (M.Phil. Clinical Psychology). They cover the therapeutic relationship in full, from the working alliance and its models to the client, therapist, illness, technique and other factors that shape it, and fold in the relevant professional bodies (RCI, IACP, WHO and APA) where each is examinable.

How citations work

  • A citation in square brackets names the full book and its author, for example [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text] or [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy], so you can trace any claim back to the work it comes from.
  • Where a point rests on a professional body rather than a book, the citation names the document or body and its year, for example [American Psychological Association, Ethics Code 2017], keeping any section number.

How to read the formatting

  • Words in bold are the memory hooks: skim the bold words first to recall the gist of a sentence, then read the full sentence to fill in the detail.
  • A term followed by text in rounded brackets is a plain-language gloss, for example alliance (the bond and agreement on goals and tasks), so hard clinical terms stay but are explained simply.
  • Every topic carries one flowchart figure that pictures the idea and one mnemonic that packs its key points into a single memorable word.

1The therapeutic relationship and working alliance: definition and models

The therapeutic relationship, or alliance, is the collaborative emotional bond and working partnership between client and therapist; it is both the vehicle through which every technique is delivered and, for many authors, a curative factor in its own right.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology characterises the therapeutic alliance (the cooperative working relationship between client and therapist) as the cooperative working relationship, seen as essential for progress, that combines the client's affective bond with the therapist and their agreement on the goals and tasks of treatment. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Guideline The World Health Organization, in its mhGAP and psychological-interventions guidance, characterises effective care as being delivered within a respectful, trusting and collaborative provider-client relationship, which it treats as a foundation for engagement and adherence rather than an optional extra. [World Health Organization, mhGAP Intervention Guide]
  • Theoretical view Gabbard describes the therapeutic alliance as a fundamental concept that applies across all forms of psychotherapy, denoting the collaborative bond that lets client and therapist work together toward shared aims. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Theoretical view Greenson (1967), reported in Corsini and Wedding, framed the working alliance as the rational, non-neurotic partnership in which the client aligns with the therapist's method to pursue the work of therapy. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]

Why the relationship matters (exam form)

  • The alliance is described as the vehicle (the carrier through which specific methods take effect) of therapy, meaning that techniques work through the relationship, so even an excellent method fails when the bond with the client is weak. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Theoretical view Rogers (1957) argued that the relationship conditions of empathy, warmth and genuineness are necessary and sufficient for change, so that for him the relationship itself is the active ingredient rather than a mere background. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Theoretical view Many later clinicians, especially behaviour therapists, regard those relationship conditions as necessary but not sufficient, holding that a good bond is required yet must be paired with specific techniques to produce change. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The alliance is a pantheoretical common factor, which means it cuts across psychodynamic, cognitive-behavioural, humanistic and systemic schools instead of belonging to any single model. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • A strong early bond helps the anxious or reluctant client feel safe enough to disclose, so trust is the precondition that lets assessment, formulation and intervention actually proceed. [Christine Lister-Ford, A Short Introduction to Psychotherapy]

Models and components of the relationship

  • Theoretical view Bordin (1979) proposed the working alliance as a pantheoretical model with three interdependent parts, namely agreement on goals, agreement on tasks, and the emotional bond of trust and liking between the pair. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • In Bordin's model goals are the agreed outcomes the client wants, tasks are the in-session activities believed to reach them, and the bond is the affective tie that makes the shared work feel safe and worthwhile. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Theoretical view Rogers (1957) named three core conditions, which are accurate empathy (grasping the client's feelings as if they were one's own), unconditional positive regard (non-judgemental warmth and acceptance of the person) and congruence (being genuine, with outward manner matching inner experience). [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Rogers stressed that the core conditions must be perceived by the client, because warmth and understanding only help when the client actually experiences them, not merely when the therapist intends them. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Greenson (1967) and Gelso split the relationship into three strands, namely the working alliance, the transference-countertransference configuration, and the real relationship of genuine, realistic perceptions of each other. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Theoretical view Luborsky described a helping alliance with two types, the first being the client's experience of the therapist as supportive, and the second a sense of shared work toward common goals, while Zetzel used the alliance as the basis for analytic work. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Theoretical view From an attachment perspective after Bowlby, the therapist functions as a secure base (a dependable figure from whom the client can safely explore distress), so the client's own attachment style strongly shapes how the bond forms. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Transference (the client's displacement onto the therapist of feelings from past relationships) can be positive or negative and, when understood, becomes rich material rather than an obstacle to the work. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Countertransference (the therapist's own emotional reactions to the client) is managed through self-awareness and supervision and can be used as information about what the client evokes in others. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Resistance (the client's conscious or unconscious opposition to the therapy process) shows as silence, lateness, avoidance or intellectualisation, and is explored rather than fought, because it signals where the work is threatening. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • The relationship moves through stages, namely engagement and formation, a deepening working phase, and termination, where ending is itself therapeutic because it can revive earlier experiences of loss and separation. [Christine Lister-Ford, A Short Introduction to Psychotherapy]
Bordin vs Rogers at a glance
  • Bordin (working alliance): bond + agreement on goals + agreement on tasks; pantheoretical, suits every model.
  • Rogers (core conditions): empathy + unconditional positive regard + congruence; must be perceived by the client.
  • Greenson/Gelso: working alliance + transference-countertransference + real relationship.
  • Shared message: a collaborative, trusting bond is the common ground beneath all schools.
Mnemonic
BeGoT
Bond, Goals, Tasks — Bordin's three parts of the working alliance; pair it with Rogers' ECG (Empathy, Congruence, unconditional positive reGard) to recall both leading models.

2Client characteristics that shape the relationship

What the client brings, including their readiness to change, expectations, attachment style, coping style, insight and culture, strongly influences how quickly and how firmly a working alliance can form.

  • Theoretical view Prochaska and DiClemente's stages of change run through precontemplation (not yet seeing a problem), contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance and relapse, and interventions work best when matched to the client's stage. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Confronting a precontemplative client who does not yet want change typically provokes resistance, so a gentler, exploratory stance such as motivational interviewing builds the bond far better early on. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • A client's expectations and hope are a major common factor, because someone who believes therapy can help engages more fully, while role expectations shape what they think the work will involve. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Attachment style predicts how the bond forms, since secure clients build an alliance quickly whereas insecure styles make trust slower and more effortful to establish. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Avoidant clients tend to keep emotional distance, anxious-ambivalent clients may become over-dependent, and disorganised clients often show frequent ruptures that the therapist must patiently repair. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Theoretical view Beutler distinguishes coping styles, so that externalising clients who act out suit symptom-focused methods while internalising clients who turn inward suit insight-oriented work. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Theoretical view Reactance, meaning a client's sensitivity to being controlled (a tendency to resist perceived pressure on their freedom), matters because high-reactance clients do better with non-directive approaches that leave them in charge. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Psychological mindedness, insight, verbal ability and intelligence ease insight-oriented therapy (therapy that works by understanding hidden patterns and meanings), because such work depends on reflecting on one's own mind. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The old YAVIS stereotype (young, attractive, verbal, intelligent, successful) versus the HOUND stereotype (homely, old, unintelligent, non-verbal, disadvantaged) exposes a long-standing bias toward favouring easier, more articulate clients. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Culture and demographics such as age, gender, language, religion and beliefs about illness shape the bond, and a culturally mismatched therapy that ignores these often ends in early dropout. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Clients who explain distress through spiritual or somatic frames, or who face heavy stigma and strong family expectations, need the therapist to work respectfully within that worldview rather than against it. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Severity, distress and functioning matter, because severe impairment, marked interpersonal difficulty and chronic problems all make a stable alliance harder and slower to achieve. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • A client's previous therapy experience colours the new relationship, so that past help that failed lowers hope while a past good experience primes trust in the current therapist. [Christine Lister-Ford, A Short Introduction to Psychotherapy]
  • The referral source matters, because a voluntary client who seeks help engages more readily than a mandated or family-pushed client who may feel coerced into attending. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A client's social support and life circumstances, including housing, finances and relationships, shape the energy and stability they can bring to the therapeutic work. [Christine Lister-Ford, A Short Introduction to Psychotherapy]
Mnemonic
CARE-PS
Change-readiness, Attachment style, Reactance and coping, Expectations and hope, Psychological mindedness, Social and cultural context — the client characteristics that shape the alliance.

3Therapist characteristics that shape the relationship

Therapists vary in how well they form alliances; their personal qualities, professional competence, stance and self-awareness matter more than demographic matching, and some therapists are reliably more effective than others.

  • The core personal qualities are empathy, warmth and genuineness, together with respect, trustworthiness, patience and a hope-inspiring attitude that together let the client feel understood and safe. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Emotional stability, flexibility, humour, self-awareness and tolerance of ambiguity help the therapist stay steady and responsive when the client is distressed, hostile or ambivalent. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Professional competence rests on training, experience, supervision and the capacity to formulate a case and adapt the treatment, so skill and character work together rather than separately. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Cultural competence (the knowledge, awareness and skills to work respectfully across cultures) lets the therapist respond to the client's values and context, which strongly supports engagement in diverse settings. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Therapist effects are real, meaning some therapists consistently achieve better outcomes than others, and these differences between therapists are often larger than the differences between treatment methods. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Therapeutic stance and style, such as how directive or active the therapist is, must be tuned to the client, because a style that suits one client can alienate another. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Self-disclosure (the therapist sharing something personal) can strengthen the bond when it is brief and serves the client, but harms the work when it shifts the focus onto the therapist's own needs. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Countertransference from unresolved conflicts, a need to rescue, over-identification, anger, boredom or attraction can distort the relationship unless the therapist notices and works on it. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Supervision and personal work are the main safeguards, letting the therapist process their reactions so that personal blind spots do not silently damage the alliance. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • A therapist's expectations and belief in the treatment, together with genuine positive regard for the client, raise the client's own hope and strengthen engagement. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Demographic matching of gender, ethnicity or language can comfort some clients, but it matters less than the therapist's warmth, respect and cultural responsiveness. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Professional code Competence and ethical conduct are professional obligations, and in India the RCI Act 1992 sets standards of conduct and etiquette for registered rehabilitation professionals including clinical psychologists. [Rehabilitation Council of India Act, 1992]
  • Burnout erodes empathy and patience, so a depleted or overloaded therapist forms weaker alliances, which makes self-care an ethical as well as a personal concern. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Harmful therapist qualities include coldness, rigidity, authoritarianism, criticism, over-involvement and disrespect for boundaries, all of which damage trust and can make the client worse. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The decisive variable is the therapist's attitude toward the client, since a respectful, responsive and self-aware therapist builds strong alliances across a wide range of clients and problems. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
Mnemonic
WE-CARES
Warmth and empathy, Experience and competence, Cultural responsiveness, Attitude and positive regard, Reflective self-awareness, Ethical conduct, Stance tuned to the client — the therapist qualities that build the alliance.

4How the nature and severity of illness influence the relationship

The type, severity and chronicity of the disorder, along with insight, risk, trauma history and cognitive state, change how quickly trust can form and how active the therapist must be to hold the relationship.

  • Alliances form most easily with mild to moderate anxiety and adjustment problems, because such clients usually retain insight, motivation and the capacity to collaborate. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Personality disorders, especially borderline, paranoid and antisocial presentations, strain the bond through mistrust, testing and rapid shifts, so the alliance needs careful, consistent tending. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Psychosis makes engagement hard because suspicion and poor insight can lead the client to doubt the therapist's intentions, so trust is built slowly and concretely. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Severe depression brings withdrawal, hopelessness and low energy, which slow responsiveness and mean the therapist must carry more of the early activity and warmth. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Substance use often involves denial and ambivalence, while eating disorders carry ambivalence about recovery, so both call for a non-confrontational, motivation-building stance early on. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Severity and chronicity lengthen the time to build a bond, and repeated failed treatments lower hope, so the therapist must actively rebuild expectation of improvement. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Poor insight and motivation, as in acute psychosis or mania, reduce collaboration, so the work may begin with engagement and psychoeducation rather than deeper therapeutic tasks. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Comorbidity, such as depression combined with anxiety or substance use, complicates the picture and can divide the client's attention and the alliance across competing problems. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Suicidality and risk demand a more active, structured stance and may strain boundaries, because safety planning and monitoring temporarily reshape the usual therapeutic frame. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Professional code Managing risk also engages legal and ethical duties, since the APA Ethics Code 2017 permits disclosure without consent to protect the client or others from serious harm. [American Psychological Association, Ethics Code 2017, Std 4.05]
  • A trauma and abuse history makes trust difficult, so the therapist prioritises safety and gradual pacing, letting the client set the speed rather than pressing for early disclosure. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Cognitive impairment from organic disorders or intellectual disability calls for simplified communication and often family involvement, which changes the shape of the relationship. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Medication and physical illness, through sedation, side effects, pain or fatigue, affect engagement and alertness, so the therapist adapts session length and expectations accordingly. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Across presentations the principle holds that the sicker or more mistrustful the client, the more the therapist must adjust pace, structure and activity to protect a fragile bond. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
Mnemonic
SICK-TM
Severity and chronicity, Insight and motivation, Comorbidity, Kind of disorder, Trauma history, risk and Medication/physical illness — the illness factors that shape the alliance.

5How technique and therapeutic approach influence the relationship

The techniques chosen, how well they fit the client, how clearly they are explained, and how flexibly they are applied all feed back into the alliance, so method and relationship are tightly linked rather than separate.

  • Theoretical view Beutler's prescriptive matching tailors the method to the client, so directive techniques suit low-reactance clients while non-directive work suits high-reactance clients. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Matching also follows coping style, with symptom-focused techniques suiting externalisers and insight-focused work suiting internalisers, so a good fit itself supports the bond. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • A clear, credible rationale for the treatment raises the client's expectation and strengthens the alliance, because people engage more when they understand why a task should help. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Timing and accuracy of interventions matter, so a well-timed, accurate interpretation (a statement linking the client's pattern to its hidden meaning) deepens the bond. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • A premature or inaccurate interpretation tends to produce defensiveness, because the client feels misunderstood or exposed before enough trust has been built. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • An overly directive style can trigger reactance in clients who resist being controlled, while an overly passive style can leave clients feeling confused, unheld or frustrated. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Confrontation, exposure or challenging techniques used too early can strain the alliance, so the bond is built first and demanding work is introduced once trust can bear it. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Responsive flexibility (adjusting the plan to the client in the moment) improves the alliance, whereas rigid manual adherence in the face of a rupture tends to worsen it. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Homework and between-session tasks shape the task agreement, so negotiating tasks the client sees as relevant and doable keeps the collaborative part of the bond strong. [Judith S. Beck, Cognitive Behavior Therapy: Basics and Beyond]
  • The format of therapy, whether individual, group or family, changes the relationship, since group and family work add multiple bonds and shared cohesion to manage. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Teletherapy by video can produce an alliance comparable to in-person work, but it is vulnerable to technical problems and the loss of some non-verbal cues. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Guideline Because flexibility helps, the therapist treats the manual as a guide not a cage, adapting technique to the client's reactions while keeping the treatment coherent and evidence-based. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • The overarching rule is that technique and relationship interact, so the best outcomes come from applying an effective method responsively within a strong, well-tended alliance. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
Mnemonic
MR-FIT
Matching (Beutler), Rationale credibility, Flexibility vs rigid adherence, Interpretation timing and accuracy, Tasks, format and teletherapy — the technique factors that shape the alliance.

6Other factors: setting, culture, ruptures and repair, transference, boundaries, teletherapy

Beyond the people and the method, the frame and setting, culture and power distance, ruptures and their repair, transference, professional boundaries and technology all shape the relationship, and ethics bodies govern the boundary issues directly.

Setting, culture and context

  • The therapy frame of privacy, comfort, location, fees, session length, frequency and cancellation policy sets the stable conditions within which trust can grow. [Christine Lister-Ford, A Short Introduction to Psychotherapy]
  • Duration affects depth, because a longer treatment allows a deeper bond while abrupt endings strain the relationship and can reactivate earlier experiences of loss. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Culture and power distance shape the bond, and in many Indian settings clients may defer to the therapist as a doctor figure and avoid openly disagreeing, which the therapist must actively invite. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Stigma, language barriers, gender norms and religion all influence engagement, so the therapist works respectfully within the client's social and cultural world rather than against it. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Life events and external stress during therapy, along with third parties such as family, insurers or courts in mandated work, pull on the relationship from outside the room. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Therapist-client fit or chemistry is hard to measure yet important, so inviting early feedback and being willing to refer elsewhere when the fit is poor is itself good practice. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]

Ruptures and repair

  • Theoretical view Safran and Muran describe ruptures (tensions or breakdowns in the alliance) in two forms, namely withdrawal ruptures where the client goes silent, compliant or changes the topic, and confrontation ruptures where the client criticises, shows anger or makes demands. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Repair uses meta-communication (openly talking about what is happening between client and therapist), empathic validation and flexibility with tasks or goals, and is associated with better outcome. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • A repaired rupture can strengthen the alliance, so the rupture-repair cycle is treated as valuable therapeutic material rather than as a failure of the therapist. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • The therapist takes responsibility for their own contribution to a rupture, because owning the strain models honesty and makes it safe for the client to raise future tensions. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
Withdrawal vs confrontation ruptures (Safran & Muran)
  • Withdrawal rupture: client turns away - silence, compliance, changing the topic, vague agreement.
  • Confrontation rupture: client turns against - criticism, anger, complaints, demands on the therapist.
  • Repair for both: notice it, name it gently (meta-communication), validate, and adjust tasks or goals.
  • Outcome: a well-repaired rupture often leaves the alliance stronger than before.

Boundaries, dual relationships and the bodies

  • Transference and countertransference (the client's displaced feelings and the therapist's reactions to them) are managed openly, with supervision, so that strong feelings in the room inform rather than derail the work. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Professional code Professional boundaries protect the client, and the APA Ethics Code 2017, Standard 3.05 Multiple Relationships warns against second relationships that could impair objectivity or risk exploitation or harm. [American Psychological Association, Ethics Code 2017, Std 3.05]
  • Professional code The APA Ethics Code 2017, Standards 10.05 to 10.08 prohibit sexual intimacies with current therapy clients and tightly restrict them with former clients, making this one of the clearest boundary rules in the code. [American Psychological Association, Ethics Code 2017, Std 10.05]
  • Professional code In India the RCI Act 1992 empowers the Rehabilitation Council to prescribe standards of conduct, etiquette and ethics for registered professionals, so boundary and competence duties carry statutory backing. [Rehabilitation Council of India Act, 1992]
  • Guideline The World Health Organization likewise frames the provider-client relationship as resting on trust, respect and confidentiality, which boundary-keeping is designed to protect. [World Health Organization, mhGAP Intervention Guide]
  • Secondary source The IACP (Indian Association of Clinical Psychologists) is understood from secondary summaries to broadly align with APA and RCI duties on competence and boundaries, and no IACP clause numbers are quoted here because the primary code was not accessed. [Indian Association of Clinical Psychologists (secondary summary only)]
  • Teletherapy adds boundary and privacy demands, since the therapist must secure the setting, confirm the client's location for safety, and manage confidentiality across the technology used. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Mnemonic
SCRuB-T
Setting and frame, Culture and power distance, Ruptures and repair, Boundaries and dual relationships, Transference and teletherapy — the other factors that shape the alliance.

7Measuring the alliance and its link to outcome

The alliance can be measured with validated scales, and meta-analyses show a modest but remarkably consistent link between a strong alliance, especially early in therapy, and better treatment outcome across diagnoses and formats.

  • The alliance is measured with validated self-report and observer scales, chiefly the Working Alliance Inventory (WAI) of Horvath and Greenberg, the California Psychotherapy Alliance Scales (CALPAS), the Helping Alliance Questionnaire (HAq) and the brief Session Rating Scale. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • The WAI follows Bordin directly, scoring the three components of bond, goals and tasks, which makes the model measurable and lets therapist and client track the partnership over time. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Theoretical view Horvath and Symonds (1991) and later Horvath et al. (2011) found a moderate and consistent association between alliance and outcome of about r = .28 across many studies. [John C. Norcross (ed.), Psychotherapy Relationships That Work (APA Division 29 task force)]
  • Theoretical view Fluckiger et al. (2018) confirmed this alliance-outcome link in a large updated meta-analysis, showing it holds across therapies, diagnoses and formats rather than being tied to one school. [John C. Norcross (ed.), Psychotherapy Relationships That Work (APA Division 29 task force)]
  • The relationship is held to explain more outcome variance than the specific technique for many disorders, which is why common-factors researchers place the alliance at the centre of effective therapy. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • The early alliance, often measured by sessions three to five, predicts later outcome, so monitoring the bond early gives a practical, actionable warning sign well before termination. [John C. Norcross (ed.), Psychotherapy Relationships That Work (APA Division 29 task force)]
  • Causation runs both ways, because a good alliance partly causes improvement and is partly an effect of early symptom relief, so the two reinforce each other over a course of therapy. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Guideline The Norcross and Lambert APA task force classified the alliance, empathy, goal consensus, group cohesion and collecting client feedback as demonstrably effective relationship elements. [John C. Norcross (ed.), Psychotherapy Relationships That Work (APA Division 29 task force)]
  • Routine feedback, using brief alliance and outcome measures each session, lets the therapist catch a weakening bond and repair it, which is itself linked to better results. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • The clinical message is that measuring the alliance is not academic, since a low early score is a cue to slow down, invite feedback and repair the relationship before pressing on with technique. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Because the correlation is modest in size but highly consistent, the alliance is best read as a necessary foundation that works together with effective technique rather than a guarantee of success on its own. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • Theoretical view The convergence of Bordin's model, the WAI and the meta-analytic evidence gives the alliance a rare status in psychotherapy, as a concept that is at once theoretically clear, measurable and empirically supported. [John C. Norcross (ed.), Psychotherapy Relationships That Work (APA Division 29 task force)]
