1Relationship between structure and function of the brain

Neuropsychology rests on the assumption that the brain's structure and its functions are tightly linked, so that the organisation of neural tissue and its connections gives rise to cognition and behaviour, and damage to a structure predictably changes a function, an idea refined from strict localisation into the modern large-scale-network view.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: what the structure–function relationship means and why neuropsychology depends on it.
  • Background: the core assumption that mind and behaviour emerge from brain tissue and its wiring.
  • Key concepts: levels of structure, the three kinds of connectivity, and the network view.
  • Evidence and examples: the lesion method and imaging that reveal structure–function links.
  • Significance: why the relationship anchors the whole unit and clinical practice.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Standard neuropsychology treats the field as the study of the relation between brain and behaviour, asking how the structure and activity of the nervous system produce cognition, emotion and action. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Standard text A central aim is described as the elucidation of the relationship between structure and function of large-scale brain systems, that is, how the neural connectional network underlies cognitive functions. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • Classical account The modern view holds that cognitive functions arise from interactions within and between a distributed set of brain regions, so cognition is a global integration of local specialised processing. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]

Exam points

Definition and core assumption

  • The structure–function relationship means that the brain's physical organisation, its cells, regions and connections, is the basis of its functions, so that mind and behaviour are products of nervous tissue rather than of a separate immaterial agent. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Neuropsychology depends wholly on this link, because it infers how the brain works by observing how changes in structure, from injury or disease, produce changes in function such as memory, language or movement. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The guiding assumption is that the brain shows functional specialisation, meaning different structures make different contributions, which is why a focal lesion (an area of damaged or destroyed brain tissue) removes some abilities while sparing others. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]

Levels of structure that carry function

  • Structure operates at several levels, from single neuron (a nerve cell that receives, processes and transmits information) through local circuits and cortical columns to whole regions and systems, and function can be mapped onto any of these levels. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The neuron is the functional unit that lets us receive information, process it and produce actions, while glia (support cells that hold neurons together and nourish and insulate them) maintain the tissue that makes this possible. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • At the largest scale, the cerebral cortex carries higher functions such as thought and language, so its surface organisation into lobes and areas is a map of where many functions are chiefly handled. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]

The four lobes and their broad functions

  • The frontal lobe handles movement, speech production, and the executive functions of planning, judgement, problem-solving and the regulation of behaviour and emotion, so it is the brain's control and personality centre. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The parietal lobe integrates touch, pain, temperature and position sense, builds the sense of space and the body schema, and supports reading, arithmetic and the guidance of action in space. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • The temporal lobe carries hearing, the recognition of what things are, the comprehension of language, and, through its medial structures, the laying down of new long-term memories. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The occipital lobe is devoted to vision, receiving and interpreting the visual world, so that damage there produces blindness or specific visual deficits with the eyes themselves intact. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Deeper structures matter too: the brainstem sustains the vital functions of breathing and heartbeat and arousal, the cerebellum coordinates balance and smooth movement, and the thalamus is the relay through which most sensory traffic reaches the cortex. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]

Connectivity: the three kinds

  • Brain connectivity describes how distinct units are linked, and the unit note distinguishes three kinds: structural, functional and effective connectivity, each capturing a different sense in which regions are related. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • Structural connectivity (the physical white-matter fibre pathways joining regions) refers to the anatomical links between regions and can be estimated non-invasively with diffusion tensor imaging (a scan that traces the direction of water movement along fibre tracts). [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • Functional connectivity (a statistical correlation in activity between regions, without a stated cause) refers to temporal correlations or statistical dependencies between the activity of remote regions, but it does not show direction or causation. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • Effective connectivity (the directed causal influence one region exerts over another) describes the causal influence one neural element exerts over another, uniting the structural wiring with the functional pattern into a directed diagram. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]

The large-scale-network view

  • A newer paradigm holds that cognition results from the dynamic interactions of distributed areas operating in large-scale networks, such as the networks for vision, attention, memory and motion. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • On this view the architecture of the structural connections shapes the functional interactions among areas, so the fixed wiring constrains which flexible functional patterns can arise. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • This network view softens strict localisation, because a single function is seen to depend on a distributed circuit, and damage to a hub region can disturb activity far beyond the site of the lesion. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]

Evidence, significance and a clinical note

  • The oldest evidence for the link is the lesion method, in which a lost function is matched to the damaged structure, as when loss of fluent speech is matched to damage in the left frontal lobe. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Modern imaging supplies converging evidence, because techniques that show activity during a task reveal which structures engage for which functions, confirming the structure–function map in the living brain. [Brain Imaging Techniques (unit note)]
  • The relationship can change across the lifespan and in disease, since the coupling between structure and function strengthens with age and is disrupted in conditions such as schizophrenia and epilepsy. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • Clinically the link is the whole justification for neuropsychological assessment, because a reliable structure–function map lets a clinician reason from a pattern of deficits back toward the likely site and nature of brain pathology. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
Structure and function at a glance
  • The brain's structure (cells, regions, connections) gives rise to its functions (cognition, behaviour).
  • Functional specialisation: different structures make different contributions, so focal damage removes specific abilities.
  • Three connectivities: structural (wiring), functional (correlation), effective (causal direction).
  • Modern view: cognition emerges from distributed large-scale networks, not isolated spots.
  • Evidence: the lesion method plus imaging; significance: it justifies neuropsychological assessment.
Mnemonic
SHAPE
Structure Houses And Produces Experience — the brain's structure houses and produces behaviour; remember the three connectivities (Structural wiring, Functional correlation, Effective causation) and that cognition emerges from distributed networks, not single spots.

2The rise of neuropsychology as a distinct discipline

Neuropsychology is a relatively young discipline whose roots run from ancient Egypt and the Greeks through Willis, Gall, Bouillaud, Broca, Wernicke, Hughlings-Jackson, Lashley, Hebb and Luria, maturing into a named field only after Broca localised speech and after Hebb's and Kolb and Whishaw's integrative textbooks.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: what neuropsychology is and that it is a recent, distinct discipline.
  • Early history: the ancient brain-versus-heart debate and the mind–body problem.
  • Nineteenth-century pioneers: Gall, Bouillaud, Broca, Wernicke who localised function.
  • Twentieth-century shapers: Hughlings-Jackson, Lashley, Hebb and Luria.
  • Emergence as a field: the naming and the founding textbooks.
  • Significance and a one-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Neuropsychology is defined as the branch of psychology concerned with how the brain and nervous system influence cognition and behaviour, and especially how brain injury or illness affects them. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Standard text It is both an experimental and a clinical field that seeks to discover how the brain correlates with the mind through the study of neurological patients, bridging neurology and psychology. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Secondary source The first textbook defining the field, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology, was published by Kolb and Whishaw in 1980, a convenient marker of the discipline's maturity. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]

Exam points

Definition and the newness of the field

  • Neuropsychology is the field that studies the brain–behaviour relationship, combining neurology's focus on nervous-system pathology with psychology's focus on cognition, which is why it is described as both experimental and clinical. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • It is a relatively new discipline, even though its questions are ancient, and it became a recognisable named field only in the twentieth century with integrative theory and textbooks. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • The word itself was popularised through its use as a subtitle of Donald Hebb's 1949 book The Organization of Behavior, signalling a deliberately multidisciplinary science of brain and behaviour. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Ancient roots and the mind–body problem

  • In ancient Egypt, medical writings from the time of Imhotep described the brain and head trauma, yet Egyptians still saw the heart, not the brain, as the seat of the soul. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Aristotle reinforced this heart-centred view, treating the brain as a radiator that cooled the blood, whereas Hippocrates argued the brain held the greatest power and was the seat of the mind. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • The philosopher René Descartes framed the enduring mind–body problem (the question of how a non-physical mind relates to the physical body), proposing dualism (the view that mind and body are distinct but interacting) with the pineal gland as their meeting point. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Galen, physician to the Roman gladiators, inferred from head injuries that the brain governs sensation and movement, but located mental life in the fluid-filled ventricles (the fluid-filled cavities inside the brain), a 'ventricular doctrine' that dominated for over a thousand years. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Thomas Willis took a physiological view of the brain in the seventeenth century, coined the words 'hemisphere' and 'lobe', and used 'neurology', linking higher structures to complex functions. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]

Nineteenth-century localisers

  • Franz Josef Gall proposed that mental faculties sat in specific brain regions and founded phrenology (the discredited claim that skull bumps reveal mental faculties), which, though wrong, put brain localisation onto the scientific agenda. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Pierre Flourens demolished phrenology with controlled lesion experiments, arguing instead that the cortex worked as a whole and could recover function, an early statement of the holistic position. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Jean-Baptiste Bouillaud argued from many clinical cases that speech is localised in the frontal lobes and was among the first to use large samples, extending Gall's localisation on firmer ground. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Paul Broca in 1861 examined the patient 'Tan', found a left frontal lesion at autopsy, and so showed that language is localised and lateralised, a demonstration widely taken to launch the science. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Carl Wernicke in 1874 showed that a posterior temporal lesion disturbs language comprehension, described a connectionist model of language, and predicted a 'conduction' aphasia from a disconnection. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]

Twentieth-century shapers

  • John Hughlings-Jackson proposed that the nervous system is organised in a functional hierarchy of levels, so disease produces 'dissolution', a reversal of evolution toward simpler behaviour. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Karl Lashley sought the memory trace or engram (the hypothetical physical location of a stored memory) in rats and concluded, from his lesion studies, in favour of mass action (the idea that the cortex acts as a whole, by amount not location) and equipotentiality (the idea that intact cortex can take over a lost region's role). [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Donald Hebb gave the field a unifying theory in which cell assemblies and strengthened synapses link neural activity to learning, providing a bridge from physiology to psychology. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Alexander Luria built a comprehensive functional-systems account of the brain from his work with brain-injured soldiers, shaping both the theory and the clinical assessment of the new discipline. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]

Emergence, significance and a note

  • The field finally crystallised when localisation, hierarchy and functional-systems ideas were combined with rigorous patient study and, from 1980, gathered into a defining textbook. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Its rise matters because it replaced spirit- and heart-based explanations of behaviour with a testable science that traces cognition and its disorders to the brain. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Modern neuropsychology now spans research, clinical and forensic settings, showing how far the discipline has travelled from the ancient belief that the brain was a useless organ discarded at burial. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
The rise of neuropsychology at a glance
  • Ancient roots: heart-versus-brain debate (Egypt, Aristotle) → brain as seat of mind (Hippocrates).
  • Descartes' mind–body problem and dualism; Willis coins 'hemisphere', 'lobe', 'neurology'.
  • Localisers: Gall (phrenology), Flourens (holism), Bouillaud, Broca (1861), Wernicke (1874).
  • Shapers: Hughlings-Jackson (hierarchy), Lashley (mass action, equipotentiality), Hebb, Luria.
  • Named field: Hebb (1949) popularises the word; Kolb & Whishaw (1980) write the defining textbook.
Mnemonic
GB-WHLH
Gall, Bouillaud, Wernicke, Broca, Hughlings-Jackson, Lashley, Hebb/Luria — the chain of pioneers from phrenology through the localisation of speech to the hierarchy, mass action and the functional-systems theories that named the discipline.

3The logic of cerebral organization

The brain is organised on a logic of hierarchy and functional systems: Hughlings-Jackson's layered levels control behaviour from the spinal cord up to the forebrain, while Luria grouped the cortex into functional units and three processing zones, with redundancy and plasticity allowing parts of a system to compensate for loss.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: what is meant by the 'logic' or organising principles of the brain.
  • Background: Hughlings-Jackson's hierarchy and the evolution–dissolution idea.
  • Key concepts: Luria's functional units and the primary–secondary–tertiary zones.
  • Levels and redundancy: parallel organisation, plasticity and compensation.
  • Evidence and significance: how the logic explains deficits and recovery.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Classical account Hughlings-Jackson conceived the nervous system as organised in layers arranged in a functional hierarchy, each higher level controlling more complex behaviour through the lower levels. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Classical account Luria divided the cortex into functional units, a posterior sensory unit that receives, processes and stores information and an anterior motor unit that forms intentions and programs action. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Standard text Luria's broader account describes three principal functional units or blocks, one for arousal, one for receiving and processing information, and one for planning and regulating activity. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]

Exam points

Definition and organising principles

  • The 'logic' of cerebral organization refers to the general principles by which the brain is arranged, chiefly hierarchy, functional systems, levels of processing and redundancy, rather than a mere list of parts. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The brain is not a bag of equal tissue, because it shows an orderly plan in which simple functions sit at lower levels and increasingly complex functions are built upon them at higher levels. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • A key principle is that complex behaviours are run by functional systems, chains of cooperating regions, so a single ability draws on several structures working together rather than one centre. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]

Hierarchical organisation (Hughlings-Jackson)

  • Hughlings-Jackson arranged the nervous system into a hierarchy of levels, often the spinal cord, the brainstem and the forebrain, with each higher level governing more complex aspects of behaviour. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • He borrowed from evolution the idea that the brain was built in steps, so that higher levels, added later, control and refine the older, simpler machinery beneath them. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Damage to the highest levels therefore produces dissolution (a reversal of evolution, leaving simpler, more primitive behaviour), in which the person keeps a repertoire of behaviours but of a simpler, more automatic kind. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • This logic reconciled the old dispute, because experiments showing an animal could still walk after cortical loss simply revealed lower-level behaviour surviving once the higher controller was gone. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Luria's functional units and zones

  • Luria grouped the cortex into a posterior sensory unit, which receives and stores information, and an anterior motor unit, which forms intentions and executes programs of action. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • His wider model describes three functional units or blocks: a brainstem unit for arousal (the general level of alertness and readiness of the brain), a posterior unit for processing information, and a frontal unit for planning. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]
  • Within each cortical unit he described three zones arranged one above the other, so processing moves from simple to integrated as it passes from zone to zone. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The primary zone handles raw sensation or movement, the secondary zone (cortex that organises and combines inputs into patterns) organises it, and the tertiary zone (association cortex that integrates across the senses for the most complex functions) integrates across systems. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Note that accounts differ: Kolb and Whishaw summarise Luria as dividing the cortex into two functional units, while Luria's own writings set out the three-block scheme, a difference worth stating plainly. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Levels, redundancy and plasticity

  • Because functions are built on multiple levels, a behaviour can survive in simplified form when a higher level is lost, which is the hierarchical logic seen from the clinical side. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The brain shows redundancy and plasticity, so within limits intact tissue can take over part of a lost region's role, which Lashley once overstated as equipotentiality. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Recognising this plasticity (the brain's capacity to reorganise and let areas take on new roles) is one practical pay-off of localization work, because it underlies the possibility of recovery and rehabilitation. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]

Evidence and significance

  • The organising logic is supported by the orderly march of symptoms in epilepsy and the graded effects of lesions, which fit a layered, systems-based plan rather than a single undifferentiated mass. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Its significance is that it lets a clinician predict the pattern of a deficit from the level and system affected, and understand why some functions recover while others do not. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
The logic of cerebral organization at a glance
  • Organising principles: hierarchy, functional systems, levels of processing, redundancy.
  • Hughlings-Jackson: layered levels (cord, brainstem, forebrain); damage to the top causes dissolution.
  • Luria: posterior sensory unit and anterior motor unit; wider three-block (arousal, processing, planning) model.
  • Zones: primary (raw) → secondary (organise) → tertiary (integrate across systems).
  • Redundancy and plasticity allow partial compensation; the logic predicts deficits and recovery.
Mnemonic
HULiP
Hierarchy, Units, Levels, Plasticity — the brain is a hierarchy (Hughlings-Jackson) of functional Units (Luria) built in Levels (primary–secondary–tertiary zones) with Plasticity and redundancy that allow compensation.

4Localization of functions

Localization of function is the principle that specific mental operations are handled by specific brain regions, demonstrated by Broca's and Wernicke's language areas and the stimulation maps of Fritsch, Hitzig and Ferrier, and set against the opposing equipotentiality or mass-action view, with the modern position a qualified, network-based localisation.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: what localization of function means.
  • Background: the debate between localizationism and holism/equipotentiality.
  • Key evidence: Broca's and Wernicke's areas and the stimulation maps.
  • Mapping and modularity: Brodmann's areas and the idea of modules.
  • The localization debate resolved: a qualified, network-based view.
  • Significance and a one-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Cerebral localization is defined as the mapping of the cerebral cortex into areas and the correlation of those areas with particular cerebral functions. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Classical account The localizationist position holds that different parts of the brain have different functions, so that focal damage removes a specific ability while sparing others. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Classical account The opposing view, developed by Flourens and later Lashley, holds that the cortex works as a whole through mass action and equipotentiality, de-emphasising fixed centres. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Exam points

Definition and the two positions

  • Localization of function is the principle that a specific function is carried out by a specific region, so that mapping the cortex into areas and matching them to functions is possible and clinically useful. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • The clinical form of the idea is the diagnosis of the site of a lesion from the patient's signs and symptoms, reasoning backward from the deficit to the damaged structure. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • It stands against equipotentiality (the idea that any part of a functional area can do the work of the whole), which with mass action (the idea that the cortex acts by the amount of tissue, not its location) claims the cortex acts as an undifferentiated whole. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]

The founding language evidence

  • Paul Broca localised speech production to the posterior third of the left inferior frontal gyrus, now Broca's area, after his patient 'Tan' proved to have a lesion there, giving the first strong evidence for localisation. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Damage to this region causes Broca's aphasia (non-fluent, effortful, telegraphic speech with relatively preserved comprehension), in which the patient understands well but produces slow, laboured, agrammatic output. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Carl Wernicke localised language comprehension to the posterior superior temporal region, now Wernicke's area, where a lesion disturbs understanding while speech stays fluent. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Damage there causes Wernicke's aphasia (fluent but meaningless speech with poor comprehension), and a lesion of the connecting fibres produces a conduction aphasia (good comprehension and speech but very poor repetition), exactly as Wernicke predicted. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]

Stimulation and mapping evidence

  • Fritsch and Hitzig in 1870 showed that electrically stimulating points on the dog's cortex produced movements of specific muscles, the first experimental proof that the motor cortex is topographically organised. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • David Ferrier and others extended stimulation mapping to the auditory and other cortices in primates, and Penfield later mapped motor and sensory cortex in conscious human surgical patients. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Penfield and Rasmussen outlined the motor and sensory homunculus (a distorted body map on the cortex, with large areas for the hands and lips), showing that body parts needing fine control occupy disproportionately large cortical territory. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Korbinian Brodmann divided the cortex into roughly fifty-two numbered areas on cytoarchitectonic (based on the microscopic arrangement and types of cells) grounds, giving localisation a durable anatomical map still used today. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]

Modularity and the debate resolved

  • The idea of modularity sharpens localisation, proposing that the mind has specialised, somewhat independent processors, so a specific module can be selectively impaired by focal damage. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Lashley's mass action and equipotentiality were later understood as a misreading of his rat data, because running a maze needs several cortical areas, so large lesions impaired many functions at once. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • The modern position is a qualified localisation, in which functions are localised yet distributed across cooperating regions, combining the truth in localizationism with the network view of organisation. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]

Localising signs by lobe

  • Frontal-lobe damage produces impaired memory and planning, poor judgement and insight, disinhibition or apathy, and language problems such as Broca's aphasia, the Phineas Gage case being the classic illustration of a changed personality after frontal injury. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Parietal-lobe damage produces somatosensory loss, astereognosis (inability to recognise objects by touch alone), and, on the right, disturbances of the body schema such as neglect, anosognosia (denial or unawareness of one's own deficit), and dressing or constructional apraxia. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Temporal-lobe damage produces hearing and auditory-recognition problems, Wernicke's aphasia on the left, memory disturbance, and complex phenomena such as déjà vu or olfactory hallucinations in temporal-lobe seizures. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Occipital-lobe damage produces visual-field loss (hemianopia (loss of one half of the visual field)) and the visual agnosias (failures to recognise what is seen, with vision itself intact), including prosopagnosia (the specific inability to recognise faces). [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • A revealing cluster is Gerstmann syndrome (agraphia, acalculia, finger agnosia and right–left confusion from the left angular gyrus), whose four signs together point precisely to the dominant parietal region. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]

The aphasias as a localising map

  • Broca's (motor, non-fluent) aphasia follows damage to areas 44/45, leaving good comprehension but effortful, telegraphic, agrammatic speech. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Wernicke's (sensory, fluent) aphasia follows damage to area 22, leaving fluent but poorly understood speech with impaired comprehension. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Conduction aphasia follows damage to the arcuate fasciculus connecting the two areas, so comprehension and speech are good but repetition is strikingly poor, exactly the disconnection Wernicke predicted. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Anomic aphasia shows mainly word-finding difficulty, and a lesion of the angular and supramarginal gyri can yield fluent but unintelligible jargon, so the type of aphasia itself maps the site of damage. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • The connecting fibre tract is named: conduction aphasia (good comprehension and fluent speech but a striking inability to repeat words just heard) follows damage to the arcuate fasciculus (the fibre bundle linking Wernicke's area to Broca's area), confirming that the two language areas must be joined to repeat speech. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Wernicke's fluent output is often called 'word salad', because despite correct grammar, rhythm and intonation the words do not convey meaning, which pinpoints a comprehension rather than a production fault. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Reading and writing have their own site: alexia (an inability to read despite intact comprehension and speech) and agraphia (an inability to write despite intact comprehension and speech) follow damage to the left angular gyrus, showing this region handles the language-related visual code. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

The Wernicke–Geschwind model and its limits

  • The Wernicke–Geschwind model strings seven left-hemisphere components into language circuits: primary visual cortex, angular gyrus, primary auditory cortex, Wernicke's area, arcuate fasciculus, Broca's area and primary motor cortex. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • To answer a heard question, sound runs from primary auditory cortex to Wernicke's area for comprehension, and the reply passes via the arcuate fasciculus to Broca's area and on to the motor and articulatory cortex. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • To read a word aloud, the path runs from primary visual cortex to the left angular gyrus, which turns the visual code into an auditory one, then to Wernicke's area and onward along the same output route. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • The model is now known to be too neat: damage at its boundaries often has little lasting effect, damage elsewhere can cause aphasia, real aphasia is rarely 'pure' (usually both receptive and expressive), and the localisation varies markedly between individuals. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

Significance

  • Localisation is the working logic of clinical neuropsychology and neurology, letting a clinician infer where the brain is damaged from the pattern of language, memory or perceptual deficits. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • It also guides neurosurgery, where mapping eloquent 'no-go' areas before operating protects speech and movement and increases the precision of safe resection. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
Localization of functions at a glance
  • Localization: specific functions in specific regions; the clinical art of locating a lesion from signs.
  • Opposed by equipotentiality and mass action (Flourens, Lashley): the cortex as a whole.
  • Language evidence: Broca's area (production) and Wernicke's area (comprehension).
  • Mapping: Fritsch–Hitzig and Ferrier stimulation, Penfield's homunculus, Brodmann's 52 areas.
  • Modern view: qualified, network-based localisation; it guides diagnosis and neurosurgery.

BROCA
Broca, Regions, Opponents, Cytoarchitecture, Areas — Broca and Wernicke localised language to Regions; Opponents (Flourens, Lashley) argued equipotentiality; Brodmann's Cytoarchitecture mapped the numbered Areas that localisation still uses.

5Lateralization of functions

Lateralization is the principle that the two cerebral hemispheres are specialised for different functions: the left usually dominates language, analysis and sequencing, the right spatial, musical and holistic processing, a division revealed by Broca's left-sided speech finding and most dramatically by the split-brain studies of Sperry and Gazzaniga.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Lateralization refers to the finding that functions can be localised to one side of the brain, a property Broca first demonstrated when speech proved to depend on the left hemisphere. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Standard text The dominant (usually left) hemisphere is characterised as specialised for language, analytical and mathematical skills and the temporal sequencing of stimuli. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Standard text The non-dominant (usually right) hemisphere is characterised as specialised for spatial perception, singing and the playing of musical instruments. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]

Exam points

Definition and discovery

  • Lateralization, also called cerebral dominance (the tendency for one hemisphere to lead a given function) or asymmetry, is the principle that the two hemispheres are not identical in function but specialised for different kinds of processing. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • The discovery grew directly from Broca's finding, because locating speech in the left frontal lobe showed that a function could belong to one side, which he named lateralisation. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The two hemispheres look nearly symmetrical and are joined by the corpus callosum (the great fibre bridge that connects the two hemispheres), yet they divide cognitive labour between them. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

The dominant left hemisphere: aphasia and apraxia

  • As early as 1836 Dax reported that none of his roughly forty patients with speech problems had damage confined to the right hemisphere, an early clue that speech loss is tied to the left side. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • About twenty-five years later Broca (1861) examined two aphasic patients post-mortem and found diffuse left-hemisphere damage centred in the inferior left prefrontal lobe, just in front of the primary motor face area, defining Broca's area for grammar and speech production. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Here aphasia (a loss or impairment of language not due to general sensory, motor or intellectual deficit) is the key sign, so its reliable link to left-sided damage was the first evidence that language is a left-hemisphere function. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Liepmann then showed that apraxia (difficulty performing movements to command with either hand, though they occur spontaneously) was almost always associated with left-hemisphere damage too, extending the left's dominance beyond language to skilled action. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • This weight of evidence led to calling the left the 'dominant' and the right the 'minor' hemisphere, a once-standard terminology that later work would soften into relative, not absolute, specialisation. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

Left- and right-hemisphere specialisations

  • The left hemisphere is usually dominant for language, covering speech and writing, and for analytical, mathematical and sequential processing that unfolds step by step in time. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • The right hemisphere is usually dominant for spatial perception of three-dimensional objects, for music such as singing and playing instruments, and for holistic, simultaneous processing. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • A useful contrast is that the left tends to work analytically and verbally while the right works holistically and spatially, though both cooperate in nearly every real task. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Right-hemisphere damage characteristically produces spatial and body-schema disturbances such as neglect of the left side, dressing and constructional difficulties, mirroring the left's language role. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Language is the most lateralised of all abilities, yet the right hemisphere is not wordless: it can understand single written and spoken words and, importantly, detects prosody (the melody, stress and rhythm of speech that carry tone and emphasis) and the thread of discourse (how sentences connect into a coherent, extended conversation). [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • A deeper contrast is in how memory and perception are organised, the left placing experience in a larger context (relating parts to the whole) while the right attends to Gestalt whole-pattern characteristics, summarised as analytical (left) versus holistic (right) processing. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Because its abilities are different rather than lesser, the right should not be dismissed as the 'minor' hemisphere; it simply specialises in spatial, emotional, musical and holistic work the left does less well. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • These functional asymmetries have anatomical correlates: the language-related planum temporale (the cortical surface behind the primary auditory area, on the upper temporal lobe) and frontal operculum are usually larger in the left hemisphere, while Heschl's gyrus (the ridge of cortex carrying the primary auditory area) is larger in the right. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Left-handers tend to have more symmetrical planum temporales, suffer less often from aphasia after left-hemisphere damage and more often after right-hemisphere damage, which points to a more diffuse representation of language in their brains. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

The split-brain experiment in animals (Myers & Sperry, cats)

  • The method that transformed the field began with Myers and Sperry's 1953 cat experiment, designed to reveal the function of the brain's largest commissure (a band of fibres joining matching areas of the two hemispheres), the corpus callosum. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • They used four groups of cats: one with the corpus callosum severed, one with the optic chiasm severed, one with both severed, and an intact control group, so that information could be restricted to a single hemisphere. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • After all cats learned a pattern-discrimination task with a patch over one eye, the patch was switched to the other eye; only the both-severed group behaved as if the task were brand new, having to relearn it with no savings. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • The conclusions reshaped the field: the forebrain can act as two separate forebrains, each able to learn and store its own memories, and the corpus callosum's job is to carry information between the hemispheres. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

Split-brain evidence

  • The clearest evidence came from split-brain patients, in whom surgeons cut the corpus callosum to control severe epilepsy, which stops the hemispheres from sharing information directly. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Work by Roger Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga used the crossed wiring of vision, in which each visual field projects to the opposite hemisphere, to speak to one hemisphere at a time. [Michael S. Gazzaniga, Cognitive Neuroscience: The Biology of the Mind]
  • When an object was shown only to the right visual field, the left (speaking) hemisphere could name it, but when shown only to the left field, the right hemisphere recognised it yet could not speak its name. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • These studies showed that each hemisphere has its own abilities and awareness, confirming lateralisation in the most direct way and earning Sperry a share of the Nobel Prize. [Michael S. Gazzaniga, Cognitive Neuroscience: The Biology of the Mind]
  • The human operation is a commissurotomy (surgical cutting of the corpus callosum to stop seizures spreading between the hemispheres), done for life-threatening epilepsy; it is remarkably effective and leaves few obvious effects in everyday life. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Testing flashes a stimulus to one visual half-field or places an object in one hand under a ledge, so that it reaches only one hemisphere, revealing that each patient has two independent streams of consciousness. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • An object reaching the left hemisphere (right visual field or right hand) can be named aloud and retrieved with the right hand but not the left, whereas an object reaching the right hemisphere (left field or left hand) is retrieved by the left hand yet the patient says nothing was presented. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Cross-cuing (one hemisphere signalling the other by a nonneural route, such as an outward head movement) shows the hemispheres can still communicate indirectly: a patient near-perfect at naming a colour seen by the mute hemisphere was found to be correcting himself after the knowing hemisphere shook his head, so scoring only first guesses dropped him to chance. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Split-brain patients can even learn two things at once, reaching into two bags simultaneously and pulling out with each hand the different object each hemisphere had seen. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • In the helping-hand phenomenon the hemispheres are given conflicting information, and the hand guided by one hemisphere reaches for an answer while the other hand, driven by the hemisphere that sees the error, grabs it and pulls it to the correct choice. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

Three theories of cerebral asymmetry

  • The analytic-synthetic theory proposes two modes of thinking, an analytic mode in the left (logical, sequential, handling pieces of the whole) and a synthetic mode in the right (immediate, overall judgements of the whole). [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • The motor theory argues the left is specialised for fine motor control, of which speech is one example, supported by evidence that left lesions disrupt even non-speech facial movements more than right lesions and that the disruption tracks the degree of aphasia. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • The linguistic theory holds the left's primary job is language itself, drawing on deaf signers who lose the ability to sign after left-hemisphere damage even though they can still make the required hand movements. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

Tests of cerebral lateralization

  • The first evidence of language laterality came from simply comparing the effects of left-sided against right-sided unilateral lesions, before any technique could test a living brain directly. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • The sodium amytal (Wada) test (an anaesthetic injected into one carotid artery briefly puts one hemisphere to sleep to reveal the language side) and the dichotic listening test (different words played to the two ears at once; the ear reported better reveals the dominant hemisphere) are the two methods routinely used today to assess which hemisphere holds language. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Functional imaging agrees, with PET and fMRI typically showing more activity in the left hemisphere than the right during language tasks, confirming the lesion-based picture in the intact brain. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

Handedness, dominance and cautions

  • Language is left-lateralised in almost all right-handers and in most left-handers too, so left-hemisphere dominance for speech is the usual pattern across the population. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Put in numbers, about 95% of right-handed people and roughly 70% of left-handed or ambidextrous people are left-hemisphere dominant for speech, so right-handedness predicts left dominance far more strongly than left-handedness predicts right dominance. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Dominance is not wholly fixed, because early left-hemisphere damage can make the right hemisphere take over speech and the left hand become preferred, showing the young brain's plasticity for language. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • The Wada test (an injection that briefly sends one hemisphere to sleep to find the language side) and modern imaging are used before surgery to confirm which hemisphere holds language in a given patient. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • The specialisations are relative, not absolute, so popular talk of purely 'left-brained' or 'right-brained' people over-simplifies what is a matter of degree and cooperation. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Significance

  • Lateralisation lets a clinician predict which hemisphere is damaged from the profile of deficits, for example language loss pointing left and spatial neglect pointing right. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • It also protects patients in surgery, because knowing the language-dominant side guides the surgeon away from eloquent cortex and informs techniques such as the Wada procedure and cortical mapping. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
Lateralization of functions at a glance
  • Lateralization = hemispheric specialisation; the hemispheres differ in function though joined by the corpus callosum.
  • Left (dominant): language (Dax, Broca's area), analysis, mathematics, sequencing, skilled action (Liepmann's apraxia).
  • Right (minor, not lesser): spatial perception, music, emotion, prosody and discourse, holistic processing; its damage causes neglect.
  • Anatomy follows function: planum temporale/frontal operculum larger left, Heschl's gyrus larger right; left-handers more diffuse.
  • Split-brain: Myers & Sperry's cats (two forebrains); human commissurotomy gives two streams of awareness, cross-cuing and the helping hand.
  • Three theories of asymmetry: analytic-synthetic, motor, linguistic.
  • Tests of laterality: left-vs-right lesions, the sodium amytal (Wada) test, dichotic listening, and PET/fMRI.
  • Handedness: ~95% of right-handers and ~70% of left-handers are left-dominant for speech; early left damage can shift dominance right.
Mnemonic
LARS-WD
Left = Analysis, Right = Spatial; Wada, Dichotic — the Left hemisphere leads language, Analysis and sequencing, the Right leads Spatial, musical and holistic work; split-brain studies (Myers & Sperry's cats, then human commissurotomy) proved each side can act alone, and laterality is tested by the Wada and Dichotic-listening tests.

6Approaches and methodologies of clinical neuropsychologists

Clinical neuropsychologists assess, diagnose and rehabilitate people with brain injury or disease using standardised test batteries, either fixed batteries such as the Halstead-Reitan and Luria-Nebraska and the Indian NIMHANS battery, or a flexible, hypothesis-driven selection, supported by the case and lesion method, interview and behavioural observation.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Clinical neuropsychology is the application of neuropsychological knowledge to the assessment, management and rehabilitation of people who have suffered illness or injury affecting the brain. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Standard text Neuropsychological tests are described as standardised tasks, administered first to reference groups to yield normative data (typical scores from a reference group, used to judge a patient) against which an individual's performance is compared. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Standard text Clinical assessment is characterised as distinguishing deficits due to brain pathology from those due to emotional or other, potentially reversible, causes. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]

Exam points

Definition and the clinician's task

  • The clinical neuropsychologist applies knowledge of brain–behaviour relations to assess, diagnose and rehabilitate patients whose brain injury or illness has caused cognitive or behavioural problems. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • A core task is to decide whether a difficulty reflects brain pathology or a reversible cause such as depression, which refines a vague diagnosis into a specific, useful one. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • The work rests on standardised tests whose scores are compared with normative data, so that a patient's performance can be judged against what is typical for their age and background. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]

The case and lesion method

  • The oldest clinical method is the case and lesion method, in which a patient's pattern of spared and lost abilities is matched to the site of brain damage to infer that region's normal role. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • This method made the discipline, from Broca's single case 'Tan' onward, and it still frames how a clinician reasons from a deficit profile to a likely lesion. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Careful interview and behavioural observation sit alongside testing, because the history, the manner of failure and everyday function all inform the interpretation of scores. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]

Fixed versus flexible batteries

  • A fixed battery (a standard set of tests given in full to every patient) applies the same comprehensive set of tests to everyone, giving broad, comparable coverage and a fixed scoring scheme. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • The Halstead-Reitan Battery is the classic fixed battery, a fixed set of tests yielding an impairment index that screens broadly for the presence and lateralisation of brain damage. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • The Luria-Nebraska Battery is a fixed battery that systematised Luria's qualitative examination into standardised items grouped into clinical scales. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • The NIMHANS neuropsychological battery, developed in India, adapts and norms tests for the Indian population, an example of culturally appropriate standardisation of a battery. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • A flexible battery (tests chosen case by case to test specific hypotheses) instead selects tests to answer the specific referral question, following a hypothesis-testing logic that tailors the examination to the patient. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • The trade-off is that fixed batteries give standard, comparable breadth but can be long and insensitive to a specific question, while flexible batteries are efficient and targeted but harder to compare across patients. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]

The assessment process and domains

  • A full assessment samples several cognitive domains, including attention, memory, language, praxis (the ability to carry out skilled, planned movements), perception and executive function, so that the whole profile is mapped. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Example tests named in the literature include the Wechsler memory and intelligence scales, the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test and the Boston Naming Test, each linked to particular processes. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Scores are interpreted against norms and the patient's own baseline, looking for the pattern of relative strengths and weaknesses rather than any single number in isolation. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]

Rehabilitation, settings and significance

  • Beyond diagnosis, clinicians plan cognitive rehabilitation, using retraining and compensatory strategies that draw on the brain's plasticity to improve everyday functioning after injury. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • They work in hospitals, rehabilitation units, private practice and forensic settings, often within an interdisciplinary team, and may give expert opinion in medico-legal cases. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • The significance of the clinical approach is that it turns the structure–function map into practical diagnosis, prognosis and treatment, directly helping patients recover function. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
Clinical methods at a glance
  • Clinical neuropsychology = assessment, diagnosis and rehabilitation of brain-injured patients.
  • Foundations: the case/lesion method, interview, behavioural observation and standardised tests with norms.
  • Fixed batteries: Halstead-Reitan, Luria-Nebraska, NIMHANS (India); broad and comparable.
  • Flexible batteries: tests chosen to test hypotheses; efficient and targeted.
  • Domains sampled across attention, memory, language, praxis, perception, executive function; then rehabilitation.

7Approaches and methodologies of cognitive neuropsychologists

Cognitive neuropsychology studies brain-damaged people to understand the normal mind, using information-processing models, the single-case method and the logic of double dissociation to argue that the mind is built of separable modules, complemented by modern electrophysiological (EEG, ERP) and haemodynamic (PET, fMRI) imaging and by stimulation methods such as TMS.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Cognitive neuropsychology is described as a distillation of experimental and clinical neuropsychology, seeking to understand the mind and brain by studying people with brain injury or neurological illness. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Standard text One line of work investigates how the pattern of errors produced by brain-damaged individuals can constrain our understanding of mental representations and processes. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • Classical account The approach assumes functional modularity, that the mind is made of specialised, separable components, so that focal damage can impair one component while sparing others. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Exam points

Definition and distinctiveness

  • Cognitive neuropsychology uses the breakdown of cognition after brain damage to work out the structure of the normal, healthy mind, rather than mainly to diagnose or treat the patient. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • It differs from the clinical branch in its primary goal, which is theoretical, using patients as natural experiments that reveal how intact cognition is organised. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • It emerged as a distillation of experimental and clinical neuropsychology, borrowing the experimentalist's models and the clinician's patients. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]

Information-processing models and single cases

  • The approach builds information-processing models (box-and-arrow diagrams of the separable stages of a mental task), which treat a cognitive ability as a flow of distinct stages that can be mapped and then selectively broken. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • It relies on the single-case method, studying one informative patient in depth, because averaging across patients with different lesions can blur the very dissociations that matter. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Rich single cases in the clinical tradition, such as those described by Oliver Sacks, show how a focal deficit can fractionate the mind in striking and theoretically telling ways. [Oliver Sacks, The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat]

Modularity and the logic of dissociation

  • The key assumption is modularity, the view that the mind is composed of specialised processors, so that a single process can be knocked out while the rest of cognition continues. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • A single dissociation (one patient is impaired on task A but normal on task B) suggests the two tasks use at least partly different processes, but it can also arise if one task is simply harder. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • A double dissociation (one patient fails A but not B, another fails B but not A) is the strong proof, because it shows the two abilities depend on separable systems rather than on shared difficulty. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The classic example is the double dissociation between Broca's and Wernicke's aphasias, where one spares comprehension but ruins production and the other does the reverse, implying separate language components. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

From the Wernicke–Geschwind model to a cognitive-neuroscience view of language

  • Because humans are the only species with language, lesion comparisons across species are impossible, so the cognitive-neuroscience approach leans on brain imaging of healthy people to study it. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Its first move is to break each Wernicke–Geschwind component into constituent cognitive processes, namely phonological analysis of sounds, grammatical analysis of structure and semantic analysis of meaning. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Its second claim is that language areas are not solely dedicated to language, since processes such as short-term memory and visual pattern recognition serve reading as well as many other behaviours. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • The sharpest contrast is in where language lives: the Wernicke–Geschwind model assumes a few large, circumscribed language areas, whereas cognitive neuroscience assumes many small, widely distributed and specialised ones. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

Dyslexia as a window on reading

  • Dyslexia (a pathological difficulty in reading that is not due to general visual, motor or intellectual deficit) comes in a developmental form, apparent as a child learns to read, and an acquired form, caused by brain damage in someone who could already read. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Developmental dyslexia has reported brain differences (for example a missing planum-temporale asymmetry) but none clearly decisive, and it is hard to tell cause from effect, since little reading experience may itself shape the brain. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Acquired dyslexia exposes two reading-aloud routes: a lexical procedure (recognising a whole stored word on sight and saying it) and a phonetic procedure (sounding a word out letter by letter from its spelling). [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • Surface dyslexia (loss of the whole-word lexical route, so irregular words cannot be read) lets the reader pronounce non-words and regular words but not irregular ones such as 'have' or 'steak', whereas deep dyslexia (loss of the sounding-out phonetic route, so novel strings cannot be read) blocks non-words while sparing irregular words such as 'yacht'. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]
  • The two map differently onto the brain, with deep dyslexia following left-hemisphere damage and surface dyslexia following partial left-hemisphere or right-hemisphere damage, a neat double dissociation of the reading routes. [course lecture materials (Lateralization and the Split Brain)]

Modern electrophysiological methods

  • Electroencephalography (EEG, recording the brain's electrical activity from scalp electrodes) records ongoing electrical activity with excellent timing, and its magnetic counterpart is magneto-encephalography. [Brain Imaging Techniques (unit note)]
  • Event-related potentials (ERPs, averaged brain waves time-locked to a stimulus) are slow waves produced by averaging many trials, which lifts the signal out of background noise and reveals the brain's response to each event. [Brain Imaging Techniques (unit note)]
  • ERP components are named by polarity and timing, such as N100 or P300, and the N400 was the first component linked to language, growing larger when a word is semantically surprising. [Brain Imaging Techniques (unit note)]
  • Because ERPs lift the signal out of noise by averaging many trials, the amplitude of a component such as the N400 tracks processing difficulty, so harder or more surprising items give a larger wave. [Brain Imaging Techniques (unit note)]

Modern haemodynamic and stimulation methods

  • Positron emission tomography (PET, which images blood flow or metabolism using a radioactive tracer) images function from blood flow and glucose metabolism, and uses subtraction, taking a rest image away from a task image to reveal the regions a task engages. [Brain Imaging Techniques (unit note)]
  • Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI, which images activity from blood-oxygen changes) detects the blood-oxygen-level-dependent signal that follows neural activity, giving good spatial detail without any radioactive tracer. [Brain Imaging Techniques (unit note)]
  • A general trade-off holds that EEG and ERP have fine timing but coarse location, whereas PET and fMRI have fine location but slower timing, so methods are chosen to complement one another. [Brain Imaging Techniques (unit note)]
  • Magneto-encephalography (MEG, recording the tiny magnetic fields produced by neural activity) is the non-invasive magnetic counterpart of EEG, sharing its fine timing while locating sources somewhat more precisely. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]
  • Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI, which traces white-matter tracts by the direction of water diffusion) reconstructs the brain's fibre pathways, so structural connectivity itself can now be mapped and related to function in the living brain. [The Relation between Structure and Function of the Brain (unit note)]
  • Transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS, a magnetic pulse that briefly disrupts or excites a cortical area) can create a brief, reversible 'virtual lesion', letting researchers test a region's causal role without permanent damage. [Cerebral Localization (unit presentation)]

Significance and a caution

  • These approaches are not mutually exclusive, and most neuropsychologists combine lesion evidence, cognitive models and imaging to converge on how a function is organised. [A History of Neuropsychology (unit overview)]
  • The significance of cognitive neuropsychology is that it turns patients into a theory of the normal mind, grounding psychology's models of memory, language and perception in the architecture of the brain. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
Cognitive methods at a glance
  • Cognitive neuropsychology studies patients to model the normal mind (a theoretical, not mainly clinical, goal).
  • Tools: information-processing models, the single-case method, the modularity assumption.
  • Core logic: double dissociation proves separable systems (e.g. Broca's vs Wernicke's aphasia; deep vs surface dyslexia).
  • Language: it breaks Wernicke–Geschwind components into phonological, grammatical and semantic processes — small, widely distributed areas, not a few large ones.
  • Dyslexia: lexical vs phonetic reading routes; surface (lose lexical) vs deep (lose phonetic) dyslexia.
  • Electrophysiology: EEG/MEG and ERPs (N100, P300, N400) — fine timing, coarse location.
  • Haemodynamic/stimulation: PET (subtraction) and fMRI (BOLD) — fine location; TMS gives a 'virtual lesion'.
