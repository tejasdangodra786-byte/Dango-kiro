MET vs MI — related, not identical

Motivational Interviewing (MI)Motivational Enhancement Therapy (MET)
A clinical style/method of conversation that evokes (draws out) a person's own motivation. Not time-limited; no fixed session count. A brief, manualised treatment that applies MI and adds structured personalised assessment feedback (the “enhancement”) and a negotiated change plan. Project MATCH used 4 sessions.

In short: MET = MI style + personalised feedback + a defined structure. This handbook keeps that DNA but spreads it across 12 sessions so each therapeutic task gets proper time — useful for complex dependence, opioids, co-occurring problems, and adolescents.

1 · Introduction & Theoretical Foundation

Definition, purpose & rationale

MET is a brief, person-centred (focused on the patient as a whole person), directive (gently steered toward change) counselling approach that mobilises (sets in motion) the patient's own motivation and resources. Its aim is not to teach coping skills (that is CBT) but to resolve ambivalence (settle mixed feelings) and build commitment so the patient starts and sustains change. Rationale (underlying reasoning): motivation strongly predicts engagement and outcome, and the clinician's style can increase or decrease it.

Motivation is a changeable state, not a fixed trait

“Unmotivated” describes a moment in a conversation, not a personality. Your style can shift it.

Ambivalence is the normal core of addiction

“I want to quit and I don't” is expected, not denial (refusing to accept reality). MET works through ambivalence rather than fighting it.

Intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation

Extrinsic (outside pressure — court, family, job) gets a person through the door; intrinsic (from within — own values, health, children) sustains change. MET converts external pressure into internal reasons.

Change talk vs sustain talk

Change talk = the patient's own words for change. Sustain talk (words defending the status quo — keeping things as they are) = words against change. MET selectively evokes change talk.

Self-efficacy & responsibility

Self-efficacy (belief in one's own ability to succeed) predicts change. Responsibility and choice stay with the patient — this lowers resistance.

Empathy & the alliance

Accurate empathy (understanding and reflecting the patient's feelings) and a strong therapeutic alliance (trusting working bond) are the therapist factors most reliably linked to better substance-use outcomes.

Why confrontation backfires

Arguing, blaming, labelling (“you're an addict in denial”), and lecturing provoke psychological reactance (pushing back when we feel our freedom is threatened) — the patient defends the status quo and talks themselves into using. The “righting reflex” (the helper's urge to fix and set the person straight) is the commonest trap. In MET, if the patient argues against change, the therapist changes course — reflect, roll with it, emphasise autonomy (the person's right to choose).

2 · The MI Spirit & the Four Processes

The spirit (the attitude behind the words)

  • Partnership — working with the patient, not doing things to them.
  • Acceptance — absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, affirmation.
  • Compassion — actively promoting the patient's welfare, not your agenda.
  • Evocation — drawing motivation out of the patient (“you already have what you need”), not installing it.

The four processes (the flow of MI/MET)

1 Engaging — establish a trusting, collaborative relationship. (Phase I)
↓
2 Focusing — agree a direction/goal(s) for the conversation. (Phase I–II)
↓
3 Evoking — elicit the patient's own motivations (change talk). (Phase II)
↓
4 Planning — develop commitment and a concrete change plan. (Phase III–IV)

These processes are sequential but recursive (they build in order but you can loop back any time) — if engagement slips during planning, return to engaging. The 12-session program (§6) maps its four phases onto these processes.

3 · Core Clinical Skills — OARS and Beyond

The engine of MET. Each skill is shown as Purpose → Therapist example → Patient response → Appropriate follow-up.

OARS

O — Open questions (questions that invite more than yes/no).
T: “What's a day like when you don't use?” → P: “Restless, honestly.” → Follow-up (reflect): “Hard to settle without it.”
A — Affirmations (genuine recognition of strengths/effort — not flattery).
T: “You came even though it's uncomfortable — that takes resolve.” → P: “Didn't see it that way.” → Follow-up: “What does that say about how you handle hard things?”
R — Reflective listening (saying back the meaning you heard).
T: “You're worried this is slipping out of your control.” → P: “Exactly.” → Follow-up (deepen): “And part of you wants to grab the wheel back.”
S — Summaries (pulling together what's been said).
T: “So money, health, and your daughter all pull you toward change, while the one pull back is how it helps you relax. Right?” → P: “Yeah.” → Follow-up (key question): “Where does that leave you?”

Reflection types

TypePurposeExample
SimpleRepeat/rephrase.P: “I'm tired of it.” → T: “You've had enough.”
ComplexAdd meaning/feeling/guess.P: “I'm tired of it.” → T: “Part of you is ready for something different.”
Double-sidedHold both sides; end on the change side.“You enjoy how it relaxes you, and you're worried about your health.”
Amplified (slightly over-stated — use sparingly)Reflect sustain talk a touch too strongly so the patient self-corrects.P: “It's not that bad.” → T: “So there's no downside at all.” → P: “Well, the debt is bad…”

Beyond OARS

  • Emphasising autonomy: “It's up to you — nobody can decide this for you.”
  • Eliciting change talk: evocative questions — “Why might you want to change? How would you do it? What are the best reasons?”
  • Responding to sustain talk: reflect, emphasise autonomy, reframe, shift focus — never argue.
  • Developing discrepancy (highlighting the gap between current behaviour and goals/values).
  • Importance & confidence rulers (0–10): then “Why that number?” and “Why not lower?”.
  • Elicit–Provide–Elicit (E-P-E): ask permission & what they know → give focused info neutrally → ask what they make of it. The MET way to give feedback/advice.
  • Supporting self-efficacy: recall past successes; affirm small steps.

4 · Change Talk & Sustain Talk — DARN-CAT

Change talk is the patient's own language favouring change; evoking and reflecting it predicts behaviour change. Preparatory = DARN; mobilising = CAT.

CodeMeaningSubstance-use example
D — Desirewant, wish, like“I want to be clean for my kids.”
A — Abilitycan, could, able“I could probably cut the evening dose.”
R — Reasonsspecific if/then“If I stop smoking my chest will clear.”
N — Needmust, have to“I have to do something — the debt's out of hand.”
C — Commitmentwill, going to, promise“I'm going to see the doctor Monday.”
A — Activationready, willing, prepared“I'm ready to give it a real try.”
T — Taking stepsalready acting“I threw out my stash yesterday.”

Recognising & responding to sustain talk — without confrontation

P: “Smoking is the only thing that calms my nerves.”
T (reflect, don't challenge): “Right now it's your main way to cope with stress.” → P: “Yeah.” → T (evoke): “If you ever did decide to change, what else has helped you relax, even a little?” → patient supplies alternatives (change talk).

Core move for sustain talk: reflect → emphasise autonomy → ask an evocative question. Never “but you should…”.

5 · Assessment Before MET

A structured assessment precedes MET; in the Project MATCH model its results are fed back to the patient. Good assessment, done in MI style, is itself motivational.

Domains, questions & probes

DomainEstablishExample questions & probes
Present useSubstance(s), amount, frequency, route (how taken — oral/smoked/injected), duration, pattern.“Walk me through a typical day of using.” Probe: “How much on a heavy vs a light day?”
Last use & cravingsRecency; craving intensity/frequency; cue reactivity (how strongly reminders trigger urges).“When did you last use?” “0–10, how strong do cravings get, and what sets them off?”
TriggersPeople, places, emotions, times.“What's usually happening right before you use?”
ConsequencesPhysical, psychological, family, work, legal, financial.“What has using cost you — even small things?”
Previous attemptsWhat helped, why it stopped.“Tell me about a time you cut down. What worked, even briefly?”
Withdrawal & overdose riskSeverity, seizures/DTs (delirium tremens — a severe, dangerous alcohol-withdrawal state), OD history, injecting, mixing.“What happens to your body without it?” “Ever overdosed or passed out?”
Patient's understandingTheir own view.“What's your sense of how the drug fits your life now?”
Readiness / importance / confidenceRulers 0–10.“How important is changing, 0–10? How confident could you, 0–10?”
Strengths & supportsSkills, past successes, family, faith, work.“Who's in your corner?” “What have you succeeded at before?”
BarriersHousing, peers, pain, co-use, stigma, access.“What would make change hard?”
Goals & preferencesAbstinence (stopping completely) vs reduction; patient's own goal.“If things went well, what would be different?”

Instruments

WHO ASSIST, AUDIT (alcohol), Fagerström (nicotine), timeline follow-back, SOCRATES/readiness ruler, a user profile for feedback. SUPPLEMENTARY In Indian de-addiction settings add clinical history, physical exam, and labs (LFTs (liver function tests), viral serology for injectors).

Sample documentation (de-identified)

28-y/o male, inhaled heroin/“smack” ~0.5 g/day ×3 yrs, 20 cig/day. Last use 6 h ago. Cravings 8/10, triggered by peer group & evening boredom. One prior detox (relapsed in 3 wks). Withdrawal: body aches, insomnia; no seizures; no injecting, no reported OD. Consequences: debt, marital conflict, missed work. View: “It relaxes me; I know it's a problem but I'm not sure I can stop.” Importance 6/10, confidence 3/10. Strengths: steady job, supportive wife, stopped once. Goal: initially “cut down.” Plan: medical review for MOUD; MET to build confidence; naloxone counselling given injecting peers.
⚠ Flag immediately for medical/psychiatric care: suicidal ideation, overdose risk, severe alcohol/benzodiazepine (sedative drug class) withdrawal risk (seizure/DT history), pregnancy, acute illness.

PHASE I · Engagement & Assessment Feedback (Sessions 1–3)
Session 1 · Engagement & Rapport

1 Objectives: establish a trusting, collaborative alliance; orient the patient to MET; begin to understand their world; instil hope. 2 Rationale: engagement is the strongest early predictor of retention (staying in treatment); nothing else works without it. 3 Preparation: private space; review referral info; set a non-rushed tone; have consent & confidentiality points ready.

4 Step-by-step

  1. Warm welcome; introduce MET collaboratively.
  2. Explain confidentiality & its limits (risk to self/others, legal mandates).
  3. Open-ended invitation to tell their story.
  4. Listen with OARS; reflect far more than you ask.
  5. Elicit the patient's hopes/concerns; affirm coming in.
  6. Agree a loose agenda for the sessions ahead.

5–8 Scripts, questions, responses

T: “Thanks for coming in. Before anything else — what's been going on in your life lately, in your own words?”
P: “Honestly, I'm only here because my wife pushed me.”
T (reflect + autonomy + evoke): “You came partly for her — and whatever we do here is still your choice. While you're here, what would make this time worth it for you?”
P: “I suppose I'm tired of the arguments.”

Questions to ask: “What's a typical day like?” · “What are the good things and the less-good things about why you're here?” · “What are you hoping might come of this?”

9 Change/sustain talk

“I'm tired of the fighting.” vs “I don't really have a problem.” — reflect both; don't correct sustain talk yet.

10 Worksheet

#1 My Substance-Use Story (collaborative, non-judgemental).

11 Mistakes

  • Rushing to assessment/forms before rapport.
  • Taking the family's side against the patient.
  • Correcting denial; filling silences instead of reflecting.

12 Homework

Optional: notice one thing about use they'd want different.

13 Documentation

Engagement quality, patient's stated reason for attending, early change/sustain talk, agreed agenda, risk screen.

14 Move on when…

A working alliance exists and the patient is willing to continue. If guarded, spend another session engaging — do not force feedback.

Session 2 · Personalised Assessment Feedback

1 Objectives: deliver structured, personalised assessment feedback in MI style (the “enhancement” of MET); let the patient draw their own conclusions. 2 Rationale: objective feedback given without argument raises intrinsic motivation while protecting autonomy. 3 Preparation: compile a one-page feedback sheet — use levels vs norms (compared with others), risk markers, consequences; have rulers ready.

4 Step-by-step (Elicit–Provide–Elicit)

  1. Ask permission to share results.
  2. Elicit what the patient already knows/expects.
  3. Provide one finding at a time, neutrally; pause.
  4. Reflect their reaction; avoid defending the data.
  5. Summarise; ask “What do you make of all this?”

5–8 Scripts

T: “Would it be okay if I shared what the assessment showed, then you tell me what fits and what doesn't?”
P: “I guess.”
T (provide, neutral): “Your reported use puts you in the high-risk range compared with most men your age. How does that land?”
P: “I don't use more than my friends.”
T (double-sided): “So among your circle this feels normal — and seeing it in the high-risk range is a surprise.”
P: “The liver result did scare me a bit.”

9 Change/sustain talk

“The liver thing scared me.” vs “Everyone I know uses.”

10 Worksheet

#11 How Use Affects My Life; #6/#7 Rulers.

11 Mistakes

  • Lecturing from the sheet; arguing with denial.
  • Dumping all findings at once.
  • Pushing a goal before the patient is ready.

12 Homework

Reflect on which feedback felt most relevant.

13 Documentation

Feedback delivered, patient reactions, change talk elicited, importance/confidence.

14 Move on when…

The patient has engaged with the feedback and voiced some concern. If dismissive, stay with engagement/feedback.

Session 3 · Developing Discrepancy & Mapping Readiness

1 Objectives: develop discrepancy between current use and the patient's goals/values; map importance, confidence & readiness; consolidate focus. 2 Rationale: discrepancy felt in the patient's own terms is the motor of change. 3 Preparation: review feedback reactions; prepare ruler & “why this number” prompts.

4 Step-by-step

  1. Review the week & any homework.
  2. Use importance ruler → “why that number?” → “why not lower?”.
  3. Use confidence ruler the same way.
  4. Reflect/summarise all change talk.
  5. Gently hold up the gap: current use vs stated hopes.

5–8 Scripts

T: “On 0–10, how important is changing right now?” → P: “Maybe a 6.” → T: “Why a 6 and not a 3?” → P: “Because my health scares me and my wife's at her limit.” → T: “Your health and your marriage are pulling you. What would move it from a 6 to an 8?”

9 Change/sustain talk

Everything after “why not lower?” is usually change talk — reflect and amplify.

10 Worksheet

#6 Importance Ruler, #7 Confidence Ruler, #8 Why This Number, #9 Why Not Lower.

11 Mistakes

  • Creating discrepancy by lecturing (patient defends use).
  • Treating ruler numbers as scores/diagnoses.

12 Homework

Notice moments in the week when use clashed with something they care about.

13 Documentation

Importance/confidence, discrepancy themes, stage of change, change talk.

14 Move on to Phase II when…

The patient voices meaningful discrepancy and some desire/reason to change. If still precontemplative, keep engaging & evoking concern.

PHASE II · Evoking Motivation (Sessions 4–6)
Session 4 · Exploring Ambivalence & Decisional Balance

1 Objectives: explore the real benefits and costs of using and of changing; let the balance tip in the patient's own words. 2 Rationale: honouring the perceived benefits first lowers defensiveness and lets costs register. 3 Preparation: decisional-balance worksheet; resist the urge to argue the “pro-change” side.

4 Step-by-step

  1. Review week.
  2. Explore “good things / things you'd miss” about use.
  3. Explore “less-good things” about use.
  4. Explore benefits & worries about changing.
  5. Summarise all four quadrants; ask “Where does this leave you?”.

5–8 Scripts

T: “What are the good things about using — the things you'd miss?” → P: “It calms me after work.” → T: “It helps you unwind. And the not-so-good side?” → P: “I'm broke, and we fight about it.” → T (key question): “Given both sides, where does that leave you?”
Sustain talk, no argument: P: “I can stop any time I want.” → T: “You feel in control — and it's completely your call. If you did test that, what might you try first?”

9 Change/sustain talk

Log benefits of change & costs of using = change talk; keep eliciting DARN.

10 Worksheet

#3 Benefits & Costs of Continuing, #4 Benefits & Costs of Changing, #5 Decisional Balance.

11 Mistakes

  • Only listing cons of use (ignoring real benefits).
  • Taking the pro-change side — the patient then argues for use.

12 Homework

Complete the decisional balance at home.

13 Documentation

Four-quadrant summary, shift in balance, change talk.

14 Move on when…

Costs of using and benefits of change feature prominently in the patient's speech.

Session 5 · Personal Values & Developing Discrepancy

1 Objectives: connect change to the patient's core values and life goals; deepen discrepancy; strengthen intrinsic motivation. 2 Rationale: change rooted in values (not fear or pressure) is more durable. 3 Preparation: values worksheet; be ready to let silences work.

4 Step-by-step

  1. Elicit top values (“What matters most to you — as a person, parent, worker?”).
  2. Explore how the patient wants to be seen/remembered.
  3. Gently explore how use fits (or clashes) with those values.
  4. Reflect the discrepancy; let them name it.

5–8 Scripts

T: “You've said being a good father is everything to you. How does using fit with the father you want to be?” → P: “It doesn't. That's not who I want to be.” → T: “There's a real gap between how you're living and who you want to be — and you want to close it.”

9 Change/sustain talk

Value-based statements are powerful change talk (Need/Reasons). Amplify.

10 Worksheet

#10 My Personal Values & Goals, #12 Strengths & Previous Successes.

11 Mistakes

  • Imposing your values; moralising.
  • Rushing past emotion to “so you'll quit, then.”

12 Homework

Write one value and one example of use clashing with it.

13 Documentation

Core values, discrepancy themes, change talk.

14 Move on when…

The patient articulates a values-based reason for change.

Session 6 · Building Confidence & Self-Efficacy

1 Objectives: raise confidence that change is possible; address the “I want to but I can't” gap; surface strengths, supports & past successes. 2 Rationale: importance and confidence are independent — high importance + low confidence needs efficacy-building, not more reasons. 3 Preparation: confidence ruler; strengths worksheet; brief solution-focused questions.

4 Step-by-step

  1. Confidence ruler → “why that number?” → “why not lower?”.
  2. Review past successes (any domain) & how they were achieved.
  3. Elicit strengths, skills & supports.
  4. Ask “What would raise your confidence by one point?”.
  5. Affirm; summarise ability change talk.

5–8 Scripts

T: “Confidence 0–10 that you could change if you decided?” → P: “About a 3.” → T: “Why a 3 and not a 0?” → P: “I did stop for three weeks once.” → T (affirm + build): “You've already proven you can — that's a strength. What would nudge the 3 toward a 5?”

9 Change/sustain talk

Target Ability change talk (“I could…”, “I managed before…”).

10 Worksheet

#7 Confidence Ruler, #12 Strengths, #15 Coping & Support (preview).

11 Mistakes

  • Reassuring hollowly (“you'll be fine”) instead of eliciting evidence.
  • Jumping to planning before confidence is adequate.

12 Homework

List three personal strengths and one past success.

13 Documentation

Confidence rating & change, strengths, ability change talk.

14 Move on to Phase III when…

Importance and confidence are both reasonably present and the patient signals readiness (“I need to do something”).

PHASE III · Planning & Action (Sessions 7–9)
Session 7 · Negotiating the Change Plan

1 Objectives: confirm readiness & the patient's chosen goal; co-create a concrete, patient-authored change plan. 2 Rationale: a plan the patient writes carries commitment; a prescribed plan does not. 3 Preparation: change-plan & goals worksheets; know local treatment options.

4 Step-by-step

  1. Recap motivation (“Last time you said… where are you now?”).
  2. Clarify the goal in the patient's words (abstinence or reduction).
  3. Offer a menu of options; the patient chooses.
  4. Draft steps: who/what/when.
  5. Elicit commitment language; summarise the plan back.

5–8 Scripts

T (key question): “Given all we've discussed, what do you think you'll do?” → P: “I want to stop the smack. I'll ask the doctor about the medicine.” → T (elicit plan): “Great — what's the very first step this week: the appointment, telling your wife, or changing your evenings?”

9 Change/sustain talk

Target Commitment/Activation/Taking steps; reflect and strengthen.

10 Worksheet

#16 My Change Plan, #17 My First Three Steps, ★#25 Short-term & ★#26 Long-term Goals.

11 Mistakes

  • Writing the plan for the patient.
  • Over-ambitious goals that set up failure.

12 Homework

Carry out step one; bring the plan next time.

13 Documentation

Goal, plan verbatim, commitment strength.

14 Move on when…

A written, patient-owned plan with a concrete first step exists.

Session 8 · Triggers, High-Risk Situations & Coping

1 Objectives: identify triggers (cues that set off use) and high-risk situations; build a personalised coping toolkit; begin craving management. 2 Rationale: most lapses occur in predictable high-risk situations; planning for them protects the change. 3 Preparation: triggers & coping worksheets; brief urge-surfing/“play-the-tape” techniques.

4 Step-by-step

  1. Map triggers (people/places/feelings/times) using a recent episode.
  2. Rank the top high-risk situations.
  3. Brainstorm coping options per trigger; the patient selects.

  5. Introduce craving skills: urge-surfing (riding out a craving like a wave until it passes), “play the tape forward” (imagining the full consequences of using).

Session 11 · Relapse Prevention & Learning from Lapses

1 Objectives: strengthen relapse-prevention; distinguish a lapse from a relapse; create an emergency “if-then” plan; de-shame slips. 2 Rationale: treating a lapse (a single slip) as total failure (the “abstinence-violation effect” (all-or-nothing reaction that turns one slip into a full relapse)) is a key relapse driver — reframing prevents it. 3 Preparation: relapse-prevention & lapse worksheets.

4 Step-by-step

  1. Identify remaining high-risk situations & early warning signs.
  2. Build “if-then” coping plans for each.
  3. Normalise lapses; separate lapse from relapse.
  4. Create an emergency plan (who to call, what to do).
  5. Rehearse a difficult scenario.

5–8 Scripts

T (after a lapse): “You used once last week and then stopped yourself. That slip doesn't erase three good weeks — what helped you get back on track?” → P: “I called my brother instead of my old friends.” → T: “Reaching for support at the right moment — that's exactly how a slip stays a slip.”

7 · MET with Adolescents & Young Adults

ADOLESCENT Based on Naar-King & Suarez, Motivational Interviewing with Adolescents and Young Adults. The spirit and skills are the same; the delivery adapts to developmental stage.

Key adaptations

  • Autonomy is paramount: adolescents are developmentally primed to resist authority — lean hard on choice and collaboration; avoid the “expert/parent” stance.
  • Engagement first & longer: spend more time building rapport; use the young person's own language and interests.
  • Present-focused motivation: teens respond less to distant health risks and more to now — money, freedom, relationships, image, sport, school.
  • Shorter, more frequent, flexible contacts; be comfortable with silence and brevity.
  • Developmentally-tuned reflections and simpler language; be genuine, not “trying to be cool”.
  • Family/caregiver involvement balanced with the adolescent's confidentiality — clarify limits early.
  • Strengths & identity: affirm emerging identity and competence; connect change to who they want to become.
  • Context: school, peers, social media, co-occurring mental-health issues (anxiety, depression, trauma, ADHD) are central.
Sample adolescent dialogue.
T: “It's totally your call what you do with weed — I'm not here to lecture you. I'm just curious: what do you like about it, and is there anything about it you're not so keen on?”
P (17 y): “It chills me out. But I'm skint and I keep missing football.”
T (reflect what matters to them): “So it relaxes you — and it's eating your money and your football, which you love.”
P: “Yeah… football's the one thing I'd actually hate to lose.”
⚠ With under-18s: clarify confidentiality limits with the young person and caregivers; assess self-harm/suicide risk, exploitation, and safeguarding; coordinate with family and services per local child-protection law.

Worksheets for youth: prefer #10 Values & Goals, #6/#7 Rulers, ★#25 Short-term goals, #13 Triggers — kept short, concrete, present-focused.

9 · Common Clinical Challenges

For each: a short dialogue showing empathy, reflective listening, autonomy support & follow-up. The core move is almost always reflect → affirm autonomy → evoke, don't argue.

“I don't have a problem.”
T: “From where you stand, this isn't a problem — and I'm not here to label you. What made others feel it might be?” → P: “My wife keeps on about money.” → T: “So money is one thing even you can see is tight.”
“I can stop whenever I want.”
T: “You feel in control — and it really is your choice. If you ever did test that, what might you try first?”
“My family forced me to come.”
T: “You're here for them, not because you've decided anything — that's honest. While you're here, what would make this hour worth it for you?”
“Substance use is the only thing that helps me.”
T: “Right now it's your main way to cope. What else has ever given you even a bit of that relief?”
“I don't want to quit.”
T: “You're not looking to quit, and I won't push that. Is there anything about your use you would want different — even a little?”
“I tried before and failed.”
T: “That stung. And you did stay clean three weeks — that's not nothing. What worked, even for a while?”
“I'll think about it.”
T: “It's a big decision and you want to weigh it. What might you turn over this week?”
Missed sessions.
T (warm, no scolding): “Good to see you back — I'm glad you came. What's been happening?” Explore barriers collaboratively.
Minimal participation.
T: “You're quiet today — that's okay. Would it help to sit with just one thing, or would you rather we stop early?” Offer control.
Anger / defensiveness.
T: “I've touched a nerve, and I'm sorry — this is your call entirely. What would be helpful to talk about?” Reflect feeling, drop the agenda, restore autonomy.
Conflicting family expectations.
T: “Your family wants one thing and you're not sure you want the same — both are real. What do you want for yourself?” Avoid siding against the patient; consider a separate family session.
A lapse after making a plan.
T: “You slipped once then stopped yourself — that recovery matters. Slips are common and don't erase progress. What can we learn?” → Worksheet #19.

13 · Evidence, Limitations & Ethical Practice

Evidence (summary)

MET/MI have a substantial evidence base. In Project MATCH, 4-session MET gave drinking outcomes broadly comparable to longer CBT and Twelve-Step Facilitation — striking for fewer sessions. Reviews and the Cochrane literature on MI/MET report benefits for engagement, retention (staying in treatment) and reduced use, though effect sizes vary by substance, population, setting & comparison, and some effects are modest or short-lived without follow-up. Prioritise the original Project MATCH reports, Miller & Rollnick, Naar-King & Suarez, and Cochrane reviews as verifiable sources; figures here are summarised, not exact statistics.

MET can: improve engagement/retention, strengthen motivation & commitment, reduce use for many, serve as a low-intensity entry point. MET cannot: replace detox, pharmacotherapy or management of severe dependence; guarantee abstinence; or “cure” addiction through motivation alone.

Limitations

  • Outcomes vary across substances (e.g., stronger engagement evidence for alcohol/cannabis than some stimulant outcomes) and populations.
  • Effects depend heavily on context and continuing care/follow-up.
  • Motivation is necessary but not sufficient — pair with medical treatment & skills where indicated.

Ethical practice

  • Informed consent & confidentiality — explain purpose, voluntariness & limits (risk, legal mandates; for minors, clarify with youth & caregivers).
  • Respect for autonomy — patient chooses goals; avoid coercion/confrontation.
  • Non-stigmatising language — “a person who uses heroin”, not “addict/junkie”; describe behaviour, not identity.
  • Cultural sensitivity — adapt to family structure, religion, gender & local norms.
  • Appropriate referral & multidisciplinary care — coordinate with physicians, psychiatry, social work & community resources.
  • Do not overstate efficacy or claim motivation alone resolves dependence.

★ One-Page Quick-Reference Guide

12-session program at a glance

  • Phase I (S1–3): engage → feedback → discrepancy.
  • Phase II (S4–6): decisional balance → values → confidence.
  • Phase III (S7–9): change plan → triggers/coping → support/meds.
  • Phase IV (S10–12): review → relapse-prevention → consolidation/discharge (+boosters 1/3/6/12 mo).

Four MI processes

Engaging → Focusing → Evoking → Planning (loop back any time).

OARS

Open questions · Affirmations · Reflections · Summaries

Golden rules

  • Evoke, don't install. Reflect, don't argue.
  • Emphasise autonomy & choice.
  • If the patient defends use, change your approach.
  • Ask permission before advice (E-P-E).

Change talk — DARN-CAT

Preparatory: Desire · Ability · Reasons · Need
Mobilising: Commitment · Activation · Taking steps

Rulers (0–10)

Importance & Confidence, then “Why that number?” / “Why not lower?” (never “why not higher?”).

Responding to sustain talk

Reflect → emphasise autonomy → evocative question. Add: reframe, shift focus, come alongside.

Change planning

Goal (patient's words) → reasons → steps → supports → how you'll know it's working → obstacles & “if-then” responses → short-term goals building toward long-term goals.

⚠ Always: screen OD/withdrawal/suicide risk; coordinate medical care & (opioids) naloxone + MOUD.

Glossary of Difficult Terms

  • Abstinence — stopping use completely.
  • Abstinence-violation effect — the all-or-nothing reaction that turns one slip into a full relapse.
  • Adherence — sticking to the agreed treatment.
  • Ambivalence — mixed feelings; wanting and not wanting change at once.
  • Amotivation — loss of drive/initiative.
  • Autonomy — a person's right to make their own choices.
  • Cue reactivity — how strongly reminders trigger urges.
  • Decisional balance — weighing pros & cons of using vs changing.
  • Delirium tremens (DTs) — a severe, dangerous alcohol-withdrawal state.
  • Detoxification (detox) — medically-supervised withdrawal.
  • Discord — tension/strain in the therapeutic relationship.
  • Discrepancy — the gap between current behaviour and goals/values.
  • Empathy — understanding and reflecting another's feelings.
  • Evocation — drawing motivation out of the patient.
  • Extrinsic motivation — motivation from outside pressure.
  • Euphoria — intense pleasure/“high”.
  • Intrinsic motivation — motivation from within (own values).
  • MOUD — medications for opioid use disorder (e.g., buprenorphine, methadone).
  • Naloxone — an antidote that reverses opioid overdose.
  • NRT — nicotine replacement therapy (patch, gum, etc.).
  • OCR — optical character recognition (turning scanned images into text).
  • Overdose — a dangerous excess dose.
  • Pharmacotherapy — treatment with medicines.
  • Psychological reactance — pushing back when freedom feels threatened.
  • Relapse / lapse — a fuller return to use / a single slip.
  • Retention — staying in treatment.
  • Righting reflex — the helper's urge to fix/correct the person.
  • Self-efficacy — belief in one's own ability to succeed.
  • Sustain talk — the patient's words defending the status quo.
  • Tolerance — the body needing more drug for the same effect.
  • Urge-surfing — riding out a craving until it passes.
