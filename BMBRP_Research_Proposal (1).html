Section 02

Background and Introduction

Substance Dependence in India

Substance use disorders (SUDs) represent one of the most prevalent, costly, and treatment-resistant mental health conditions in India. According to the National Drug Dependence Treatment Centre (NDDTC) and the Ministry of Social Justice and Empowerment (2019), an estimated 14.6% of the Indian population (approximately 160 million people) uses alcohol, 3.1 million use opioids, and 2.3 million use cannabis regularly. Madhya Pradesh, the state in which the proposed study is situated, consistently reports high rates of opioid use — particularly doda (opium husk), heroin, and pharmaceutical opioids — alongside high alcohol dependence prevalence.

Substance dependence is not merely a problem of physiological addiction. It involves profound disruptions in impulsive responding, affective regulation, craving management, and coping capacity — the psychological capacities that determine whether a person remains abstinent after detoxification. National relapse data consistently shows rates of 60–80% within the first three months post-discharge, driven primarily by craving, negative emotional states, and the absence of psychological skills to manage triggers without substance use.

The Problem with Treatment As Usual

The standard treatment model at district-level de-addiction centres in India follows a predominantly biomedical framework: medically supervised detoxification, pharmacological management of withdrawal symptoms and craving, and brief psychoeducation. While this protocol effectively manages the acute withdrawal phase, it addresses very little of the psychological substrate that drives relapse. Patients are discharged having achieved biological sobriety but without the psychological tools to navigate the craving, emotional dysregulation, and environmental triggers that characterise early recovery.

⚠️ The critical gap: TAU produces sobriety. It does not produce the psychological skills required to maintain sobriety in the face of real-world triggers, craving, and emotional distress.

What is Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention?

Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention (MBRP), developed by Bowen, Chawla, and Marlatt (2011) at the University of Washington, is the most evidence-supported psychosocial intervention designed specifically for this gap. MBRP integrates mindfulness meditation practices from MBSR (Kabat-Zinn, 1990) and MBCT (Segal, Williams, and Teasdale, 2002) with the cognitive-behavioural Relapse Prevention framework of Marlatt and Gordon (1985). The core therapeutic mechanism is teaching patients to observe craving and emotional distress mindfully — as transient mental events — without automatically acting on them.

The brief adapted version (B-MBRP) condenses this into 6 structured individual sessions suitable for inpatient delivery, making it feasible within the typical 3–5 week IPD admission window of Indian district hospitals.

Evidence for MBRP

Bowen et al. (2014) — JAMA Psychiatry
N=286 three-arm RCT. MBRP participants at 12-month follow-up reported significantly fewer days of substance use (31%) and significantly decreased heavy drinking compared to standard Relapse Prevention and TAU. The landmark RCT establishing MBRP superiority.
Riya, Kumar & Chandel (2025) — Indian J Psychiatry
Quasi-experimental pre-post study, N=50, alcohol dependence, India. MBRP + usual care significantly improved emotion regulation (DERS-18) and reduced alcohol craving (ACQ-SF-R) compared to usual care alone. Most directly comparable Indian study.
Solomon et al. (2024) — Indian J Physiol Pharmacol
RCT, N=100, Indian de-addiction centre. MBSR reduced perceived stress from mean 36.0 to 18.9 in experimental group vs 36.9 to 33.4 in control. Uses only PSS — no craving, impulsivity, or mindfulness outcomes measured.
Brief MBI — OUD Inpatient (2023) — PMC
6-week brief mindfulness in underserved inpatient OUD patients significantly decreased perceived stress, drug craving, and emotional regulation difficulties post-intervention. Authors recommended a large-scale RCT with control group as next step — exactly what this study provides.

Section 03

Research Gap

A systematic review of the Indian and international literature reveals the following specific gaps that this study directly addresses:

1
No Indian study has evaluated MBRP specifically in a district-level de-addiction IPD The two Indian studies identified (Solomon et al., 2024; Riya et al., 2025) used MBSR rather than MBRP, and neither was conducted at a district government hospital with a mixed opioid-alcohol dependence population. MBRP, being specifically designed for addictive behaviours, is theoretically and mechanistically more appropriate for this population than MBSR.
2
No Indian study has used FFMQ + BIS-11 + craving measure together as a multi-domain outcome battery Existing Indian studies have used either stress alone (PSS) or craving alone (ACQ-SF-R). None has simultaneously measured the five domains most relevant to relapse risk — mindfulness capacity, impulsivity, craving, depression, and stress — which is essential for understanding the full spectrum of B-MBRP's effects.
3
No Indian study with a waitlist control design in this population and setting Without a control condition, it is impossible to determine whether observed changes are due to the mindfulness intervention or to the natural improvement that occurs with any inpatient admission (detoxification, rest, nutrition, structured environment). A waitlist control is the minimum methodological standard to attribute change to the specific intervention.
4
No Indian study has included a post-discharge follow-up in a mindfulness + substance dependence design All existing Indian MBI studies measure outcomes only at the end of the inpatient stay. Whether mindfulness skills are maintained and whether they translate to reduced relapse in the first 4–6 weeks post-discharge — the highest-risk period — has not been examined in an Indian sample.
5
Absence of LMIC-specific data for brief inpatient MBRP adaptations The 2024 Brazilian feasibility study noted that "there is a dearth of studies examining MBRP's feasibility and efficacy in low- and middle-income countries." India, as a LMIC with a distinct linguistic, cultural, and healthcare-infrastructure context, requires its own evidence base — and district-level hospitals represent the majority of India's de-addiction treatment capacity.
📌 Research Gap Statement: No published study has evaluated a brief, individually-delivered MBRP-adapted intervention with a waitlist control design, a multi-domain outcome battery (FFMQ + BIS-11 + craving + PHQ-9 + PSS), and a post-discharge follow-up in a district-level Indian de-addiction IPD — making this study uniquely positioned to fill a specific, documented, and clinically important gap.

Section 04

Rationale and Justification

Why Mindfulness for Substance Dependence?

Impulsive reactivity to craving and negative affect is the primary proximal mechanism of relapse. Mindfulness directly targets this mechanism by training the capacity to observe craving as a transient mental event rather than an automatic trigger for use. Neurobiologically, mindfulness practice strengthens prefrontal cortex regulation over amygdala reactivity — the precise neural deficit that chronic substance use produces.

Why MBRP Specifically over MBSR or MBCT?

MBSR was designed for stress reduction in medical populations. MBCT was designed for depressive relapse prevention. MBRP was specifically designed for substance use disorder relapse prevention, incorporating trigger mapping, urge surfing, SOBER breathing space, and permission-giving thought defusion — all directly targeted at the addictive behaviour cycle. It is the most appropriate protocol for this population.

Why a Brief Adapted Protocol (6 sessions)?

The full MBRP protocol (8 weekly sessions) was developed for outpatient aftercare. Indian district hospital admissions typically last 3–5 weeks. A 6-session brief inpatient adaptation (one session every 3–4 days) is feasible within this window, can be delivered individually to accommodate variable discharge dates, and is framed appropriately as a pilot study. Evidence from brief inpatient MBI studies supports efficacy even with condensed formats.

Why This Setting?

District hospital de-addiction IPDs represent the primary treatment access point for substance-dependent patients in semi-urban and rural India. Research conducted at this level generates findings that are directly applicable to the healthcare context where the majority of Indian patients actually receive care — unlike tertiary centre studies (NIMHANS, AIIMS) whose findings may not transfer to district-level resource constraints.

Why a Waitlist Control Rather Than No Control?

A single-group pre-post design cannot separate the effect of the intervention from the natural improvement that occurs during any inpatient admission. A waitlist control — where the control group receives TAU and is measured at the same time points — allows confident attribution of observed change specifically to B-MBRP. This is the strongest feasible design in an IPD context where a no-treatment control would be unethical.

Why Add a 4-Week Follow-Up?

The first 4 weeks post-discharge are the highest-risk period for relapse. Measuring outcomes only at discharge (end of IPD stay) cannot determine whether mindfulness skills are retained and applied in the real world. A brief follow-up assessment (phone or OPD) at 4–6 weeks adds the single most clinically meaningful data point — whether the intervention effects are maintained when patients face real-world triggers.

Section 05

Objectives

Primary Objectives

PO1
To evaluate the efficacy of B-MBRP on craving in substance-dependent patients, comparing experimental (B-MBRP + TAU) versus waitlist control (TAU only) groups at post-assessment
PO2
To evaluate the efficacy of B-MBRP on impulsivity (total and subscale scores) in substance-dependent patients at post-assessment, comparing the two groups
PO3
To evaluate the efficacy of B-MBRP on mindfulness capacity (FFMQ total and subscale scores), comparing experimental and waitlist control groups at post-assessment

Secondary Objectives

SO1
To examine the effect of B-MBRP on depression severity (PHQ-9) and perceived stress (PSS-10) at post-assessment
SO2
To assess maintenance of intervention effects at a 4-week post-discharge follow-up in the experimental group on primary outcome measures
SO3
To identify baseline predictors of treatment response — whether initial mindfulness, impulsivity, or craving severity predicts degree of change following B-MBRP
SO4
To examine whether B-MBRP effects differ by substance type (alcohol vs. opioid dependence) as an exploratory secondary comparison
SO5
To assess participant acceptability and engagement with the B-MBRP protocol in this population — session attendance, home practice compliance, and subjective feedback

Section 06

Hypotheses

All primary hypotheses are directional (one-tailed for primary outcomes where direction is clearly predicted by theory and prior evidence). Exploratory hypotheses are non-directional.

Primary Hypotheses (H1–H3)

H1
Craving: Substance-dependent patients who receive B-MBRP + TAU will show a significantly greater reduction in craving scores (Penn Alcohol Craving Scale / OCDUS) at post-assessment compared to those who receive TAU alone.
Directional: Experimental < Control at post · Mechanism: Urge surfing and mindful observation of craving reduce craving intensity and urgency
H2
Impulsivity: Patients in the experimental group will show significantly greater reduction in BIS-11 total impulsivity scores, specifically on the Motor Impulsivity and Non-planning Impulsivity subscales, at post-assessment compared to the waitlist control group.
Directional: Experimental < Control at post · Mechanism: SOBER breathing space and mindful responding train response inhibition and delay of gratification
H3
Mindfulness: Patients in the experimental group will show significantly greater increase in FFMQ total mindfulness scores at post-assessment, particularly on the Non-reactivity and Acting with Awareness subscales, compared to the waitlist control group.
Directional: Experimental > Control at post · Mechanism: Direct mindfulness skill training through formal practice and SOBER exercises

Secondary Hypotheses (H4–H6)

H4
Depression and Stress: The experimental group will show significantly greater reduction in PHQ-9 depression scores and PSS-10 perceived stress scores at post-assessment compared to the waitlist control group.
Directional: Experimental < Control at post
H5
Follow-up Maintenance: Gains on FFMQ, BIS-11, and craving scores achieved by the experimental group at post-assessment will be maintained at the 4-week follow-up assessment, with no significant deterioration from post to follow-up.
Non-directional: Tests whether effects are durable beyond the inpatient stay
EXP
Exploratory — Substance Type: The magnitude of B-MBRP effects (craving reduction, impulsivity reduction, mindfulness increase) will not differ significantly between opioid-dependent and alcohol-dependent patients, supporting the transdiagnostic applicability of MBRP.
Exploratory: No directional prediction — genuinely open question in the Indian literature

Null Hypotheses (for formal testing)

H0
There will be no significant difference between the experimental (B-MBRP + TAU) and waitlist control (TAU only) groups on craving, impulsivity, or mindfulness scores at post-assessment after controlling for pre-assessment scores.

Section 07

Study Design

Design Classification

Pilot Quasi-Experimental Study with Waitlist Control — Pre-Test Post-Test Design with Follow-Up

This is a quasi-experimental design (not a true RCT) because allocation is by admission cohort rather than individual randomisation. This is standard, accepted, and published for inpatient intervention research where randomising individual patients to different conditions within the same ward creates contamination risk.

Design Schematic

GroupPre-Assessment (T1)Weeks 1–3Post-Assessment (T2)Follow-Up (T3)
Experimental
Cohort 2 — B-MBRP + TAU		 All tools administered
Day 7–10 post-admission		 6 B-MBRP sessions (every 3–4 days) + routine TAU All tools re-administered within 3 days of Session 6 FFMQ + BIS-11 + Craving + PHQ-9 at 4–6 weeks post-discharge
Waitlist Control
Cohort 1 — TAU only		 All tools administered
Day 7–10 post-admission		 Routine TAU only (detox + pharmacotherapy + standard psychoeducation) All tools re-administered at matched time point (Day 28–30) Offered B-MBRP as ethical obligation after data collection

Why Cohort-Based Allocation?

In an inpatient ward where patients are housed together, individual randomisation creates contamination — patients in the experimental group discussing sessions with control-group patients, or the therapeutic atmosphere affecting the control group indirectly. Cohort allocation (first admission batch = control, next batch = experimental) prevents this contamination while still providing a matched comparison group. This approach has been used in all published inpatient MBRP studies.

❌ Why Not Single-Group Pre-Post
Cannot determine whether change is due to B-MBRP or to natural improvement from detoxification, rest, and structured ward environment. Cannot publish findings as evidence of intervention efficacy.
✅ Why Waitlist Control
Both groups experience the inpatient environment; therefore any difference between them at post-assessment can be attributed specifically to B-MBRP. Ethical — control group is not denied treatment, just the mindfulness add-on, and receives it afterwards.

Section 08

Sample, Setting, and Sampling

Setting

Study Site

De-addiction Inpatient Department (IPD) / OPD, District Hospital, Guna, Madhya Pradesh

Assessment sessions conducted in a quiet consultation room or designated therapy space within the ward. B-MBRP sessions conducted in the same space — private, undisturbed, with the researcher and patient only. The inpatient setting is ideal: patients are available, medically stable post-detox, not acutely intoxicated, and can be accessed across multiple sessions without requiring them to travel.

Sample Size and Justification

For a pilot quasi-experimental study with two groups and pre-post measurement, a minimum of N = 20 per group (total N = 40) is the standard recommendation in MBRP intervention pilot literature. This provides adequate power to detect medium-to-large effect sizes (Cohen's d ≥ 0.60), which existing MBRP trials consistently report for craving (d=0.61), impulsivity (d=0.48), and mindfulness (d=0.72). To account for 20–25% attrition (LAMA, medical complications, premature discharge), recruit 25 per group, targeting a final analysable sample of 20 per group (total N=40).

Recruitment Target: N = 50 → Analysable Sample: N = 40

Recruit 25 per cohort. Expect 4–5 dropouts per cohort due to LAMA (Leave Against Medical Advice), medical deterioration, or refusal to continue after consent. Final analysable sample of 20 per group is sufficient for a pilot study with exploratory-to-confirmatory framing.

Sampling Method

Consecutive purposive sampling — all eligible patients admitted to the de-addiction IPD during the recruitment period are approached. This is the standard and appropriate method for clinical inpatient research. Random sampling from an inpatient ward is not feasible as the total eligible population at any one time is small.

Inclusion Criteria

  • ICD-10 confirmed diagnosis of substance dependence — alcohol (F10.2x) or opioid (F11.2x) — confirmed by the treating psychiatrist
  • Age 18–55 years
  • Minimum 7 days post-admission at the time of pre-assessment (past acute withdrawal phase, medically stable)
  • Minimum 5th standard education or sufficient Hindi literacy to complete self-report tools
  • Expected to remain admitted for at least 3 weeks following pre-assessment (sufficient time for all 6 sessions)
  • Willing and able to provide written informed consent
  • Reachable by phone for follow-up assessment (has own phone or a family member's contact)

Exclusion Criteria

  • Comorbid psychotic disorder (F20–F29) — active psychosis would confound all self-report measures and make mindfulness training unsafe
  • Active suicidal ideation or behaviour at pre-assessment — requires psychiatric management, not psychological intervention
  • Clinically evident intellectual disability — precludes meaningful engagement with the B-MBRP protocol
  • Severe medical comorbidity preventing participation in sessions (e.g., Wernicke's encephalopathy, acute hepatic failure)
  • Prior formal mindfulness training (MBSR, MBCT, yoga-based mindfulness for more than 4 weeks) — ceiling effect on FFMQ baseline
  • Polysubstance dependence where neither alcohol nor opioids is the primary substance of dependence
  • Delirium or severe cognitive impairment at time of pre-assessment
  • Does not speak or understand Hindi (required for session delivery and all tools)

Section 09

Assessment Tools — In Detail

Five standardised, validated assessment tools are used alongside a self-designed sociodemographic and clinical proforma. Each tool is described below with its structure, scoring, psychometric properties, interpretation, and specific relevance to this study.

🧘
Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire
FFMQ — Baer et al. (2006) · 39 items · 5-point Likert scale
PRE + POST + FOLLOW-UP

What It Measures

FFMQ measures five distinct facets of mindfulness as a trait or dispositional quality. It is derived from a factor analysis across five existing mindfulness scales and is the most widely validated multidimensional mindfulness measure in clinical research.

Observing
8 items — noticing internal/external experiences, sensations, thoughts, feelings without reacting
Describing
8 items — ability to label internal experiences with words
Acting with Awareness
8 items — attending to present activity vs. autopilot behaviour — MOST relevant to relapse
Non-judging of Inner Experience
8 items — refraining from evaluating thoughts and feelings as good or bad
Non-reactivity to Inner Experience
7 items — allowing thoughts and feelings to come and go without being swept away — MOST relevant to craving
Total Score Range
39–195 · Higher = greater mindfulness capacity

Scoring

Items rated 1 (never or very rarely true) to 5 (very often or always true). Some items are reverse-scored. Subscale scores and total score are computed by summing relevant items. No established cut-offs for clinical populations — used as a continuous variable for change measurement.

Reliability and Validity

Cronbach's alpha for total scale: 0.91. Subscale alphas: 0.75–0.91. Validated in Indian samples (Keng et al., 2011; used in Indian clinical psychology research since 2015). Sensitive to change following mindfulness interventions — FFMQ is the primary self-report outcome in all published MBRP trials.

Why Used in This Study

FFMQ is your primary intervention-specific outcome. B-MBRP directly trains mindfulness capacity. If the intervention works, FFMQ Non-reactivity and Acting with Awareness subscales should show the largest and most statistically significant change. If FFMQ does not change while other outcomes do, this suggests the mechanism of change may not be mindfulness per se — an important secondary finding.

Expected direction: FFMQ total and Non-reactivity subscale scores increase significantly in experimental group relative to control at post-assessment.
⚡
Barratt Impulsiveness Scale — 11
BIS-11 — Patton, Stanford & Barratt (1995) · 30 items · 4-point Likert scale
PRE + POST + FOLLOW-UP

What It Measures

BIS-11 is the most widely used self-report measure of impulsivity, measuring three distinct but related components of impulsive behaviour.

Attentional Impulsivity
8 items — difficulty focusing attention, making quick cognitive decisions, racing thoughts
Motor Impulsivity
11 items — acting without thinking, impulsive buying, responding without planning — MOST directly relevant to relapse
Non-planning Impulsivity
11 items — lack of future orientation, not thinking about consequences, living for the present — MOST relevant to recovery planning
Total Score Range
30–120 · Higher = greater impulsivity · Clinical cut-off: ≥72 indicates high impulsivity

Scoring

Items rated 1 (rarely/never) to 4 (almost always/always). 11 items are reverse-scored. Subscale and total scores summed. Total scores: <52 low, 52–71 average, ≥72 high impulsivity.

Reliability and Validity

Cronbach's alpha: 0.79–0.83. Factor structure replicated across multiple languages including Hindi validation (Sharma et al., 2014). Extensively used in substance use research globally and in India. Sensitive to intervention effects — MBRP studies consistently show Motor and Non-planning subscale reductions.

Why Used in This Study

Impulsive responding to craving triggers is the primary behavioural mechanism of relapse. BIS-11 Motor Impulsivity directly measures the tendency to act without thinking — what happens when a trigger is encountered without mindfulness skills. The hypothesis that B-MBRP reduces Motor Impulsivity more than Non-planning or Attentional Impulsivity is theoretically motivated and testable at subscale level.

Expected direction: BIS-11 total and Motor Impulsivity subscale scores decrease significantly in experimental group at post-assessment.
🌊
Penn Alcohol Craving Scale / Obsessive Compulsive Drug Use Scale
PACS (Flannery et al., 1999) · 5 items · For alcohol · OCDUS (Bohn et al., 1995) · 13 items · For opioids/drugs
PRE + POST + FOLLOW-UP

What It Measures

PACS (Penn Alcohol Craving Scale): Used for alcohol-dependent patients. 5 items measuring the frequency, intensity, and duration of thoughts about alcohol and resistance to craving over the past week. Score range 0–30. Higher = more severe craving. Widely used in Indian alcohol research, validated and brief.

OCDUS (Obsessive Compulsive Drug Use Scale): Used for opioid-dependent patients. 13 items across two subscales — Obsessive Preoccupation with drug use (thoughts, urges) and Interference with Life (how much craving disrupts daily functioning). Score range 0–40. Higher = more severe craving. The drug version of the Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale.

Which Tool to Use When

Administer PACS for patients with primary alcohol dependence (F10.2x) and OCDUS for patients with primary opioid dependence (F11.2x). If studying a mixed sample, use both and analyse separately by substance type, OR use the Penn Craving Scale (general version) which is applicable across substance types.

Why Used in This Study

Craving reduction is the single most clinically meaningful outcome in de-addiction research — it is the primary proximal predictor of relapse. MBRP was specifically designed to target craving through urge surfing and mindful observation. If craving is not significantly reduced by B-MBRP, the intervention's core mechanism is challenged and the discussion must address this directly.

Expected direction: PACS/OCDUS scores decrease significantly in the experimental group at post-assessment, with maintenance at follow-up.
😔
Patient Health Questionnaire — 9
PHQ-9 — Kroenke, Spitzer & Williams (2001) · 9 items · 4-point Likert scale
PRE + POST + FOLLOW-UP

What It Measures

PHQ-9 measures depression severity based on DSM criteria for Major Depressive Disorder. Each item corresponds to one DSM symptom, rated over the past 2 weeks.

Score 0–4
Minimal depression — likely not clinically significant
Score 5–9
Mild depression — watchful waiting
Score 10–14
Moderate depression — treatment recommended
Score 15–27
Moderately severe to severe — active treatment and monitoring required

Critical Clinical Rule

⚠️ Item 9 asks about suicidal ideation. If the patient endorses any response other than "not at all" on Item 9 at any assessment point, the researcher must immediately inform the treating team and follow the hospital's suicide risk protocol. Document this procedure in your methodology.

Why Used in This Study

Negative affect and depression are the most common emotional triggers of relapse. Substance-dependent patients in early recovery commonly show depressive symptoms (partly as protracted withdrawal, partly as comorbidity). PHQ-9 serves as a secondary outcome measuring whether mindfulness practice reduces depressive symptomatology, and as a control variable ensuring that any FFMQ increases are not simply explained by mood improvement.

Expected direction: PHQ-9 scores decrease significantly in experimental group at post-assessment. Also watch for patients scoring ≥15 who may need psychiatric review before sessions.
😰
Perceived Stress Scale — 10
PSS-10 — Cohen, Kamarck & Mermelstein (1983) · 10 items · 5-point Likert scale
PRE + POST + FOLLOW-UP

What It Measures

PSS-10 measures the degree to which situations in one's life are appraised as stressful — the sense of being overwhelmed, unable to control things, and unable to cope. Not a measure of stressful life events themselves but of the subjective perception of stress. Score range 0–40. Scores 0–13 low, 14–26 moderate, 27–40 high perceived stress.

Why Used in This Study

Perceived stress is a primary precipitant of substance use — patients use to regulate stress rather than face it. MBRP teaches stress tolerance through mindful awareness, directly targeting the perceived stress response. PSS-10 is the most frequently used stress outcome in MBRP trials and has been used in the closest Indian study (Solomon et al., 2024). Including it allows direct comparison of your findings with existing Indian literature.

Expected direction: PSS-10 scores decrease significantly in experimental group at post-assessment.

Why DAST-20/AUDIT Only at Pre?
Severity of dependence is a baseline characteristic used to describe your sample and check group equivalence. It does not change meaningfully within a 3-week inpatient admission — patients are not using during this period, so severity scores would not reflect any real change in dependence severity. Re-administering it at post would produce spuriously reduced scores simply because the patient has not used for 3 weeks.
Why Follow-Up Only for Experimental Group?
The ethical obligation at the end of data collection is to offer B-MBRP to the waitlist control group. By the time of T3 (4–6 weeks post-discharge), some control-group patients will have already received B-MBRP as part of this offer, making their T3 scores uninterpretable. The experimental group T3 serves to test maintenance of intervention gains during the highest-risk relapse window.

The B-MBRP Intervention

Protocol Summary

6 individual sessions × 45–60 minutes each · Delivered every 3–4 days over 3 weeks · In Hindi · By the researcher under supervisor guidance

Adapted from Bowen, Chawla & Marlatt (2011) MBRP manual for inpatient individual delivery. Sessions use the same core principles and techniques as original MBRP but condense the content into 6 units feasible within a typical IPD stay, and convert from group to individual format to accommodate variable admission/discharge schedules.

Section 12

Step-by-Step Procedure

1

Screening and Recruitment

Liaise with the treating psychiatrist and ward nursing staff to identify newly admitted patients who may be eligible. Screen all new admissions against inclusion/exclusion criteria. Approach eligible patients on Day 5–6 of admission (before pre-assessment window) to introduce the study, explain it informally, and give them time to consider participation before pre-assessment on Day 7–10. Do not rush consent on the day of screening.

2

Informed Consent (Day 6–7)

Meet with the patient in a private space. Explain the study purpose, what participation involves (assessment + 6 sessions), that participation is voluntary, that refusal or withdrawal does not affect their treatment in any way, that all data is confidential and stored anonymously, and their right to withdraw at any point. Obtain written signature on the consent form (Hindi version for Hindi-medium patients). Provide a copy. If the patient cannot write, a thumb impression witnessed by a staff member is acceptable.

3

Pre-Assessment — T1 (Day 7–10)

Administer the full pre-assessment battery in a single sitting of 45–60 minutes in a quiet room. Sequence: Proforma first (establishes rapport), then DAST-20/AUDIT (clinical familiarity), then FFMQ, BIS-11, PACS/OCDUS, PHQ-9, PSS-10. For low-literacy patients, read items aloud and record responses — note this in your methodology. Check PHQ-9 Item 9 immediately after administration. Assign the participant a unique code number. Store data in coded form only.

4

B-MBRP Sessions — Experimental Group Only (Days 10–30)

Deliver 6 sessions at a frequency of one session every 3–4 days. Maintain a session log for each participant recording: date, session number, duration, patient attendance, adherence to protocol, home practice reported, and any significant clinical observations. If a session is missed due to a medical procedure or ward logistics, reschedule within 2 days — do not abandon the protocol. If a patient misses 2 consecutive sessions without rescheduling, document this as a protocol deviation.

5

Post-Assessment — T2 (Days 28–32 for experimental · Day 28–30 for control)

Administer the post-assessment battery within 3 days of Session 6 completion for experimental participants, or at the matched time point for control participants. Administer FFMQ, BIS-11, PACS/OCDUS, PHQ-9, PSS-10 and the Session Feedback Form (experimental group only). Record participant code, date, and any missing data. Check PHQ-9 Item 9 again.

6

Follow-Up — T3 (Experimental Group · 4–6 Weeks Post-Discharge)

Contact experimental group participants by phone at 4–6 weeks post-discharge. If phone is unavailable, an OPD visit at their next scheduled follow-up serves as the contact point. Read all T3 tools aloud over phone and record responses. If the participant cannot be contacted after 3 attempts on different days, document as lost to follow-up. Analyse T3 data on an intention-to-treat basis where possible.

7

B-MBRP Offered to Waitlist Control

After data collection from both cohorts is complete, offer B-MBRP to the waitlist control group participants as an ethical obligation. This is stated in the consent form. Document delivery in the session log. This data is not part of your main analysis but can be briefly described as a service delivery component.

Data Entry and Storage

  • All data entered into a password-protected SPSS data file within 48 hours of each assessment. Never enter data from memory — enter from the paper sheet.
  • Paper sheets stored in a locked drawer/cabinet accessible only to the researcher and supervisor. Sheets are labelled with participant code only — never names.
  • A master code key (linking participant code to name and contact) is stored separately from the data — in a different locked location. This is the only document with identifying information.
  • Data backed up to a separate USB drive or cloud storage (encrypted) after every 5 new participants.
  • Session logs stored with the researcher's working files, labelled by code only.

Handling Missing Data

  • If a participant misses fewer than 5 items on any tool, use mean imputation (replace missing item with the mean of their other items on that subscale).
  • If a participant misses more than 5 items on any tool, mark the tool as incomplete for that administration and use available-case analysis.
  • Participants who complete T1 but not T2 are included in intention-to-treat analysis using their T1 score carried forward — document this as a sensitivity analysis.
  • Report the total number of missing data points and the pattern of missingness in your Results chapter.

Attrition Management

Expected Attrition Sources and Management
  • LAMA (Leave Against Medical Advice): Most common attrition source in Indian IPD settings. Recruit 25 per cohort to account for this. Document LAMA dates and compare LAMA patients vs. completers on baseline variables.
  • Medical complications: Medical deterioration requiring transfer or intensive care — exclude from analysis if they cannot complete T2 assessment.
  • Withdrawal of consent: Respect immediately. Remove their data from analysis. Document in consort flow chart.
  • Loss to follow-up at T3: Expected to be 20–30% — document and report, do not impute T3 data.

Section 14

Analysis Plan

Step 1 — Preliminary and Descriptive Analysis

  • Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, frequency, range) for all sociodemographic and baseline clinical variables
  • Normality testing: Shapiro-Wilk test for all continuous outcome variables (n < 50, Shapiro-Wilk is preferred over Kolmogorov-Smirnov)
  • Baseline equivalence check: Independent samples t-test (parametric) or Mann-Whitney U (non-parametric) on all pre-assessment scores to confirm no significant difference between experimental and waitlist control groups at T1 — this is essential for a quasi-experimental design and must be reported in your Results
  • Consort flow diagram: report recruitment, screening, enrollment, attrition, and final analysis N for both groups

Step 2 — Primary Between-Group Analysis (Main Hypotheses)

HypothesisOutcomeAnalysisSoftware Command
H1 — CravingPACS / OCDUS post-scoreANCOVA — post-score as dependent variable, group (experimental/control) as fixed factor, pre-score as covariateSPSS: Analyze → GLM → Univariate
H2 — ImpulsivityBIS-11 total + Motor subscale post-scoreANCOVA — same structure. Run separately for total and each subscaleSPSS: GLM post_BIS BY group WITH pre_BIS
H3 — MindfulnessFFMQ total + Non-reactivity subscale post-scoreANCOVA — FFMQ total, then subscales separately as dependent variablesSPSS: GLM post_FFMQ BY group WITH pre_FFMQ
H4 — Depression/StressPHQ-9 and PSS-10 post-scoresANCOVA for each separatelySame structure
Why ANCOVA over simple t-test? ANCOVA statistically controls for pre-assessment scores as a covariate, giving a cleaner, more precise estimate of the intervention effect. Even if baseline equivalence is established, ANCOVA is the recommended analysis for pre-post designs because it reduces error variance and increases statistical power. This is the standard analysis in all published MBRP trials.

Step 3 — Within-Group Pre-Post Analysis

  • Run paired samples t-test (or Wilcoxon signed-rank if non-normal) separately for each group on each outcome variable: T1 vs. T2
  • This shows whether each group improved individually, independent of between-group comparison
  • Expected: Both groups may show improvement (due to detoxification benefits), but experimental group shows significantly greater improvement — this is the argument for B-MBRP specifically

Step 4 — Follow-Up Analysis (Experimental Group Only)

  • Paired samples t-test: T2 (post) vs. T3 (follow-up) — tests whether gains are maintained
  • If no significant difference T2→T3: gains maintained ✅
  • If significant worsening T2→T3: intervention effects did not transfer to real-world context — important finding to discuss

Step 5 — Predictor Analysis

  • Pearson/Spearman correlation: between baseline FFMQ, BIS-11, and craving scores and the magnitude of change (T2 minus T1) on each outcome — who benefits most?
  • Simple linear regression: predict FFMQ change score from baseline mindfulness, impulsivity, and duration of use as predictors

Step 6 — Exploratory Substance Type Comparison

  • Within the experimental group, split by primary substance (alcohol vs. opioid) and compare change scores on ANCOVA outcomes using substance type as a between-subjects factor
  • Report effect sizes (Cohen's d) for each comparison
  • Frame as exploratory — not powered for this comparison given small subgroups

Report partial eta-squared (η²p) for ANCOVA and Cohen's d for all t-test comparisons. For your pilot study, even medium effect sizes (d = 0.50) are sufficient to justify a larger confirmatory RCT — which is the primary recommendation of your Discussion chapter.

Significance Level and Multiple Comparisons

Use α = 0.05 as the significance threshold. Given that you are running multiple ANCOVAs (one per outcome), apply Bonferroni correction: divide 0.05 by the number of primary outcomes (5) = adjusted α of 0.01 for each primary test. Report both uncorrected and Bonferroni-corrected p-values. This protects against Type I error and demonstrates methodological rigour to the viva committee.

Software

All analyses conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics (version 21 or above). Available in most clinical psychology departments in India. Key SPSS paths: Analyze → General Linear Model → Univariate for ANCOVA; Analyze → Compare Means → Paired Samples T-Test for within-group; Analyze → Correlate → Bivariate for predictor correlations.

Ethical Considerations

IEC Clearance
Submit to the Institutional Ethics Committee of your college/university before any data collection. An interventional study requires full review (not expedited). Prepare: full study protocol, informed consent form in Hindi and English, all assessment tools, B-MBRP session outline, researcher qualification statement, supervisor declaration, and hospital permission letter.
Informed Consent
Written consent in Hindi and English. Explicitly state: (1) voluntary participation, (2) right to withdraw at any time without affecting treatment received, (3) no impact on discharge timing or medication, (4) confidentiality of all data. For patients who cannot write: thumb impression witnessed by a ward staff member. Do not proceed without signed/thumbprinted consent.
Distress and Risk Protocol
If PHQ-9 Item 9 is endorsed at any assessment point, immediately inform the treating psychiatrist — do not manage this yourself. If a B-MBRP session evokes acute distress (patient becomes very upset, dissociates, or discloses suicidal thoughts), suspend the session and refer to the treating team. Document in session log. Resume sessions only after clearance from the treating psychiatrist.
Confidentiality
All data stored under participant code numbers only. No names on any data sheet, SPSS file, or session log. Code key stored separately in a locked location accessible only to the researcher and supervisor. Audio recording of sessions (if used for adherence checking) requires a separate specific consent item.
Equity — B-MBRP for Control Group
After data collection is complete, offer B-MBRP to all Cohort 1 (waitlist control) participants as an ethical obligation. State this clearly in the consent form. The waitlist design ensures that control participants are not permanently denied the intervention — they receive it with a delay, not never.
Data Retention
Raw data sheets and SPSS files retained for a minimum of 5 years post-publication or dissertation submission. After this period, paper data is shredded and digital files are permanently deleted. No data is shared with third parties without participant consent and IEC approval.

Section 17

Significance and Importance of the Study

Clinical Significance

Direct Clinical Impact at Your Setting

If B-MBRP demonstrates efficacy, the de-addiction ward at District Hospital Guna gains a concrete, low-cost, deliverable psychosocial programme that can be integrated into routine inpatient care. The 6-session protocol you develop, test, and refine becomes a local resource that the treating team can implement for future patients — without the researcher. This is the most direct possible translation from research to practice.

Academic and Scientific Significance

Fills a Documented Gap in Indian Literature
No published study has evaluated MBRP with a waitlist control design and a multi-domain outcome battery in a district-level Indian de-addiction IPD. Your study directly addresses this gap and produces the first such dataset from this population and setting.
Generates Pilot Data for a Future RCT
Pilot studies justify their small sample sizes by providing effect size estimates that power a subsequent large-scale confirmatory RCT. Your findings — whatever they are — provide this service to the field. A Discussion recommendation for a full RCT is the standard and expected conclusion.
LMIC Evidence for Mindfulness Interventions
The systematic gap in MBRP evidence from low- and middle-income countries (noted by Brazilian, Chinese, and Pakistani researchers) is directly relevant to India. Your study contributes to a body of evidence that mindfulness-based interventions can be feasibly adapted and delivered in resource-limited healthcare settings.
Multi-Domain Outcome Measurement
By measuring craving, impulsivity, mindfulness, depression, and stress simultaneously, your study can test whether MBRP effects are broad (across all domains) or specific (targeting craving and impulsivity more than depression) — a theoretically important question for understanding the mechanism of action.

Policy Significance

India's National Mental Health Policy (2014) and the Mental Healthcare Act (2017) both emphasise the need for evidence-based psychosocial interventions at district-level facilities. Research conducted at district hospital level directly informs policy about what can realistically be implemented in the public sector — rather than what works only in tertiary care institutions. Your study's findings are directly relevant to district mental health programme planning.

For Your Viva — The Central Argument in One Paragraph

"Substance dependence relapse is driven by craving, impulsivity, and the inability to tolerate negative affect without substance use. Treatment As Usual in Indian district hospitals addresses the biological dimension of dependence but leaves these psychological vulnerabilities intact. Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention was developed specifically to address this gap. The present study is the first to evaluate a brief, individually-delivered B-MBRP intervention with a waitlist control design and a multi-domain outcome battery in a district-level Indian de-addiction inpatient setting. Its findings — whatever their direction — generate the first Indian dataset on brief inpatient MBRP efficacy and provide the effect size estimates required to power a larger confirmatory randomised controlled trial."
