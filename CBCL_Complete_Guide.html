1. Introduction & Historical Background

1.1 Developer & Origin

The Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) was developed by Thomas M. Achenbach and Craig Edelbrock in 1983 at the University of Vermont, Department of Psychiatry, Burlington, Vermont, USA.

The instrument was substantially revised in 2001 by Thomas M. Achenbach and Leslie A. Rescorla, incorporating DSM-oriented scales and updated norms. This revision forms the current standard version in clinical use.

1.2 The ASEBA System

The CBCL is the flagship instrument of the Achenbach System of Empirically Based Assessment (ASEBA) — a comprehensive, multi-informant assessment system designed to evaluate behavioral, emotional, and social functioning across the lifespan. ASEBA represents one of the most extensively researched and widely used assessment frameworks in child and adolescent mental health worldwide.

1.4 Theoretical Foundation

Unlike many psychological instruments that are derived from a specific theoretical model (e.g., psychoanalytic, cognitive-behavioral), the CBCL is empirically based. This means:

  • Items were derived from clinical literature review, clinical records, and consultation with parents and clinicians — not from a priori theoretical categories
  • Scales were constructed through multivariate statistical methods (principal components analysis, factor analysis) applied to large datasets of real clinical referrals
  • The taxonomy of syndromes emerged from the data itself rather than being imposed from existing diagnostic frameworks
  • This "bottom-up" approach ensures that the instrument captures patterns of co-occurring problems as they actually present in children, rather than as theory predicts they should

3. Purpose & Applications

3.1 Primary Purposes

  1. Comprehensive Assessment: Quantify the range and severity of behavioral/emotional problems and social competencies in children and adolescents
  2. Clinical Screening: Identify children who may need clinical services or further diagnostic evaluation
  3. Treatment Planning: Establish baseline problem profiles to guide intervention selection
  4. Outcome Monitoring: Track changes in problem scores over time to evaluate treatment effectiveness
  5. Multi-Informant Comparison: Compare perceptions across parents, teachers, and youth to identify context-specific or pervasive problems

5. Structure & Content (CBCL/6-18)

The CBCL/6-18 is organized into two major sections:

5.1 PART I: Competence Items (20 Items)

These items assess the child's positive functioning and engagement:

I. Activities Scale

  • Item types: Sports participated in, hobbies and activities, organizations/clubs, jobs/chores
  • Scoring dimensions: Number of activities, amount of time spent, skill level compared to peers
  • Example items:
    • "Please list the sports your child most likes to take part in" (up to 3)
    • Rate time spent and skill compared to age-mates: less than average / average / more than average

II. Social Scale

  • Item types: Number of close friends, frequency of contact with friends, behavior with siblings and other children, behavior alone
  • Scoring dimensions: Quantity of relationships, quality of peer interaction
  • Example items:
    • "About how many close friends does your child have?" (none / 1 / 2 or 3 / 4 or more)
    • "How well does your child get along with brothers and sisters?" (worse / average / better)
    • "How well does your child play and work alone?" (worse / average / better)

III. School Scale

  • Item types: Academic performance in specific subjects, special class placement, grade repetition, other school problems
  • Scoring dimensions: Academic achievement level, presence of learning difficulties
  • Example items:
    • Rate performance in each academic subject: Failing / Below Average / Average / Above Average
    • "Is your child in a special class or special school?" (Yes/No — describe)
    • "Has your child repeated a grade?" (Yes/No — describe)

5.2 PART II: Behavior Problem Items (113 Items)

The 113 items describe specific behavioral and emotional problems. Each item is rated on a 3-point scale for the past 6 months:

  • 0 = Not True (as far as you know)
  • 1 = Somewhat or Sometimes True
  • 2 = Very True or Often True

Three items allow parents to describe additional problems:

  • Additional spaces for elaboration on specific items

6. Administration Procedure

6.1 Before Administration

Materials Needed:

  • CBCL/6-18 form (official ASEBA form — copyrighted)
  • Pen or pencil
  • Quiet, comfortable setting
  • Scoring profile (hand-scoring) OR ASEBA computer software

Pre-Administration Checklist:

  • ☐ Confirm child's age falls within 6–18 range
  • ☐ Identify appropriate informant (parent or parent-surrogate who knows child well)
  • ☐ Ensure informant has adequate literacy (approximately 5th-grade reading level)
  • ☐ Prepare the setting — private, minimal distractions
  • ☐ Have both parents fill out separate forms if possible

6.2 Instructions to the Parent

Provide the following explanation (adapted from the manual):

"This form asks about your child's behavior and activities. I'd like you to fill it out based on how [child's name] has been over the past 6 months. The checklist covers a wide variety of behaviors that children might show — many of them will not apply to your child. Just answer each item as best you can, even if some seem unusual or don't seem to apply perfectly."
"For each item, circle 0 if the item is not true of your child, 1 if it is somewhat or sometimes true, and 2 if it is very true or often true. Please answer all items as well as you can."

6.3 Step-by-Step Administration

Step 1: Demographic Information — Fill in child's full name, sex, age, date of birth, ethnicity/race, parent's type of work, date form is filled out, grade in school, name of school.

Step 2: Part I — Competence Section — Guide parent through the activities, social, and school sections. Ensure they understand the comparison format ("compared to others of the same age").

Step 3: Part II — Behavior Problems — Instruct parent to rate each of the 113 items. Remind them to consider the past 6 months. Emphasize: answer every item; if uncertain, make best estimate.

Step 4: Review for Completeness — Check that all items have been answered. Ask parent to complete any skipped items.

6.4 Special Administration Situations

SituationProcedure
Parent with limited literacyAdminister as structured interview — read items aloud, record responses
Non-English-speaking parentUse official translated version (available in 100+ languages from ASEBA)
Divorced/separated parentsEach parent fills out separately — compare profiles
Foster/adoptive parentCan complete if has known child ≥6 months; note informant status
Guardian/relativeAcceptable if knows child well; note relationship
Parent questions item meaningClarify factually without influencing response direction
Parent refuses specific itemsNote as missing; do not force. Document refusal.

6.5 Important Administration Rules

  1. Do NOT paraphrase items — read exactly as written if administering orally
  2. Do NOT provide examples of what counts as "sometimes" vs. "often" — let parent decide
  3. Do NOT discuss items with the child present
  4. DO encourage completion of all items — remind that many items won't apply
  5. DO note if parent appears to minimize or exaggerate (clinical observation)
  6. DO record time taken and any difficulties observed during completion

6.6 Timing

  • Self-administered: Typically 15–20 minutes
  • Interview-administered: 20–30 minutes
  • Allow extra time for parents who are slow readers or have questions

7. Scoring System

(Based on Manual — Appendix A: Instructions for Hand Scoring the Child Behavior Profile)

7.1 Overview of Scoring

The CBCL uses a hierarchical scoring system:

Individual Items (0, 1, 2)
    ↓ Sum specific items
Syndrome Scale Raw Scores (8 narrow-band scales)
    ↓ Sum syndrome scales
Broad-Band Raw Scores (Internalizing, Externalizing, Total Problems)
    ↓ Convert using norms (age- and sex-specific tables)
T-Scores & Percentiles
IMPORTANT (from manual): Be certain to use a scoring Profile appropriate for the child's sex and age. If a parent checks more than 1 box where only 1 should be checked, score the average of the 2 boxes checked.

8. Syndrome Scales (Narrow-Band) — 8 Empirically-Based Scales

These eight scales were derived through factor analysis of large samples of referred and non-referred children. They represent empirically derived clusters of co-occurring problems.

Scale I: Anxious/Depressed (13 items) — Internalizing

Core construct: Anxiety, fearfulness, sadness, self-consciousness, feelings of worthlessness

Key items: Cries a lot; fears school; fears doing bad things; must be perfect; feels unloved; feels worthless; nervous/tense; feels too guilty; self-conscious; suspicious; unhappy/sad; worries

Clinical meaning: Elevated scores suggest subjective emotional distress combining anxious arousal with depressive cognitions. Often reflects an over-controlled, internalizing presentation.

Scale II: Withdrawn/Depressed (8 items) — Internalizing

Core construct: Social withdrawal, lack of energy, anhedonia, preference for solitude

Key items: Enjoys little; prefers being alone; refuses to talk; secretive; underactive; unhappy/sad; withdrawn; lacks energy

Clinical meaning: Reflects behavioral manifestations of depression — reduced engagement, reduced initiative, and visible sadness.

Scale III: Somatic Complaints (11 items) — Internalizing

Core construct: Physical symptoms without known medical cause

Key items: Feels dizzy; overtired; aches and pains; headaches; nausea; eye problems; skin problems; stomachaches; vomiting

Clinical meaning: Somatization — expression of emotional distress through physical symptoms. Always recommend medical evaluation first.

Scale IV: Social Problems (11 items) — Neither

Core construct: Difficulties in peer relationships, social immaturity, social rejection

Key items: Acts too young; clings to adults; doesn't get along with peers; gets teased; not liked; clumsy; prefers younger children

Clinical meaning: Social skills deficits, peer rejection. May indicate developmental delays, ASD features, or consequences of other problems.

Scale V: Thought Problems (15 items) — Neither

Core construct: Bizarre thoughts, strange behaviors, hallucination-like experiences, obsessions

Key items: Can't get mind off thoughts; hears/sees things not there; strange behavior/ideas; stores things; repeats acts

Clinical meaning: May suggest OCD features to severe perceptual disturbances. Scores ≥70 should prompt thorough evaluation for thought disorder.

Scale VI: Attention Problems (10 items) — Neither

Core construct: Inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, disorganization

Key items: Can't concentrate; can't sit still; confused; daydreams; impulsive; poor school work; inattentive

Clinical meaning: Consistent with ADHD presentations. Must be present across settings — compare CBCL with TRF.

Scale VII: Rule-Breaking Behavior (17 items) — Externalizing

Core construct: Norm-violating behaviors, delinquent acts, dishonesty, substance use

Key items: Drinks alcohol; lacks guilt; breaks rules; bad companions; lying; runs away; sets fires; steals; truancy; uses drugs

Clinical meaning: Antisocial behavior pattern. Associated with conduct disorder, ODD, or delinquency.

Scale VIII: Aggressive Behavior (18 items) — Externalizing

Core construct: Hostile, oppositional, and physically aggressive behavior; emotional dysregulation

Key items: Argues; mean to others; demands attention; destroys things; disobedient; fights; attacks; screams; stubborn; temper tantrums; threatens

Clinical meaning: Overtly hostile, confrontational behavior. Distinguished from Rule-Breaking by interpersonal aggression and emotional volatility.

9. Broad-Band Scales (Internalizing / Externalizing / Total Problems)

9.1 Conceptual Framework

INTERNALIZING (Over-controlled problems)
├── Anxious/Depressed
├── Withdrawn/Depressed
└── Somatic Complaints

EXTERNALIZING (Under-controlled problems)
├── Rule-Breaking Behavior
└── Aggressive Behavior

NEITHER (Mixed)
├── Social Problems
├── Thought Problems
└── Attention Problems

TOTAL PROBLEMS = All 113 items

9.2 Internalizing Scale

Definition: Problems primarily within the self — emotions directed inward. Elevated Internalizing reflects an "over-controlled" pattern: worry, sadness, withdrawal, physical complaints.

Associated presentations: Depression, anxiety disorders, somatic symptom disorders, school refusal, selective mutism.

9.3 Externalizing Scale

Definition: Problems directed outward toward the environment and others. Elevated Externalizing reflects an "under-controlled" pattern: aggression, defiance, norm violation.

Associated presentations: ADHD, ODD, Conduct Disorder, intermittent explosive disorder, substance use.

9.4 Total Problems Score

Definition: Overall severity of behavioral and emotional problems. Sum of ALL 113 items (including ~17 items not assigned to any syndrome scale).

10. DSM-Oriented Scales

10.1 Background

Added in the 2001 revision to bridge empirically-derived syndromes and DSM diagnostic categories. Developed by expert panels rating each item's consistency with DSM-IV categories.

Important: These scales are NOT diagnostic instruments — they indicate consistency with DSM categories, providing hypotheses for further evaluation.

10.2 The Six DSM-Oriented Scales

1. Affective Problems (13 items)

DSM: Major Depressive Disorder / Dysthymia | Content: Sadness, loss of interest, sleep disturbance, fatigue, worthlessness, suicidal ideation

2. Anxiety Problems (6 items)

DSM: GAD, Separation Anxiety, Specific Phobia | Content: Excessive worry, separation fears, specific fears, nervousness

3. Somatic Problems (7 items)

DSM: Somatic Symptom Disorder | Content: Multiple physical complaints without known medical cause

4. ADHD Problems (7 items)

DSM: Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder | Content: Inattention, hyperactivity, impulsivity, disorganization

5. Oppositional Defiant Problems (5 items)

DSM: Oppositional Defiant Disorder | Content: Defiance, argumentativeness, anger, irritability

6. Conduct Problems (15 items)

DSM: Conduct Disorder | Content: Serious norm violations, aggression toward people/animals, destruction, theft

11. Competence Scales

11.1 Overview

The Competence Scales assess positive functioning — strengths, activities, social engagement, and academic performance.

CRITICAL RULE: For competence scales, LOWER T-scores indicate MORE problems (opposite direction from behavior problem scales).

11.2 Activities Scale

Measures breadth and quality of involvement in sports, hobbies, organizations, and jobs/chores. Low scores may reflect withdrawal, depression, restricted interests (ASD), or lack of opportunity.

11.3 Social Scale

Measures quantity and quality of social relationships. Low scores suggest social skills deficits, social anxiety, peer rejection, or ASD features.

11.4 School Scale

Measures academic performance and school functioning. Low scores may indicate learning disabilities, ADHD impact, depression effects, or intellectual limitations.

11.5 Total Competence Score

Sum of Activities + Social + School. A child can have elevated problems AND low competence (dual deficit) OR problems with adequate competence (resilience factor).

12.6 Important Notes on Cutoffs

  1. Cutoffs are NOT diagnostic thresholds — they indicate need for further evaluation
  2. Borderline range is clinically important — don't dismiss as "normal"
  3. Context matters — T=69 is clinically indistinguishable from T=70
  4. SEM ≈ 2–4 T-score points; differences <5 may reflect measurement error

13. Interpretation Guidelines

13.1 General Principles

  1. Never interpret in isolation — integrate with interview, observation, history, other tests
  2. Consider the informant — biases, relationship quality, custody disputes
  3. Consider developmental context — some behaviors are normative at certain ages
  4. Consider cultural context — behavior expectations vary across cultures
  5. Look at patterns, not isolated scores
  6. Report strengths alongside problems

13.2 Step-by-Step Interpretation Sequence

  1. Total Problems — Is there overall elevated difficulty? (T ≥ 64 = clinical)
  2. Broad-Band Scales — Internalizing vs. Externalizing vs. Mixed?
  3. Individual Syndrome Scales — Which are elevated (≥65)?
  4. DSM-Oriented Scales — Support diagnostic hypotheses?
  5. Competence Scales — Adequate or impaired?
  6. Critical Items — Self-harm (18, 91), hallucinations (40, 70), fire-setting (72), cruelty (15, 21), sexual (59, 60, 73)
  7. Cross-Informant Comparison — Agreement vs. discrepancy?
ANY item scored "2" on a critical item warrants immediate clinical follow-up

13.3 Common Profile Patterns

Pattern 1: Pure Internalizing

Elevated: Anxious/Depressed, Withdrawn/Depressed, Somatic Complaints
Consider: Depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, somatic symptom disorder
Presentation: Quiet, tearful, avoidant, somatic complaints, school refusal

Pattern 2: Pure Externalizing

Elevated: Rule-Breaking, Aggressive Behavior
Consider: ODD, Conduct Disorder, ADHD
Presentation: Defiant, aggressive, rule-violating, impulsive

Pattern 3: Mixed

Elevated: Both Internalizing AND Externalizing
Consider: Comorbidity, complex trauma, DMDD, bipolar spectrum
Note: Poorer prognosis; requires multimodal intervention

Pattern 4: Attention + Externalizing

Elevated: Attention Problems, Rule-Breaking, Aggressive Behavior
Consider: ADHD with comorbid ODD/CD (very common in clinical settings)

Pattern 5: Thought + Social Problems

Elevated: Thought Problems, Social Problems ± Withdrawn
Consider: ASD features, early-onset psychosis, severe OCD

Appendix E: Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Q1: Can I photocopy the CBCL form?

A: No. The CBCL is copyrighted. Forms must be purchased from https://aseba.org.

Q2: What if a parent rates between two values (e.g., "1.5")?

A: Score as the HIGHER value (round up to 2). Always score in direction of more pathology when uncertain.

Q3: What if more than 8 items are blank?

A: The form should NOT be scored. Return to parent and request completion if possible.

Q4: Can grandparents fill out the CBCL?

A: Yes, if they know the child well and have regular contact. Note the relationship in the report.

Q5: Which edition should I use?

A: Always use the 2001 edition (Achenbach & Rescorla) for clinical work.

Q6: How to score items on multiple scales?

A: Score on EACH syndrome scale. For broad-band totals, count overlapping items only ONCE.

Q7: Child is exactly 6 — which version?

A: Use CBCL/6-18. Under 6 (e.g., 5 years 11 months) → CBCL/1.5-5.

Q8: CBCL for children with intellectual disability?

A: Yes, but interpret with caution. Some behaviors may be appropriate for mental age.

Q9: How often to re-administer?

A: Every 3–6 months for treatment monitoring.

Q10: Is there an Indian-normed version?

A: No official Indian norms (as of 2024). Use US norms or multi-society norms with cultural sensitivity.

Q11: Parent and teacher profiles completely different?

A: Normal (r ≈ .28). Discrepancies suggest context-specific problems. Report both and discuss environmental factors.

Q12: CBCL in forensic/custody evaluations?

A: Yes, with caveats: obtain from BOTH parents; note informant bias in adversarial contexts; supplement with objective measures.
