Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)

A simple, detailed guide to all 18 items and how to score each one from 1 (not present) to 7 (extremely severe) — including what "moderate" means for every symptom.

What is the BPRS?

The Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) is one of the oldest and most widely used clinician-rated scales for measuring the severity of psychiatric symptoms. It was created by Overall and Gorham in 1962 (originally 16 items) and later expanded to the standard 18-item version (Guy, 1976) by adding Excitement and Disorientation.

  • Purpose: Measure positive symptoms, general psychopathology, and affective (mood) symptoms — and track how they change with treatment.
  • How it is done: A trained clinician rates the patient based on a clinical interview plus observed behaviour, usually covering the last 2–3 days.
  • Time: About 20–30 minutes.
  • Output: Each item gives a score; all items are added together for a total. Higher total = more severe illness.

The 7-Point Scoring System (used for every item)

Each of the 18 items is rated on the same 1–7 scale. The rater picks the single number that best matches how severe the symptom is.

ScoreLabelWhat it generally means
1Not presentSymptom is absent / completely normal.
2Very mildQuestionable or very slight — may be within normal limits.
3MildClearly present but slight; causes little or no impairment.
4ModerateDefinitely present and noticeable; causes some distress or interferes a little with functioning. This is the usual "clearly clinically significant" threshold.
5Moderately severeProminent symptom that clearly interferes with daily functioning.
6SevereStrong, frequent symptom that seriously disrupts functioning.
7Extremely severeOverwhelming, constant symptom; dominates the clinical picture.

Note: Some printed versions use a 0–6 scale (0 = not present, 6 = extremely severe) and some add an extra option for "not assessed." The most common modern version is 1–7. A score of "moderate" sits in the middle and is generally the point at which the symptom is judged to be definitely clinically meaningful.

Detailed Guide to Each Item

1Somatic Concern Physical health worry

What it measures: The degree of worry or preoccupation with physical health and bodily complaints — whether or not there is a real medical basis.

What to look for: Complaints about aches, pains, organs, illness; how much these thoughts dominate the person. Somatic delusions (false fixed beliefs about the body) are rated in the severe range.

  • Mild (3): Occasional realistic worry about health.
  • Moderate (4): Frequently preoccupied with bodily complaints, but can be reassured; concern is somewhat out of proportion.
  • Severe (6–7): Constant preoccupation, cannot be reassured, or bizarre somatic delusions (e.g., "my organs are rotting").
Moderate (4) = the worry is clearly present and noticeable, somewhat exaggerated, but the person can still be temporarily reassured and function.

2Anxiety Worry / fear

What it measures: Reported nervousness, worry, fear, or over-concern about the present or future (the patient's subjective experience, not observed behaviour).

What to look for: Verbal reports of feeling anxious, tense, fearful, apprehensive. Rate only on what the patient says they feel (physical tension is rated under item 6).

  • Mild (3): Some worry over current issues, easily managed.
  • Moderate (4): Reports clear, frequent worry/fear that is distressing but does not dominate the day.
  • Severe (6–7): Overwhelming fear/panic that the person cannot control and that dominates their thinking.
Moderate (4) = persistent reported anxiety that the patient finds clearly distressing, but they can still carry on with most activities.

3Emotional Withdrawal Lack of relating

What it measures: Deficiency in relating to the interviewer and the immediate situation — how much emotional contact is "there."

What to look for: Lack of eye contact, failure to engage, feeling that the person is distant or behind a glass wall. Based on observation during the interview.

  • Mild (3): Slightly distant but generally relates.
  • Moderate (4): Noticeable lack of emotional engagement; relating feels effortful and incomplete.
  • Severe (6–7): Almost no emotional contact; seems completely detached from the interview.
Moderate (4) = the person clearly seems emotionally distant and hard to reach, though some contact is still possible.

4Conceptual Disorganization Disordered thinking

What it measures: Degree to which thought processes are confused, disconnected, or disorganized — judged from how the person speaks (form of thought, not content).

What to look for: Vagueness, tangents, loose associations, derailment, irrelevant answers, made-up words. Rated on observed speech during the interview.

  • Mild (3): Occasional vagueness or circumstantial speech, still followable.
  • Moderate (4): Speech sometimes wanders or is hard to follow; clear but intermittent loosening of associations.
  • Severe (6–7): Speech is mostly incoherent or impossible to follow.
Moderate (4) = thinking is noticeably disorganized at times and you occasionally lose the thread, but conversation is still partly possible.

5Guilt Feelings Self-blame

What it measures: Over-concern or remorse for past behaviour — excessive self-blame or guilt (the patient's reported feelings).

What to look for: Statements of regret, self-blame, feeling responsible for misfortune, guilt out of proportion to events. Guilty delusions are rated in the severe range.

  • Mild (3): Some realistic regret over past actions.
  • Moderate (4): Clear, exaggerated guilt or self-blame, but can be partly reasoned with.
  • Severe (6–7): Crushing guilt or delusions of guilt (e.g., believing they caused disasters).
Moderate (4) = guilt that is clearly excessive for the situation and causes distress, but is not delusional.

6Tension Observed physical restlessness

What it measures: Observed physical and motor signs of nervousness — not what the patient reports (that is item 2, Anxiety).

What to look for: Restlessness, fidgeting, tremor, jitteriness, pacing, inability to sit still, tense posture.

  • Mild (3): Slight restlessness or fidgeting.
  • Moderate (4): Obvious signs of tension — visible fidgeting, shifting, tremor noticed during interview.
  • Severe (6–7): Cannot sit still; constant pacing, marked tremor, extreme physical agitation.
Moderate (4) = physical tension is clearly visible to the observer but the person can still remain in the interview.

7Mannerisms & Posturing Odd movements

What it measures: Unusual, bizarre, or unnatural motor behaviour — odd movements or postures that make the person stand out.

What to look for: Strange grimaces, repetitive gestures, rocking, holding odd postures, stereotyped movements. (Simple restlessness goes under Tension.)

  • Mild (3): Slightly odd movement, easy to overlook.
  • Moderate (4): Clearly peculiar movements/postures that are noticeable but not constant.
  • Severe (6–7): Frequent or bizarre posturing/movements that markedly disrupt normal activity.
Moderate (4) = distinctly unusual movements or postures are present and noticeable, but occasional rather than continuous.

8Grandiosity Inflated self-worth

What it measures: Exaggerated self-opinion, sense of superiority, special powers, identity, or grandiose beliefs.

What to look for: Claims of special talents, wealth, fame, powers, or identity (e.g., "I am chosen/famous/superhuman"). Grandiose delusions are rated severe.

  • Mild (3): Somewhat inflated self-confidence, still plausible.
  • Moderate (4): Clearly exaggerated claims of ability/importance, but not fully delusional.
  • Severe (6–7): Firm grandiose delusions (e.g., believing one is a deity or ruler).
Moderate (4) = the inflated self-view is obvious and unrealistic but the person can still entertain some doubt.

9Depressive Mood Sadness

What it measures: Reported sadness, low mood, hopelessness, despondency, or discouragement.

What to look for: Statements of feeling down, sad, hopeless, tearful; loss of pleasure. Based mainly on what the patient reports (and tearfulness observed).

  • Mild (3): Occasional low mood, lifts with distraction.
  • Moderate (4): Frequent sad mood that is clearly present most of the interview but does not fully dominate.
  • Severe (6–7): Pervasive, deep despair/hopelessness present almost constantly.
Moderate (4) = sadness is clearly present and reported regularly, with some distress, but not unrelenting.

10Hostility Anger toward others

What it measures: Animosity, contempt, anger, or hatred directed toward other people (outside the interview room) — reported, not behaviour shown to the interviewer (that is item 14).

What to look for: Reports of arguments, resentment, wanting to hurt others, irritability toward family/others.

  • Mild (3): Some irritability or annoyance with others.
  • Moderate (4): Clear, frequent anger/resentment toward others, possibly verbal arguments.
  • Severe (6–7): Intense hatred, threats, or violent behaviour toward others.
Moderate (4) = obvious recurring anger toward others (arguments, strong resentment) but not yet threats or violence.

11Suspiciousness Mistrust / paranoia

What it measures: Belief (reported or implied) that others have, or have had, harmful or discriminatory intent toward the patient.

What to look for: Mistrust, guardedness, feeling watched, followed, plotted against. Persecutory delusions are rated in the severe range.

  • Mild (3): Mild wariness or feeling people may not be fully trustworthy.
  • Moderate (4): Clear suspiciousness — feels others may be against them — but accepts it could be untrue.
  • Severe (6–7): Fixed persecutory delusions (e.g., certain a group is plotting to kill them).
Moderate (4) = definite mistrust/paranoid ideas are expressed, but the person can still consider they might be mistaken.

12Hallucinatory Behaviour Perceptions without stimulus

What it measures: Perceptions (hearing, seeing, smelling, feeling things) that occur without a real external stimulus.

What to look for: Reports of voices, visions, smells, or bodily sensations with no source. Rate by frequency and how much they affect the person. (Only count true perceptions, not vivid thoughts.)

  • Mild (3): Occasional, brief hallucination with little impact; or strong suspicion of one.
  • Moderate (4): Definite hallucinations occurring regularly but the person retains some insight/control.
  • Severe (6–7): Frequent hallucinations that strongly influence behaviour (e.g., obeying command voices).
Moderate (4) = hallucinations are clearly present and happen fairly often, causing distress, but do not fully control behaviour.

13Motor Retardation Slowed movement

What it measures: Slowing of movements and speech — reduced energy, slowed reactions (the opposite of Excitement).

What to look for: Slowed speech, long pauses before answering, reduced gestures, slumped posture, low energy. Based on observation.

  • Mild (3): Slightly slowed speech or movement.
  • Moderate (4): Clearly slowed speech and movement, noticeable pauses, reduced spontaneity.
  • Severe (6–7): Extreme slowing, near-mutism or near-immobility.
Moderate (4) = the slowing of speech/movement is obvious to the observer but the person can still participate.

14Uncooperativeness Resistance in interview

What it measures: Resistance, guardedness, or refusal to cooperate with the interviewer (hostility shown in the room — different from item 10).

What to look for: Refusing to answer, hostile/curt responses, defiance, evasiveness during the interview itself.

  • Mild (3): Slightly guarded or reluctant but answers most questions.
  • Moderate (4): Clearly resistant — gives short, grudging answers or avoids several topics.
  • Severe (6–7): Refuses to cooperate, openly hostile, or will not engage at all.
Moderate (4) = noticeable resistance/guardedness during the interview, though some cooperation continues.

15Unusual Thought Content Delusions / strange beliefs

What it measures: Unusual, odd, strange, or bizarre content of thought — the actual ideas/beliefs (the "what," whereas item 4 is the "how").

What to look for: Delusions of any type (control, reference, thought broadcasting, bizarre beliefs). Rate by how strange and how firmly held the beliefs are.

  • Mild (3): Unusual ideas that the person questions; odd but not fixed.
  • Moderate (4): Clearly abnormal beliefs held with some conviction, but partial doubt remains.
  • Severe (6–7): Firmly held bizarre delusions that dominate thinking and may drive behaviour.
Moderate (4) = definitely abnormal beliefs are present and fairly convinced, but not yet fully fixed/bizarre.

16Blunted Affect Reduced emotional expression

What it measures: Reduced emotional tone — diminished facial expression, voice tone, and gestures (how much feeling is shown).

What to look for: Flat or monotone voice, expressionless face, few gestures, reduced emotional range during the interview.

  • Mild (3): Slightly reduced expressiveness.
  • Moderate (4): Clearly reduced facial/vocal expression; emotional range is noticeably narrowed.
  • Severe (6–7): Almost no emotional expression — flat, masklike, monotone throughout.
Moderate (4) = emotional expression is obviously diminished, though brief flashes of feeling still appear.

17Excitement Heightened activity / mood

What it measures: Heightened emotional tone, increased reactivity, agitation, elevated or irritable mood, and over-activity.

What to look for: Loud/rapid speech, overactivity, heightened intensity, euphoria or irritability, pressured manner. (Added in the 18-item version.)

  • Mild (3): Slightly more animated or intense than normal.
  • Moderate (4): Clearly heightened activity/mood — louder, faster, more intense, but still controllable.
  • Severe (6–7): Markedly agitated/over-excited; very difficult to calm or interrupt.
Moderate (4) = the raised energy/mood is clearly above normal and noticeable, but the person can still be redirected.

18Disorientation Confusion about time/place/person

What it measures: Confusion or lack of correct awareness of time, place, or person. (Added in the 18-item version.)

What to look for: Errors about the date, day, location, or identity of people; general confusion about surroundings.

  • Mild (3): Minor uncertainty (e.g., unsure of the exact date).
  • Moderate (4): Clear errors in one or two areas (e.g., wrong date and unsure of place) but core orientation intact.
  • Severe (6–7): Disoriented in multiple spheres — wrong about time, place, and person.
Moderate (4) = definite, obvious orientation errors are present, but the person is not completely confused.

How to Score Overall & Interpret

  • Each item: choose one number (1–7) that best fits the most severe level of that symptom over the rating period.
  • Total score: add all 18 items. Minimum = 18 (all "not present"), maximum = 126 (all "extremely severe"). Higher = more severe overall illness.
  • Rough interpretation of totals (guideline only — clinical judgement matters more): around 18–31 = minimal/no symptoms; ~32–41 = mild; ~42–52 = moderate; 53+ = marked/severe.
  • Tracking change: the BPRS is most useful when repeated over time — a falling total suggests improvement (e.g., response to medication).

Key rule for "moderate" (4): across all items, a score of 4 marks the point where the symptom is definitely and clearly present, causes some distress or interference, but is not yet dominating the person's functioning. It is the common cut-off for "clinically significant."

Important: The BPRS should be administered and scored by a trained clinician. The anchor descriptions above are simplified summaries to explain each item and the meaning of each severity level (especially "moderate"). For formal/research use, always follow the full official manual.
