Couples Therapy Worksheets — Clinician's Guide (How to Use)

Based on: Handbook of Couples Therapy, edited by Michele Harway (John Wiley & Sons, 2005).

This worksheet series translates the clinical frameworks described across the Handbook of Couples Therapy into a usable session-by-session toolkit. It integrates the book's three organizing perspectives — the developmental (life-cycle stages), the theoretical (CBCT, EFT, Narrative, Strategic/Solution-Focused, Object Relations, PAIRS, Bowen, Feminist/Contextual), and the situational (illness, abuse, aggression, addiction, infidelity, divorce). Content paraphrased and reorganized from the source for clinical use; it is not a verbatim reproduction.

1. Who this is for

Licensed or supervised mental-health professionals (MFTs, psychologists, counselors, social workers) conducting conjoint couples therapy. The worksheets assume the clinician holds the clinical judgment described in Harway's Chapter 1 ("Setting the Stage"): couples work requires specific training beyond generalist practice, because partners arrive with different family-of-origin "daily living habits," expectations, values, and cultural backgrounds that must be actively bridged.

2. The arc of treatment at a glance

The Handbook does not prescribe a single rigid session count. Instead it describes a recognizable arc that recurs across every chapter's approach. These worksheets map that arc into a phased sequence. A typical course runs 12–20 sessions; brief/solution-focused work compresses it, EFT and object-relations work may extend it.

3. How to use the worksheets with couples

General principles (synthesized from the book):

  1. Lead with the alliance, not the forms. Harway and the PAIRS chapter both warn that the standard "50-minute hour simply cannot build the trust, connectedness, and collaboration needed" at the start. Use longer early sessions where possible and let the worksheet serve the conversation — never read it like a questionnaire.
  2. Stay non-blaming and multidirectionally partial. Across chapters (PAIRS, EFT, Strategic, Feminist/Contextual) the stance is that the therapist does not take sides; the therapist reframes blame as a cycle both partners are caught in, and holds "goodwill and positive intent."
  3. Assess before you intervene. Every theoretical chapter opens with assessment. The CBCT chapter is explicit: "Before any clinical theory or technique is applied, a functional assessment should be conducted to guide treatment planning." Do not hand out a skills worksheet (Phase 3+) before completing Phase 1–2.
  4. Screen for safety first. Chapters 16 (sexual abuse) and 17 (physical aggression) require that you rule out or assess violence and trauma early; conjoint communication work can be contraindicated or must be sequenced carefully when aggression is present. See 07.
  5. Weave culture and context through everything. Per Chapter 1 and the Feminist/Contextual chapter, cultural, gender, socioeconomic, and spiritual differences are woven into each worksheet rather than isolated — prompts invite these explicitly.
  6. Match the worksheet to the model you practice. The worksheets are modular. A CBCT clinician will lean on 06; an EFT clinician on 05; a solution-focused clinician will use the "exception/miracle/scaling" prompts in 03 and 09. Pick what fits your orientation and the couple.

Practical mechanics:

  • Give each partner their own copy of worksheets that ask for individual reflection (so neither sees the other's answers until invited).
  • Use the joint worksheets as a shared, in-session artifact you fill in together on a shared surface.
  • Assign between-session practice from 09 at the end of every session and review it at the start of the next.
  • Keep completed worksheets in the clinical file; they become the progress record and the material for the termination review (08).

5. Important clinical notes & limitations

  • These are clinical tools, not a treatment manual or a substitute for training/supervision. Use within your scope of practice.
  • Safety overrides sequence. If screening reveals ongoing intimate-partner violence, active addiction, acute risk, or untreated trauma, pause the standard arc and follow the relevant module in 07 and your jurisdiction's standards.
  • The Handbook is a 2005 multi-author text. Where you use a specific model (EFT, PAIRS, Gottman-informed CBCT, etc.), consult that model's current primary manuals for fidelity.
  • Document informed consent, no-secrets policy (how you handle individually disclosed information), and limits of confidentiality before the individual sessions in 02.

Worksheet 01 — First Session: Joint Intake, Safety Screen & Alliance

Phase 1 — Assessment & Alliance · Session 1 Source basis: Harway (Ed.), Handbook of Couples Therapy — Ch. 1 (Setting the Stage), Ch. 2 (PAIRS premarital assessment), Ch. 8 (CBCT functional assessment), Ch. 16–17 (safety screening).

How to use this worksheet (clinician instructions)

  • Purpose of Session 1: establish the alliance, understand the couple's shared story, run a safety screen, and set the frame — not to solve problems or assign skills yet.
  • Format: Conjoint (both partners present). If your setting allows, book a longer first session (90–120 min). The PAIRS chapter stresses that the standard 50-minute hour "cannot build the trust, connectedness, and collaboration" an initial inquiry needs.
  • Stance: Warm, curious, multidirectionally partial (both partners feel joined). Reframe "who's at fault" into "what's the pattern." Keep a non-blaming attitude throughout.
  • Fill it in together on a shared surface for Parts B–D; complete Part A (safety) using your clinical interview and, if needed, brief separate check-ins.
  • End the session by setting up the individual sessions (Worksheet 02) and explaining your no-secrets / confidentiality policy.

Worksheet 02 — Individual History Sessions

Phase 1 — Assessment & Alliance · Sessions 2–3 (one per partner) Source basis: Harway (Ed.), Handbook of Couples Therapy — Ch. 2 (PAIRS individual-interview format), Ch. 7 (Bowen: family-of-origin, differentiation), Ch. 9 (Object Relations: attachment patterns), Ch. 11 (EFT: attachment fears/needs), Ch. 14 (Feminist/Contextual: gendered expectations).

How to use this worksheet (clinician instructions)

  • Purpose: Gather each partner's individual social and emotional history without the other's presence. The PAIRS chapter describes two-hour individual interviews that "open windows to new understandings that have not previously been realized or considered."
  • Format: Individual session, one partner at a time. Aim for 60–120 min.
  • Stance: Empathic, curious, non-judgmental. This is an opportunity for the individual to disclose what might be uncomfortable to share in a joint session.
  • Confidentiality: Begin by restating your no-secrets policy (decided in Session 1). Make clear how information shared here will and won't be used in conjoint sessions.
  • What you're assessing: Family conditioning, attachment styles, models from the parental marriage, invisible loyalties, expectations of the relationship, sexual/abuse history, personal hopes and fears, and individual contributing factors.
  • Give each partner their own copy. They may fill in some answers before the session or you may complete it interview-style.
  • Safety check: This is the best time to check for domestic violence indicators privately — ask directly.

Worksheet 03 — Formulation, Feedback & Treatment Plan

Phase 2 — Formulation & Goals · Session 3–4 (joint feedback session) Source basis: Harway (Ed.), Handbook of Couples Therapy — Ch. 2 (PAIRS feedback session), Ch. 8 (CBCT functional assessment ➜ treatment plan), Ch. 11 (EFT cycle de-escalation formulation), Ch. 12 (Strategic/Solution-Focused: miracle question & scaling).

How to use this worksheet (clinician instructions)

  • Purpose: Share with the couple your understanding of their pattern ("the cycle"), set collaborative goals, and establish the treatment plan. This is the bridge between assessment and active intervention.
  • Format: Joint session, ideally 90 min.
  • Stance: Present the formulation as a shared cycle — not either partner's fault. The PAIRS chapter models "relabeling and reframing many past behaviors and intentions, pointing out how blame is often not the issue." The EFT chapter describes mapping the negative cycle so both partners can "see through the smoke" and recognize they are each caught in it.
  • How to deliver: Walk through each section aloud; write or draw the cycle diagram together on paper or whiteboard. Ask the couple to correct, add, or adjust — this is collaborative, not the therapist "announcing a verdict."
  • Important: Only share individual-session material that a partner has agreed may be shared (per your no-secrets policy). Summarize themes, not verbatim disclosures.

Worksheet 04 — Communication & De-escalation Skills

Phase 3 — De-escalation & Communication · Sessions 4–8 (and reinforced throughout) Source basis: Harway (Ed.), Handbook of Couples Therapy — Ch. 2 (PAIRS Dialogue Guide, "complaining without blaming," I-messages), Ch. 8 (CBCT communication: Satir's placator/blamer/distractor/computer; Gordon's I-messages; speaker–listener), Ch. 11 (EFT de-escalation of the negative cycle), Ch. 15 (managing emotional reactivity).

How to use this worksheet (clinician instructions)

  • Purpose: First active-change phase. Interrupt the negative cycle and build core communication skills: speaking without blame, listening with empathy, and de-escalating reactivity. The CBCT chapter names communication, problem-solving, and behavior exchange as the "lynchpins" of effective couples intervention — this worksheet covers communication.
  • Format: Joint sessions. Teach a skill, then have the couple practice it live in the room (enactment) while you coach. Do not just explain — the EFT and Strategic chapters both emphasize in-session enactments over didactics.
  • Prerequisite: Complete Phase 1–2 and the safety screen. If physical aggression is present, see Worksheet 07 before doing hot-topic communication work; teach the Time-Out tool first and keep topics low-intensity.
  • Pacing: Start with low-conflict topics; raise intensity only as the couple demonstrates skill. The therapist uses "shifts in intensity" (slowing tone, volume, pacing) to keep the room workable (Minuchin/EFT principle).

Each partner circles the style they fall into most under stress (CBCT, Ch. 8, citing Satir):

Tool 2 — I-Messages / Complaining Without Blaming (PAIRS Dialogue Guide)

The PAIRS Dialogue Guide is "a communication tool for complaining without blaming." Convert blame-statements into I-messages.

Formula:

"When ______ (specific situation/behavior), I felt ______ (emotion), because ______ (meaning/need). What I'd like is ______ (request)."

Practice conversions:

Blame statement ("you" message)I-message rewrite
"You never help around here."
"You always ignore me."
"You're so controlling."
(couple's own example)

Rule from PAIRS: "Express important hurt, fear, or irritation directly to each other in words, asking to be heard and understood with empathy. What is left unsaid is often more harmful than what is said."

Tool 3 — Speaker–Listener Technique (CBCT)

A structured turn-taking format that slows reactivity.

Speaker rules:

  • Speak for yourself (use I-messages)
  • Keep it brief — a few sentences, then pause
  • Don't mind-read or accuse

Listener rules:

  • Don't rebut, defend, or problem-solve
  • Paraphrase back what you heard: "What I hear you saying is…"
  • Check: "Did I get that right?"
  • Only then does the Speaker continue or hand over the floor

Tool 4 — Empathic Listening Checklist

For the Listener (adapted from PAIRS listening skills):

  • ☐ I made eye contact / turned toward my partner
  • ☐ I did not interrupt
  • ☐ I paraphrased what I heard before responding
  • ☐ I checked whether I understood correctly
  • ☐ I acknowledged my partner's feeling, even if I disagree with the content
  • ☐ I set aside my rebuttal to truly hear them first
PAIRS competency: "Give up being right. Invite and express diversity. Welcome differences as sources of vigor, perspective, and healthy growth."

Worksheet 05 — Emotion & Attachment Restructuring (EFT-informed)

Phase 4 — Restructuring the Bond · Sessions 8–14 Source basis: Harway (Ed.), Handbook of Couples Therapy — Ch. 11 (EFT: emotion, attachment, enactments, softening, attachment injuries), Ch. 9 (Object Relations: projective identification, internalized models), Ch. 15 (emotional reactivity).

How to use this worksheet (clinician instructions)

  • Purpose: Move from de-escalating the cycle (Phase 3) to restructuring the emotional bond. EFT frames couple distress as insecurity in the attachment bond: rigid interaction patterns organized by "absorbing affect states." The task is to access the softer, underlying emotions and attachment needs that drive the surface behavior, and to create new bonding interactions.
  • When to use: After the couple has achieved some de-escalation (they can talk about the cycle without the cycle taking over). EFT describes this as moving beyond "critical pursuit followed by distance and defensiveness."
  • Format: Joint sessions. Heavy use of in-session enactments — you direct partners to turn and speak the newly accessed emotion to each other, not to you.
  • Stance: Clients are the experts on their own experience; do not pathologize or label. "They are viewed as struggling with problems arising from a particular social context." Validate, reflect, and heighten emotion gently.
  • Caution: Deep emotional work can surface trauma or attachment injuries (including infidelity or abuse). Have Worksheet 07 ready; pace to the couple's window of tolerance.

Tool 1 — Secondary vs. Primary Emotion Map

Surface (secondary/reactive) emotions protect us but drive the negative cycle. Underneath are primary (vulnerable) emotions and attachment needs.

Partner APartner B

Primary emotion (underneath — hurt, fear, loneliness, shame, longing)
The attachment question underneath (e.g., "Are you there for me? Do I matter to you? Will you leave?")

Clinician prompt to access primary emotion: "When you [withdraw/criticize] in that moment… just underneath the [anger/silence], what's the softer feeling? What are you afraid of?"

Tool 3 — Enactment Script (the heart of EFT change events)

Direct the couple to turn toward each other and speak the newly accessed emotion. Use this as a scaffold:

Pursuer softening / Withdrawer re-engaging:

  1. Partner, turn to [name]. Can you tell them: "When this happens, underneath I feel ______ (primary emotion)."
  2. "What I really need from you in those moments is ______."
  3. "It's hard for me to say this because ______."

Listener's response (coach them):

  • "When I hear you say that, I feel ______."
  • "What I want you to know is ______."
  • "I want to be there for you by ______."

Tool 4 — Attachment Injury Repair

An "attachment injury" is a moment of betrayal or abandonment at a critical time (e.g., not being there during a loss, an affair, a broken promise) that becomes a recurring wound blocking trust. EFT describes specific work on these injuries.

Identify the injury:

  • What happened, and when? _______________________________________
  • Why was this moment so critical for the injured partner? ________________
  • What did it come to mean about the relationship / your safety? ___________

Repair steps (guide the couple through, in order):

  1. Injured partner stays with and expresses the pain and its attachment meaning (not just the facts).
  2. Other partner stays present, hears the impact without defending, and acknowledges the hurt they caused.
  3. Other partner expresses genuine remorse and understanding of the injury's significance.
  4. Injured partner begins to risk asking for what they need now to feel safe.
  5. The couple creates a new, corrective bonding moment.
Links to PAIRS competency: "Seek forgiveness for hurts by taking responsibility, repairing damages, and expressing regret. Let go of grudges and choose to forgive."

Worksheet 06 — Cognitive Restructuring, Problem-Solving & Behavior Exchange (CBCT)

Phase 4 — Restructuring the Bond · Sessions 6–14 (interwoven) Source basis: Harway (Ed.), Handbook of Couples Therapy — Ch. 8 (Cognitive Behavioral Couple Therapy: functional assessment, communication, problem-solving, behavior exchange, attributions, caring days/acceptance & commitment), Ch. 12 (solution-focused exceptions).

How to use this worksheet (clinician instructions)

  • Purpose: The CBCT chapter identifies three "lynchpins" of effective couples intervention: communication (Worksheet 04), problem-solving, and behavior exchange — plus the cognitive layer of attributions and beliefs. This worksheet covers the cognitive and problem-solving/behavioral components.
  • Grounding principle: "Before any clinical theory or technique is applied, a functional assessment should be conducted to guide treatment planning." Make sure Phase 1–2 is complete.
  • Format: Joint sessions; teach, model, and practice in-session, then assign as homework. CBCT is explicitly skills- and practice-oriented.
  • Balance: The chapter warns that behavioral work alone has been stereotyped as "cold and sterile." Pair these tools with the emotional and relationship focus of Worksheet 05 so the couple experiences warmth, not mechanics. Enhanced CBCT encompasses "personal characteristics, past history, and the total context of the couple's environment."
  • Match to orientation: This worksheet is the core toolkit for CBCT-oriented clinicians and a useful supplement for others.

How to use this worksheet (clinician instructions)

  • When to use: When the safety screen (Worksheet 01, Part A) or individual sessions (Worksheet 02) identify any of these issues.

Module A — Couples Facing Illness / Medical Concerns (Ch. 15)

Core framework: The interaction between biological and emotional — illness disrupts the couple's roles, intimacy, and emotional regulation.

Assessment questions:

Module B — Sexual-Abuse History (Ch. 16)

Core framework: A history of sexual abuse in one partner "may affect the overall couple's functioning and may lead to difficult therapeutic concerns."

Safety-first sequencing:

"If sexual abuse is ruled out at intake, the standard clinical assessment protocol for couples may be implemented." If abuse IS present: individual trauma assessment and stabilization come first or alongside.

Assessment questions (with the individual partner — Worksheet 02):

  1. Has the survivor received or is currently in individual trauma therapy? ☐ Y ☐ N

Intervention focus:

  • Psychoeducation: both partners learn how trauma affects the nervous system and relational behavior
  • Pacing: go slow with emotional vulnerability (Worksheet 05) — stay within the survivor's window of tolerance
  • Address the non-abused partner's feelings (helplessness, frustration) without centering them
  • Refer for individual trauma work if not already in place
  • Build a safety plan for triggered moments (link to Worksheet 04, Time-Out)
