How to read these notes

So you can revise a whole 5-mark answer from one topic.

What the sections are

  • Each numbered section is one therapy from the syllabus. It is written as a 5-mark exam answer in full but simple sentences, so you can revise straight from the point and write it back in the exam.
  • Every topic opens with a one-line summary. It then has a flowchart that pictures the core idea, a short definition box from well-known bodies and classic authors, the answer points, an 'at a glance' box, and a mnemonic that packs the key points into one memorable word.

Introduction to Affective Psychotherapies

Before the fourteen therapies, here is a simple map of the whole area. Read this once and the rest of the unit will make much more sense, because you will see what all these therapies are trying to do and how they differ.

What 'affective psychotherapies' means here

  • The word affective (to do with feelings and emotions) simply points to the heart of these therapies. They work mainly through emotion, insight, the relationship and lived experience, by talking and relating, not by giving medicines and not mainly by drilling new habits. In plain words, they help you by changing how you feel, how you understand yourself, and how you relate to others.
  • So an affective (talk-and-relationship) therapy is a planned, caring conversation. A trained therapist and a client meet regularly, and the healing comes from understanding feelings, from facing them, and from a trusting bond, rather than from a pill or a reward chart.
  • These therapies sit apart from two neighbours. They differ from the purely behavioural approach, which changes behaviour through conditioning and rewards, and from the medical approach, which treats the brain with drugs. In practice the lines blur, and many therapists mix ideas, but the affective group leads with feeling, meaning and relationship.

What the fourteen therapies share

  • They all rely on the therapeutic relationship (the trusting, caring bond between client and therapist, which is itself part of the cure). Decades of research show this bond, called the working alliance, is one of the strongest predictors of whether therapy helps, whatever the method. Think of it as the soil: the technique is the seed, but without good soil little grows.
  • They all treat emotion as information, not noise. A feeling is read as a message about an unmet need, a hidden conflict or a blocked goal, so the work is to feel it, understand it and let it change, instead of just switching it off.
  • They all aim at lasting inner change, not only quick symptom relief. The hope is more self-understanding, more freedom to choose, and healthier ways of relating, so the gains keep working after therapy ends.
  • They all give the client an active part. The client is a partner who explores, chooses and practises, not a passive patient who is simply 'treated'.

How the fourteen broadly group

  • The fourteen fall into a few natural families. Knowing the family tells you, at a glance, what a therapy will emphasise:
    • Insight / depth therapies look inward and backward to uncover hidden conflict: psychodynamic therapy (and its briefer offshoots).
    • Humanistic-experiential therapies trust the person's drive to grow and work in the here-and-now: humanistic, person-centred, existential and gestalt therapy.
    • The Adlerian approach stands a little apart, mixing early insight with practical encouragement and a strong social focus.
    • Brief and integrative therapies are short, focused and practical: brief psychotherapy, which borrows from many schools around one agreed problem.
    • Supportive therapy strengthens coping and steadies the person, rather than digging deep: supportive psychotherapy.
    • Action and expressive therapies heal through doing, playing and enacting: play therapy (for children) and psychodrama (acting out conflicts in a group).
    • Altered-state and mind-body approaches work through focused attention, relaxation and the body: clinical hypnotherapy and the oriental approaches such as yoga, meditation and tai chi.
The one idea to carry through the whole unit
  • Across all fourteen, two engines do the real work: a genuine, trusting relationship and some form of emotional change (feeling, understanding or re-living an emotion so it loosens its grip). Every technique in this unit is, in the end, a particular way of switching on one of these two engines.
  • So as you read each therapy, keep asking three questions: what is its main focus (its central idea), what are its techniques (what the therapist actually does in the room), and how does it work (why that changes the client). Those three threads run through every topic below.

How to use this unit

  • Each of the fourteen numbered topics is a self-contained study note on one therapy. You can revise any one on its own, because each gives the main focus, the basis, how a case is understood, the goals, the techniques in detail, the process or stages, the outcome, who it suits, and its research standing.

1Psychodynamic psychotherapy

Psychodynamic psychotherapy is the modern, shorter form of Freud's psychoanalysis. It sees a symptom as a clue to a hidden mental struggle, not a random fault. The therapist uses the relationship, free talking and gentle explaining to bring that hidden struggle into the open, so lasting change can happen.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology describes psychodynamic psychotherapy (therapy that works with hidden mental conflicts and with the bond between client and therapist) as a therapy that grew out of psychoanalysis. It works with unconscious processes (mental activity going on outside your awareness, like a motive you don't notice) that still shape how you act today. The aim is self-awareness and seeing how the past shapes the present. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Theoretical view Freud started psychoanalysis with Breuer's 'talking cure'. He moved on from hypnosis and catharsis (releasing bottled-up emotion so it stops pressing on you) to free association and interpretation. He summed up its aim in one line: where id was, there ego shall be (the sensible self should take charge). [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • Theoretical view Later schools widened the approach into ego psychology, object relations, self psychology and attachment. So modern psychodynamic therapy is face-to-face and briefer than classical analysis, while keeping the same core ideas. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is the hidden, unconscious part of the mind. Psychodynamic therapy holds that much of what drives us sits below awareness, like the bulk of an iceberg under the water. Old conflicts and feelings we pushed away still pull the strings today and show up as symptoms.
  • So the whole therapy aims at one thing: bring the hidden into the open. When a buried conflict is understood and felt in words, it loses its grip, and the person is freed to choose instead of being driven. Freud summed it up as 'where id was, there ego shall be' — the sensible, choosing self takes charge of the blind, wanting part.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • The change engine is insight (the 'aha' moment of seeing a hidden pattern or feeling in yourself clearly for the first time) plus a safe relationship. When a client finally sees and feels what was buried — say, that their constant people-pleasing hides a fear of being abandoned — the old pattern can relax.
  • Crucially, insight is not just understanding in the head. The client must re-live the old feeling in the room, usually toward the therapist, and have it meet a new, accepting response. That gap between the dreaded reaction and the real, kind one is what loosens the knot.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, the approach begins with Freud and Breuer and the case of Anna O., the so-called talking cure. From it Freud built psychoanalysis, moving from hypnosis and catharsis toward free association and dream interpretation. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • Its later origin runs through Jung, Adler and Klein, plus Anna Freud, Winnicott, Kohut and Bowlby. Object relations, self psychology and attachment all grew from this one root into today's briefer, face-to-face forms. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • The first basis is psychic determinism (the idea that nothing in the mind is a pure accident, so every symptom has a cause). Behaviour and symptoms have meaning, so even a slip of the tongue is read as a clue, not chance. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • A central basis is the unconscious. The mind is split into conscious, preconscious and unconscious, and into the id, ego and superego. The id is the blind 'I want it now' part; the superego is the strict inner parent that judges; the ego is the referee that keeps peace between the two and the real world. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • The developmental basis is the psychosexual stages: oral, anal, phallic, latency and genital. Too much frustration or too much indulgence can cause fixation (getting stuck at an early stage, like a part of you never fully growing up), which later shapes personality and symptoms. [Nancy McWilliams, Psychoanalytic Diagnosis]
  • Anxiety and the defence mechanisms are another basis. A defence is an automatic mental trick that keeps a painful feeling out of awareness. Repression buries it, denial refuses it, projection sees your own anger in someone else, and sublimation channels it into something useful like sport. [Nancy McWilliams, Psychoanalytic Diagnosis]
  • In formulation, a symptom is read as a compromise formation (a symptom that half-satisfies a hidden wish and half-hides it, like a nervous habit that both shows and masks a worry). Picture a tug-of-war between a forbidden wish, the conscience that forbids it, and reality; the symptom is the knot where the rope stops moving. [Nancy McWilliams, Psychoanalytic Diagnosis]
  • The guiding goal is to make the unconscious conscious. The client gains insight into hidden conflicts and patterns, the ego grows stronger, and rigid defences mature into flexible ones. This brings lasting change, not just the quick removal of one symptom. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • The therapist's stance is a key procedure. It combines neutrality (not taking sides between the client's wishes, defences and conscience), restraint and staying fairly private. The therapist acts partly as a blank screen, so the client's old relationship patterns show up clearly in the room. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • The foundational technique is free association (saying whatever comes to mind without filtering it, even if it feels silly, rude or random). How it is done: the client lies back or sits and simply reports each passing thought. Why it helps: the mind cannot wander freely for long before it bumps into the very conflict it is avoiding, and the pauses, jokes and sudden topic-changes mark exactly where it hides. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • Dream analysis is a classic technique, called the royal road to the unconscious. How it is done: the client tells the dream, then free-associates to each part of it. The therapist separates the remembered manifest content (the surface story) from the hidden latent content (the real wish behind it). Example: a dream of missing a train may, on association, lead to a fear of missing out on life. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • Interpretation is the therapist's main tool. How it is done: at the right moment, gently and tentatively, the therapist puts into words a hidden meaning the client is near to seeing — never a clever lecture, always a well-timed nudge. Why it helps: naming the buried feeling lets the thinking ego take hold of it instead of being secretly ruled by it. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Analysis of resistance is a technique in its own right. Resistance (anything the client does, often without meaning to, that blocks the therapy — arriving late, going blank, changing the subject) is not a nuisance but a signpost. How it is done: the therapist points out the block gently and wonders aloud what feeling it is guarding, because the strongest resistance usually sits right on top of the most important material. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Working with transference is the deepest technique. Transference (reacting to the therapist as if they were an important figure from your past, such as a parent) turns the session into a live replay of the client's old relationships. How it is done: the therapist notices the client treating them as, say, a critical father, names it kindly, and links it to the past; the client then gets a different, accepting response in place of the dreaded one. Why it helps: the old pattern is corrected in real feeling, not just in talk. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Working-through is the technique that makes change stick. How it is done: the same insight is returned to again and again, in many situations, over many sessions. Why it is needed: a single 'aha' rarely shifts a lifelong habit, just as understanding why you bite your nails does not stop you the first time — only patient repetition rewrites the pattern. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • In stages and process the work moves through three phases. It starts with an opening phase of assessment, agreeing the frame (times, fee, rules) and building trust. Then transference and resistance appear and are worked through in a long middle phase. Finally a planned termination reviews and locks in the gains and handles the feelings about ending. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • The outcome sought is fewer symptoms, more insight, more flexible defences and healthier relationships. A striking finding is the sleeper effect: gains often keep growing even after therapy ends, because the client carries on self-analysing. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Indications favour clients with good ego strength, curiosity about their own mind and real motivation, facing neurotic, personality or relationship problems. Acute psychosis, high suicide risk and raw crisis states are contraindications better met first by supportive work. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • In research and current status, well-known reviews (for example Shedler's) find psychodynamic therapy gives substantial, lasting benefit. Short-term forms help depression and anxiety, and specialised forms such as transference-focused and mentalisation-based therapy help borderline personality disorder, while classical long analysis stays hard to study with trials. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Psychodynamic therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Freud and Breuer, extended by ego psychology, object relations, self psychology and attachment.
  • Basis: psychic determinism, the unconscious, id-ego-superego, psychosexual stages, defences.
  • Formulation: the symptom is a compromise formation that stands for a hidden conflict.
  • Technique: free association, dream analysis, interpretation, analysis of resistance and transference.
  • Process: opening, transference, working through, termination; best for ego-strong neurotic clients.

Free association, Interpretation, Dream analysis, (analysis of) resistance and transferO, Working through and Termination — the techniques and stages of psychodynamic therapy; the aim throughout is to make the unconscious conscious.

2Brief psychotherapy

Brief psychotherapy is planned, time-limited treatment. Therapist and client agree on one problem to work on, and the therapist stays active and directive. The fixed deadline is used on purpose to fire up motivation. The aim is to ease symptoms and restore functioning, not to remake the whole personality.

Characterisations by classic authors and models
  • Theoretical view Brief therapies share a planned time limit, a single focus and an active therapist. They rest on a well-known finding: most improvement happens early, with about half of clients better within roughly eight sessions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Theoretical view Named models include Malan, Sifneos, Mann and Davanloo, each with their own short, focused dynamic therapy. Alongside them sit interpersonal therapy and solution-focused brief therapy. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Guideline The World Health Organization promotes brief, structured psychological help in routine care. It frames this as real help given through respectful, collaborative talk within a few sessions. [World Health Organization, mhGAP Intervention Guide]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is one problem, worked on fast, against a clock. Brief therapy does not try to remake the whole personality. It picks a single agreed theme — a loss, a stuck role, a current conflict — and keeps every session pointed at it.
  • Its boldest idea is that the deadline itself heals. A fixed end date is not a limitation forced by money or waiting lists; it is used on purpose, like a deadline that makes you finally finish an essay. It concentrates the mind, raises motivation and stops therapy drifting.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • The engine is focus plus pressure plus hope. By refusing to wander, the therapist lets the client make real progress on one thing; the looming end date keeps urgency high; and in the solution-focused versions, the belief that small changes snowball gives the client confidence to start.
  • There is also a practical truth behind it, called the dose-response curve: most of the improvement people ever get from therapy happens in the first handful of sessions. Brief therapy simply builds its whole design around that fact instead of fighting it.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, brief therapy grows from Ferenczi and Rank's active, time-limited technique in the 1920s. It also grows from Alexander and French's idea of a corrective emotional experience in 1946. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Its origin then branches into many models. The psychodynamic stream has Malan, Sifneos, Mann and Davanloo; the systemic stream has de Shazer and Berg's solution-focused therapy. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A practical origin driver was service pressure. Cost, long waiting lists and the proof that most gains come early all pushed clinicians toward shorter, planned treatment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is that the time limit is therapeutic. A known end date lights a fire under the work. It concentrates effort and guards against the drift and over-dependence that an open-ended course can breed. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A second basis is a tight focus (one agreed problem or theme that the whole therapy is built around). The therapist is active and directive, steering sessions back to the one theme instead of chasing every new worry the client raises. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A third basis is hope about change. The work stresses the here-and-now and the client's strengths. It assumes a small change can set off bigger ones — like one tidy shelf prompting you to tidy a whole room — especially in the solution-focused models. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In formulation the therapist builds a focal formulation. This names one recurring relationship pattern, a current loss or an unresolved conflict tied to the present problem, and keeps the work on target. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A well-known formulation grid is interpersonal therapy's four problem areas: grief, role disputes (an ongoing clash in a key relationship), role transitions (a hard life change) and interpersonal deficits (few or poor relationships). One of these is picked as the single focus. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The goals are modest and clear: resolve the focal problem, relieve symptoms and restore how the person was functioning before. The therapist also teaches skills for later, rather than rebuilding the personality. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A core procedure is careful patient selection, because brief work does not suit everyone. Sifneos' criteria look for one clear complaint, at least one meaningful past relationship, curiosity about the mind, motivation and the ability to bear some anxiety. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The contract is itself a technique. How it is done: at the very first meeting, therapist and client fix the number of sessions (usually 6 to 20, often twelve to sixteen) and the exact end date. Why it helps: naming the finish line turns time into a shared tool both people work against. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Maintaining the focus is a constant technique. How it is done: whenever the client drifts to a new worry, the therapist gently and repeatedly brings the talk back to the agreed theme. Why it helps: depth on one problem in a short time beats shallow coverage of many. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Focused interpretation and selective attention are the dynamic techniques. How it is done: the therapist comments only on material that touches the focus, lets the rest pass, and gently challenges the client's defences around that one theme while using their warm feelings toward the therapist to keep them engaged. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The miracle question is the signature solution-focused technique. How it is done: the therapist asks, miracle question ('Suppose you woke tomorrow and the problem had vanished overnight — what would be the first small sign that told you?'). Why it helps: it quietly moves the client from staring at the problem to picturing the solution in concrete, doable steps. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Scaling and exception questions complete the solution-focused kit. Scaling asks 'on a scale of 0 to 10, where are you today, and what would 1 point up look like?', making progress measurable. Exception-hunting asks 'when was the problem a little less — what was different then?', so the client's own past successes become the blueprint. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In stages and process the work runs in three parts. First is assessment and selection in the early sessions. Then an active middle phase on the focus, with a mid-point review. Finally an explicit termination that locks in the gains. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A defining process feature is that termination is discussed from the start. The ending is planned, the gains are reviewed, and a booster or follow-up session may be arranged, so therapy does not just trail off. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome is symptom relief and better functioning. Many comparisons find brief therapy as effective as longer therapy for common problems like depression, anxiety and adjustment difficulties. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Indications are sharp, focal problems: adjustment disorders, mild to moderate depression and anxiety, grief and crises, in motivated clients. Chronic, many-sided conditions and severe high-risk states are contraindications. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status the evidence is strong for interpersonal therapy and brief CBT, and supportive for short-term psychodynamic and solution-focused work. Brief models are now standard in primary care, student counselling and telehealth. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
Brief psychotherapy at a glance
  • Origin: Ferenczi and Rank, Alexander and French; models by Malan, Sifneos, Mann, Davanloo, de Shazer.
  • Basis: the time limit is therapeutic; a single focus; an active, directive therapist.
  • Formulation: a focal theme; interpersonal therapy's grief, disputes, transitions and deficits.
  • Technique: selection criteria, a fixed contract, focused interpretation, solution-focused questions.
  • Process: assessment, focused middle phase, planned termination; strong evidence for IPT and brief CBT.
Mnemonic
FAST
Focus (one theme), Active and directive therapist, Selection (clear criteria) and Time limit (fixed end date) — the four features that define brief psychotherapy; the deadline itself is used to drive the work.

3Humanistic psychotherapy

Humanistic psychotherapy is the 'third force' in psychology. It is a growth-focused approach that trusts each person's natural drive to grow. It centres on how things feel from the inside, on free choice, and on the whole person. The genuine relationship, not clever technique, is what mostly heals.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology describes humanistic therapy (an approach that stresses growth, free choice and treating the whole person, not just symptoms) as a therapy from humanistic psychology. It emphasises the person's capacity for growth and self-direction and the quality of the relationship. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Theoretical view The movement began as the third force in the 1950s and 1960s. It pushed back against the determinism of psychoanalysis and the machine-like view of behaviourism, and drew figures like Maslow, Rogers, May, Frankl and Perls. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Maslow's hierarchy of needs runs from food and safety up to self-actualisation (becoming the fullest, best version of yourself). It frames distress as what happens when growth needs get blocked. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is the person's own drive to grow. Humanistic therapy trusts that, given the right conditions, every person naturally moves toward becoming their fullest, healthiest self — much as a plant grows toward the light when it has soil, water and sun.
  • So the therapist does not diagnose and fix a broken machine. The focus is on the whole person — their inner point of view, their freedom and the here-and-now — and on providing a warm, honest relationship in which the stalled growth can start up again.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • Change works through a safe, genuine relationship that releases the blocked growth drive. When a person feels truly accepted and understood, their defences drop, they dare to look at feelings they had hidden, and the natural push toward growth resumes on its own.
  • There is no clever technique doing the work; the relationship is the technique. It is like thawing — warmth does not force the ice to melt, it simply removes what was keeping it frozen, and the natural process takes over.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, humanistic therapy emerged in the 1950s and 1960s as the self-styled third force. It was a deliberate reaction against both psychoanalytic determinism and behaviourist mechanism. The Association for Humanistic Psychology was founded in 1961. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Its origin gathers several figures under one banner. Maslow, Rogers, May, Frankl and Perls all stressed growth and choice, and its roots lie in phenomenology and existential philosophy. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The first basis is that people are inherently good and growth-oriented. Each person carries an actualising tendency (an inborn drive to grow and become your best self, like a seed that naturally pushes toward the light) that moves them forward when conditions allow. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A second basis is the stress on subjective experience, free will and responsibility. The person's own point of view and the here-and-now are the proper focus, rather than drives or conditioning. Put simply, it matters more how life feels from the inside to this person than how it looks on a test sheet. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A third basis is holism, the view of the whole person in context. The approach refuses to shrink a human being down to symptoms, reflexes or a diagnostic label. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • A fourth basis, from Maslow, is the hierarchy of needs. Physiological, safety, belonging and esteem needs build up toward self-actualisation. A blocked lower need stalls the climb — it is hard to chase your dreams when you feel unsafe or unloved — and that stalling is what shows up as distress. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In formulation, distress is seen as natural growth being blocked. It is blocked by a harsh environment, by conditions of worth (the feeling that you are only loved or valued when you meet other people's standards), or by losing touch with your own feelings. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A key formulation idea is that symptoms show disconnection from experience, not disease. So the aim is to restore awareness and self-direction, not to fix a defect. In plain terms, the person has drifted away from what they truly feel, and the symptom is the alarm bell. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The goals are self-awareness, self-acceptance and authenticity, plus personal growth. Therapy helps the person take responsibility and move toward self-actualisation in their own way. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A defining procedure is that the relationship itself is the main healer. A genuine, accepting, empathic presence matters more than any particular technique. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Empathic listening is the core technique. How it is done: the therapist listens for the feeling under the words and reflects it back accurately, so the client hears their own inner world said aloud. Why it helps: feeling deeply understood is, by itself, freeing — it tells the person they are not alone and need not hide. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Reflection and acceptance are the next techniques. How it is done: the therapist mirrors feelings without judging, advising or steering, following the client's lead rather than leading. Why it helps: unconditional acceptance dissolves the conditions of worth that first blocked the person's growth. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Focusing on the felt sense is an experiential technique (Gendlin). How it is done: the therapist invites the client to notice a vague bodily feeling — a tightness, a heaviness — and stay with it until words arrive. Example: attending to a knot in the stomach may slowly reveal an unnamed grief. Why it helps: emotion lives in the body, so the body is a doorway to feelings the mind has hidden. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Chair and experiential work round out the techniques. How it is done: the client speaks to an imagined part of themselves or another person, bringing a felt conflict into the room to be experienced, not just discussed. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In stages and process the work is client-led and unfolding, not staged by the therapist. It moves as the person feels safe enough to explore, deepen awareness and take back ownership of their experience. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A process hallmark is genuine presence and congruence from the therapist. This lowers the client's defences and frees the actualising tendency to resume its natural course. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is more self-esteem and emotional awareness, together with better relationships and a stronger inner sense of direction. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Indications cover mild to moderate distress, identity and relationship problems, depression and anxiety, and clients seeking growth. Severe psychosis or a need for structured skills training is better served, as a contraindication, by more directive work. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In research and current status, well-known meta-analyses by Elliott and colleagues find humanistic-experiential therapies give large, stable gains. They are broadly equal to CBT in many comparisons. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The approach stays alive in modern forms like emotion-focused therapy and motivational interviewing. Critics still say its concepts are vague, hard to test and perhaps too rosy about human nature. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Humanistic therapy at a glance
  • Origin: the 1950s to 1960s third force; Maslow, Rogers, May, Frankl, Perls; phenomenological roots.
  • Basis: innate goodness and an actualising tendency; subjective experience, free will, the whole person.
  • Formulation: distress is blocked growth, conditions of worth and disconnection from experience.
  • Technique: empathic listening, reflection, unconditional acceptance; relationship over technique.
  • Outcome and research: growth and congruence; Elliott et al. show large gains, equivalent to CBT.
Mnemonic
GROWTH
Growth-oriented view, Relationship as the agent of change, Own actualising tendency, Whole person, Third force and Here-and-now — the humanistic essentials; it trusts the person to move toward self-actualisation.

4Existential psychotherapy

Existential psychotherapy helps people face the hard facts of being alive: death, freedom, aloneness and the search for meaning. These facts stir up anxiety. Instead of dodging that anxiety through symptoms, the client meets it honestly and turns it into a more genuine, responsible and meaningful life.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Yalom builds existential therapy around four ultimate concerns (the unavoidable facts of life that stir up anxiety in everyone): death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness. Facing them causes anxiety, and dodging that anxiety is what turns into symptoms. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Frankl's logotherapy centres the will to meaning. His book Man's Search for Meaning, born from his time in a concentration camp, argues that meaning can be found even in suffering we cannot escape. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • Theoretical view The approach draws on philosophers Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger and Sartre, and on clinicians like Binswanger, Boss and May. This gives it a strongly philosophical flavour. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is facing the hard, unavoidable facts of being alive — that we will die, that we are free and responsible, that we are ultimately alone, and that life has no ready-made meaning. These facts stir up anxiety in everyone.
  • The central claim is that symptoms often come from dodging this anxiety rather than from a disease. So the therapy does not try to remove the anxiety; it helps the person stop running from it and instead live more honestly, more responsibly and with more meaning in its presence.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • Change comes from honest confrontation, not avoidance. When the client stops numbing or distracting themselves and squarely faces, say, the fact of death or their own freedom, the dodged anxiety stops leaking out as symptoms and becomes a spur to live more fully.
  • A simple example: a man who buries himself in overwork to avoid the thought of mortality may, once he faces it in therapy, choose to spend his evenings with his children instead. Facing the fact changed how he used his limited time. The awareness itself is what frees the choice.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, existential therapy has philosophical roots in Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger and Sartre. Its clinical roots are in Binswanger and Boss's Daseinsanalysis, which applied phenomenology to the suffering person. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Its clinical origin runs through Frankl's logotherapy from the 1930s, May's book Existence in 1958, and Yalom's Existential Psychotherapy in 1980, which organised the field for English-speaking clinicians. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • The first basis is that life brings unavoidable ultimate concerns. Death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness face everyone. They create an existential anxiety (the deep unease that comes from facing the basic facts of being alive) that cannot simply be cured away. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • A second basis is that existence precedes essence. In plain words, you are not born with a fixed self; you create yourself through your choices. So authenticity (living by your own real values and choices rather than just going along with others) stands against living a borrowed life. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A third basis, from Heidegger, is being-in-the-world across the physical, social and personal worlds. Frankl adds that a will to meaning — the hunger for life to mean something — is the deepest human drive, deeper than pleasure or power. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • In formulation, mental trouble is read as inauthentic living. It is the avoidance of freedom and responsibility, the loss of meaning, or the failure to cope with death and aloneness. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Frankl's distinctive formulation is the existential vacuum (a hollow feeling of emptiness and boredom that comes when life seems to have no meaning), which he called noogenic neurosis. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • The goals are to help clients face life's givens honestly. Therapy builds awareness, responsibility, authenticity and meaning, and encourages the person to act even while anxiety is present. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • Phenomenological exploration is the core procedure. How it is done: the therapist deliberately sets aside their own assumptions and asks open, curious questions to enter the client's own world exactly as the client sees it. Why it helps: only by seeing the world through the client's eyes can the real avoidances be found. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Using the here-and-now relationship is a technique in itself. How it is done: the therapist treats the bond in the room as a live sample of how the client meets others, and encourages 'I' statements ('I chose', 'I feel') in place of 'it happened to me'. Why it helps: owning your words is a first, concrete step to owning your life. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • Paradoxical intention is a signature logotherapy technique. How it is done: the client is asked to try hard to bring on the very thing they dread — someone who fears blushing tries to blush as red as possible. Why it works: you cannot force an anxious symptom on command, so the effort breaks the vicious circle of 'fear of the fear'. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • Dereflection is the second logotherapy technique. How it is done: the therapist turns the client's attention away from anxiously watching their own symptom and onto meaningful people and activities instead. Example: a man obsessed with his sleeplessness is guided to focus on his work and family, and the sleep often returns on its own. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • Attitude change (finding meaning in unavoidable suffering) is the third technique. How it is done: when a situation genuinely cannot be changed — a terminal illness, a bereavement — the therapist helps the client choose the stance they take toward it, which is the one freedom no one can remove. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • In stages and process, the work first builds a genuine relationship and explores the client's worldview. It then spots patterns of avoidance, deepens awareness of freedom and responsibility, and ends in committed action. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • The process is collaborative and exploratory, not driven by technique. The therapist walks alongside the client in facing hard truths, rather than repairing a defect. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is more meaning and authenticity and a greater ability to tolerate anxiety. The person then lives more deliberately in the face of life's givens. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • Indications include existential crises such as life transitions, midlife and bereavement, terminal or chronic illness, and depression marked by emptiness. Acute psychosis and a wish for quick symptom control are relative contraindications. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In research and current status, the evidence is moderate. Meaning-centred interventions help meaning, mood and quality of life, especially in people with cancer. Critics note the concepts are abstract and hard to pin down for testing. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
Existential therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Kierkegaard to Sartre; Binswanger, Boss, Frankl, May and Yalom.
  • Basis: four ultimate concerns (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness); existence precedes essence.
  • Formulation: inauthentic living, avoided responsibility and the existential vacuum.
  • Technique: phenomenological and Socratic dialogue; paradoxical intention, dereflection, attitude change.
  • Outcome and research: meaning and authenticity; moderate evidence, strong in meaning-centred care.
Mnemonic
DFIM
Death, Freedom, Isolation and Meaninglessness — Yalom's four ultimate concerns at the heart of existential therapy; facing them honestly is what turns dread into authentic living.

5Gestalt therapy

Gestalt therapy is a hands-on, here-and-now approach from Fritz and Laura Perls. Its key idea is that awareness itself heals. The therapist sets up live experiments rather than giving interpretations. The client finishes 'unfinished business' and takes back disowned parts of themselves, so as to become more whole.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology describes gestalt therapy (a hands-on therapy focused on present-moment awareness and the whole person) as an experiential approach. It stresses present experience and awareness and brings feelings, perceptions and actions together into the whole person. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Theoretical view It was developed by Fritz Perls, Laura Perls and Paul Goodman from psychoanalysis, Gestalt psychology and field theory. It became popular in the 1960s at the Esalen Institute. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Its guiding idea is the paradoxical theory of change (the odd truth that you change by fully accepting who you are now, not by forcing yourself to be someone else). Beisser put it as change coming when a person fully becomes what they are. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is present-moment awareness. Gestalt therapy holds that simply becoming fully aware of what you are feeling, doing and avoiding right now is itself what heals — you do not need the therapist to explain your past to you.
  • Instead of talking about a problem, the therapist sets up live experiments so the client experiences it directly in the room. The aim is to finish old 'unfinished business' and take back the parts of yourself you had disowned, so you feel whole again.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • Change works through heightened awareness in the here-and-now. By exaggerating a gesture, staying with a feeling or speaking to an empty chair, the client suddenly notices what they have been doing out of habit — and that fresh awareness, by itself, opens up a choice that was invisible before.
  • This rests on the paradoxical theory of change (the odd truth that you change by fully accepting and becoming who you are now, not by forcing yourself to be someone else). Fighting a feeling keeps you stuck; fully owning it is what lets it move, much as you can only leave a room once you admit you are in it.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, Gestalt therapy was developed by Fritz and Laura Perls with Paul Goodman in the 1940s and 1950s. They drew on psychoanalysis, Gestalt psychology, Reich's body work and Lewin's field theory. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Its origin reached a wide public in the 1960s at Esalen. There Perls' bold live demonstrations made the empty-chair style famous, for better and worse. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is holism and field theory: person and environment form one single field. So behaviour is understood in its context, not in isolation. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A central basis is the here-and-now. The focus is on the what and how of present experience, not the why. This is because awareness in the present moment, not a theory about the past, is what heals. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A further basis is figure and ground. The strongest need of the moment rises up to become the figure (the one need or interest that stands out from everything else right now, like a craving when you are hungry) against the background. Healthy living is a smooth flow of needs rising, being met and fading back. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Another basis is unfinished business (old, unexpressed feelings from the past that keep nagging and spilling into the present). Like an argument you never got to finish, these press for attention and stop a person living fully now. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In formulation, trouble comes from interruptions to contact at the boundary with others. These are named introjection (swallowing others' rules whole), projection (seeing your own feeling in others), retroflection (turning back on yourself what you really want to do to someone else, like getting angry at yourself instead of them), deflection (dodging real contact) and confluence (blurring into others). [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A further formulation idea is Perls' layers of neurosis: the phony, phobic, impasse, implosive and explosive layers. A client passes through them, like peeling an onion, on the way to genuine contact. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A vivid formulation is the top-dog versus underdog split. This is the inner tug-of-war between the bossy 'you should' (top-dog) and the sly, stubborn 'I won't' (underdog), which keeps a person stuck. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The goals are to increase awareness, take responsibility, join up split-off parts, finish unfinished business and shift from leaning on others toward self-support. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The empty-chair technique is the best-known Gestalt method. How it is done: the client imagines a person, or a part of themselves, sitting in an empty chair (an actual empty chair the client talks to, picturing someone absent or an inner part of themselves in it), speaks to it, then swaps seats and replies as that other side. Why it helps: a bottled-up conflict becomes a live dialogue, so feelings that were stuck get expressed and the two sides can meet. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The two-chair dialogue is the related technique for inner splits. How it is done: the top-dog sits in one chair and the underdog in the other, and the client voices both in turn. Why it helps: the warring parts stop fighting in the dark and start hearing each other, which is how they integrate. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Exaggeration and staying with the feeling are core experiments. How they are done: the therapist asks the client to make a small gesture bigger (keep tapping that foot, harder) or to not escape a rising feeling but stay with it a little longer. Why they help: both turn up the volume on something half-noticed until it reaches full awareness. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • 'I' language is a simple but powerful technique. How it is done: the client swaps 'it' and 'you' for 'I', and 'I can't' for 'I won't'. Example: 'my hand is shaking' becomes 'I am shaking', which quietly restores ownership of the experience and the choice. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Dream work is distinctive in Gestalt. How it is done: rather than interpret a dream, the therapist treats every part of it — even a wall or a dog — as a cast-off piece of the dreamer, who speaks as each part in turn. Why it helps: the client reclaims the disowned feelings the dream represents. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In stages and process the work moves from contact and the I-Thou relationship, through awareness work and experiments that heighten contact, to integration and the closing of what was unfinished. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The therapist's process stance is authentic and present, not interpretative. They offer experiments and honest, in-the-moment feedback rather than analysing from a distance. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is more awareness and responsibility, freer emotional expression, fuller integration and more lively relationships. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Indications include anxiety, depression, relationship and psychosomatic problems, and trauma-related unfinished business. Very confrontational experiments are a contraindication for fragile or psychotic clients, who may be overwhelmed. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In research and current status, the evidence base is limited but growing. The strongest support comes through emotion-focused therapy, where Greenberg and colleagues show empty-chair work for unfinished business is effective. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Gestalt therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Fritz and Laura Perls with Goodman; Gestalt psychology and field theory; Esalen in the 1960s.
  • Basis: holism, the here-and-now, figure and ground, unfinished business, awareness is curative.
  • Formulation: contact disturbances, layers of neurosis, top-dog versus underdog.
  • Technique: the empty chair, exaggeration, 'I' language, dream work; experiments not interpretations.
  • Outcome and research: awareness and integration; limited base, supported via emotion-focused therapy.
Mnemonic
AWARE
Awareness is curative, Who-and-how not why, Appropriate experiments (empty chair), Responsibility and 'I' language, and Ending unfinished business — the Gestalt essentials in the here-and-now.

6Person-centred (client-centred) therapy

Person-centred therapy is Carl Rogers' non-directive approach. The therapist offers three things: being real, accepting the client fully, and truly understanding them. Rogers held that these are enough on their own to free the client's natural drive to grow. The client is the expert on themselves and leads their own change.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology describes person-centered therapy (Rogers' non-directive therapy built on being genuine, accepting and empathic) as the non-directive form of therapy developed by Carl Rogers. The therapist's genuineness, unconditional positive regard and empathy help the client grow. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Theoretical view Rogers introduced non-directive counselling in 1942. In 1957 he set out his six necessary and sufficient conditions for change, grounding the approach in the actualising tendency. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • Theoretical view The approach is firmly humanistic yet distinct. It is Rogers' specific model built on the core conditions, whereas humanistic therapy names the broader third-force movement. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is the quality of the relationship, not any technique. Rogers claimed that if the therapist offers just three attitudes — being genuine, fully accepting the client, and truly understanding them — that is enough on its own to free the client's natural drive to grow.
  • The client, not the therapist, is treated as the expert on their own life. So the therapist never diagnoses, advises or steers. They follow the client, trusting that the person already carries within them the direction they need.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • The change engine is simple: when a person is accepted exactly as they are, they no longer need to defend or hide, so they can look honestly at feelings they once denied. As the hidden feelings come into the open and are accepted, the gap between their real self and their self-image closes, and health returns.
  • A small example: a man who was taught that anger is 'bad' has buried his, and feels tense and unreal. With a therapist who accepts even his anger without flinching, he slowly dares to feel it, name it, and own it — and the tension eases because he is no longer at war with part of himself.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, the approach is the work of Carl Rogers. His book Counseling and Psychotherapy in 1942 introduced non-directive counselling, and his Client-Centered Therapy in 1951 named the model. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • Rogers later broadened it into the person-centred approach for education, groups and conflict resolution. He also pioneered recording and studying real sessions, which started an empirical tradition in therapy research. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The foundational basis is the actualising tendency (the one inbuilt drive in every person to grow and fulfil their potential, like a plant growing toward the sun). It moves the person toward growth whenever conditions allow. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • A second basis is the self-concept and the ideal self (how you see yourself versus who you wish you were). A healthy person also trusts their own gut sense of experience as a guide, rather than only others' opinions. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • A third basis is conditions of worth (the belief that you only deserve love and approval when you meet other people's standards). They form when love was given with strings attached, and they push the person to deny feelings that clash with them. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A fourth basis is incongruence: the gap between how you see yourself and what you actually feel, or between your real and ideal self. This gap produces anxiety and defensiveness; closing it is the mark of health. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • In formulation, problems are seen as incongruence caused by conditions of worth. So therapy is not a technical repair; it simply provides the conditions in which the growth drive can start up again. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The decisive formulation claim is Rogers' six conditions for change, of which the three the therapist offers — the 'core conditions' — are congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathy. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • The first core condition is congruence (genuineness). How it shows up: the therapist is congruent (real and honest in the room, not hiding behind a professional mask or a fixed smile), matching their outward manner to their true inner feeling. Why it helps: realness invites realness, so the client dares to be genuine too. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • The second core condition is unconditional positive regard. How it shows up: the therapist prizes the client (warmly accepts and values the client no matter what they bring, with no strings attached) even when disapproving of a behaviour. Why it helps: being accepted whatever you reveal dissolves the conditions of worth that caused the trouble. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • The third core condition is empathy. How it shows up: the therapist accurately grasps the client's inner world and reflects it back, checking they have it right. Why it helps: feeling deeply and accurately understood lets the client trust and explore their own experience. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The guiding goals are more congruence and self-acceptance, judging yourself by your own standards rather than others', and becoming a fully functioning person who is open to experience. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • The defining procedure is being non-directive. How it is done: the therapist deliberately does not interpret, diagnose, advise or reassure. Why it matters: every piece of steering would quietly re-impose a new 'condition of worth', so holding back is itself the active ingredient. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Reflective listening is the central technique. How it is done: the therapist mirrors back the feeling and meaning underneath the client's words ('it sounds as if you felt abandoned'), paraphrases, clarifies and allows comfortable silences. Why it helps: the client hears their own inner world said back clearly, which deepens their self-understanding. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In stages and process, Rogers described a seven-stage continuum. The client moves from being rigid and cut off from feelings toward feelings that flow freely, arrive in the moment, and can be trusted. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In practice the process is simple: the client talks and the therapist reflects. As the client feels understood and accepted, defences drop, self-exploration deepens, and self-directed change appears. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is more self-acceptance and congruence, an inner source of judgement, less anxiety and defensiveness, and better, self-directed relationships. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • Indications are wide: mild to moderate emotional, identity and relationship problems, grief, adolescents and student counselling. Clients wanting direct advice, or with severe disorders or acute crises, are relative contraindications. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In research and current status, Rogers was a pioneer of therapy research. Meta-analyses by Elliott and colleagues find empathy, positive regard and congruence link modestly but reliably with good outcome, though critics doubt they are enough on their own. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Person-centred therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Carl Rogers (1942 non-directive counselling, 1951 client-centred therapy, 1957 six conditions).
  • Basis: the actualising tendency, self-concept, conditions of worth and incongruence.
  • Formulation: problems are incongruence from conditions of worth; provide the core conditions.
  • Technique: non-directive; congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathy; reflective listening.
  • Outcome and research: congruence and self-direction; Rogers pioneered research; conditions aid outcome.
Mnemonic
CUE
Congruence, Unconditional positive regard and Empathy — Rogers' three therapist-offered core conditions in person-centred therapy; given these, the client's own actualising tendency does the healing.

7Adlerian therapy (Individual Psychology)

Adlerian therapy, or Individual Psychology, is Alfred Adler's approach. It sees the person as a whole, as aiming at goals, and as shaped by their social world. Problems are treated as discouragement and mistaken beliefs about life, not illness. The therapy re-educates the client toward courage, cooperation and useful contribution.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Adler founded Individual Psychology after leaving Freud's circle in 1911. He stressed that the person is one indivisible whole, that behaviour is goal-directed, and that it is shaped by social context. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Theoretical view Its hallmark concept is social interest (a built-in capacity and willingness to cooperate with and care about other people). Adler treated it as the very measure of mental health. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Theoretical view The approach was carried forward by Dreikurs, Ansbacher, Mosak and Dinkmeyer into child guidance, family work and parent education. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is the person as a whole, aiming at goals, and set in their social world. Adler saw us not as pushed from behind by drives but as pulled forward by goals — a private picture of how to feel capable and belong. Problems are treated as discouragement and mistaken beliefs about life, not as illness.
  • The yardstick of health is social interest (a built-in capacity and willingness to cooperate with and care about other people). So the whole therapy re-educates the client toward courage, cooperation and useful contribution, rather than curing a disease.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • Change works through encouragement plus insight into one's lifestyle. The therapist first helps the client see the mistaken, self-defeating beliefs they formed as a child, then rebuilds their courage so they dare to act differently and connect with others.
  • The key idea is that a discouraged person behaves badly; a child who believes 'I only matter if I am the centre of attention' will misbehave to get it. Replace the belief and restore the courage, and the behaviour changes on its own — much as a plant straightens once you stop blocking its light.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, Adlerian therapy was founded by Alfred Adler, a Viennese physician and early member of Freud's circle. He broke away in 1911 to set up Individual Psychology. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Its origin also includes Rudolf Dreikurs, who adapted Adler's ideas for children, families and the democratic classroom. Adler founded the first child-guidance clinics in 1920s Vienna. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The first basis is holism and teleology (being pulled by future goals). The person is indivisible, and behaviour aims at a self-made goal, a fictional finalism (an imagined future goal you invent for yourself that quietly steers all your striving). [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A central basis is inferiority and compensation. Natural feelings of being 'not enough' spur a striving to feel capable and significant. This can grow into healthy ambition or, if it overwhelms the person, into a self-defeating inferiority complex. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • A defining basis is social interest: the inborn capacity to cooperate and contribute. Adler made it the yardstick of mental health, so well-being grows as you feel more connected to and useful to others. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • A further basis is the lifestyle (the unique set of beliefs and goals about life that you form very early in childhood and then live by). It is largely shaped by about age five from the family set-up and birth order, and then acts like a pair of tinted glasses through which you read everything. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Another basis is the contrast between private logic and common sense. Private logic is a person's own quirky, mistaken beliefs — their 'basic mistakes' — which steer them away from cooperative living. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In formulation, problems come from discouragement, mistaken beliefs and low social interest. Difficulties are seen as problems of living, not illness; in other words, a way of life that is not working. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A practical formulation tool is Dreikurs' four mistaken goals of a child's misbehaviour: getting attention, seeking power, taking revenge, and showing inadequacy to be left alone. Naming the goal tells you what the child is really after. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The goals are to boost social interest and belonging, correct mistaken lifestyle beliefs, build courage and self-worth, and swap the striving driven by inferiority for useful contribution. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Phase one, the relationship, is the first procedure. How it is done: the therapist builds an equal, respectful, collaborative partnership — two people side by side, never expert-over-patient. Why it matters: a discouraged person can only risk change inside a warm, non-judging bond. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Phase two, lifestyle assessment, is a signature technique. How it is done: the therapist explores the family set-up, birth order and especially early recollections (your very earliest specific memories, read as clues to the beliefs you live by now), treating these first memories like a projective snapshot of the client's core beliefs. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Phase three, insight, follows. How it is done: the therapist gently and tentatively (never bluntly) points out the client's mistaken beliefs, private logic and hidden goals, offering them as a guess to try on. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Phase four, reorientation, turns insight into action, and its engine is encouragement, the central Adlerian technique. How it is done: the therapist steadily highlights strengths and effort, not just results, so the client builds the courage to choose new, cooperative behaviour. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Acting 'as if' is a distinctive action technique. How it is done: the client behaves for a week as though they already had the quality they lack — acting as if (behaving as though you already had the confidence or skill you want, to try it on for size, like rehearsing a role) — which breaks the 'I could never' belief by direct experience. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Spitting in the client's soup and catching oneself are further techniques. Spitting in the soup means gently naming the hidden payoff of a behaviour so it loses its appeal (once you see you sulk to get sympathy, sulking feels less sweet). Catching oneself trains the client to spot their old pattern starting and stop it, aided by the push-button technique and homework. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In stages and process, the therapist works like an encouraging teacher or coach. Therapy is treated as education and re-education, not the cure of a disease. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is less inferiority and discouragement, more courage, greater social interest, and a more workable lifestyle and relationships. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Indications are broad: children and adolescents, parent education, family therapy, school and career counselling and group work. It fits collectivist cultures whose values match social interest, while severe psychopathology needs adaptation. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In research and current status, the evidence is limited but positive. There is support for Adlerian parent education such as STEP, for Adlerian play therapy and for counselling. Adler's ideas are recognised as foreshadowing cognitive, humanistic and family-systems therapies. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Adlerian therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Alfred Adler (Individual Psychology, 1911); Dreikurs, Ansbacher, Mosak, Dinkmeyer.
  • Basis: holism and teleology, inferiority and compensation, social interest, lifestyle and birth order.
  • Formulation: discouragement and mistaken beliefs; problems of living, not illness.
  • Technique: four phases (relationship, assessment, insight, reorientation); encouragement, acting as if.
  • Outcome and research: courage and social interest; limited but positive evidence; anticipated CBT.
Mnemonic
RAIR
Relationship, Assessment (lifestyle), Insight and Reorientation — Dreikurs' four Adlerian phases; the engine throughout is encouragement, aiming the client toward social interest.

8Transactional analysis (TA)

Transactional analysis is Eric Berne's down-to-earth approach. It reads personality as three inner 'ego states': Parent, Adult and Child. It sees problems as mixed-up ego states, repeated 'games', and a limiting life story. Using a clear, agreed contract, it works to restore autonomy and honest, game-free closeness.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Eric Berne founded transactional analysis in the late 1950s. He set out the theory in Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy and made it popular in Games People Play. He defined the goal as autonomy through awareness, spontaneity and intimacy. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view A transaction (one back-and-forth exchange between two people: you say something, I respond) is the basic unit of social life. Berne saw every exchange as coming from one of three ego states: the Parent, the Adult or the Child. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view The approach was carried forward by Harris, Steiner, Karpman and the Gouldings, who added the OK life positions, scripts, the drama triangle and redecision therapy. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is how we think, feel and relate, pictured through three inner 'selves' called ego states (three consistent ways of thinking, feeling and acting that we all switch between, called Parent, Adult and Child). At any moment you are speaking from one of them: the Parent (copied from your caregivers), the Adult (calm, here-and-now, factual) or the Child (your feelings and playfulness from long ago).
  • Problems are read as mixed-up ego states, repeated hidden-motive 'games', and a limiting life-story written in childhood. The whole therapy uses a clear, agreed contract to restore autonomy and honest, game-free closeness, in plain everyday language anyone can use.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • Change works by strengthening the Adult and rewriting the script. When the client can see which ego state they are in and which game they keep playing, they can choose to answer from the calm, reality-based Adult instead of being yanked around by an old Parent rule or a hurt Child feeling.
  • A clear example: a woman keeps starting the game 'Why don't you — Yes but' (she asks for advice, then rejects every suggestion) and ends up feeling misunderstood again, which confirms her childhood script 'nobody can help me'. Once she spots the game, she can stop playing it and ask for help straight, Adult-to-Adult.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, transactional analysis was created by Eric Berne, a Canadian-born psychiatrist. He broke from psychoanalysis to build a plain-language social psychiatry, publishing Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy in 1961 and Games People Play in 1964. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Its origin then widened through Thomas Harris (whose I'm OK, You're OK spread the life positions), Claude Steiner on scripts, Stephen Karpman on the drama triangle, and the Gouldings on redecision therapy. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The core basis is the three ego states. The Parent is the taught, judging, caring voice copied from caregivers; the Adult is the calm, factual problem-solver dealing with the here-and-now; the Child holds the feelings, wants and playfulness carried from early life. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • A second basis is the analysis of transaction (one back-and-forth exchange between two people: you say something, I respond)s. They can be complementary (replies come from the expected state, so talk flows), crossed (an unexpected state replies, so talk breaks off), or ulterior (carrying a hidden message underneath the polite surface one, like saying 'nice shirt' to mean the opposite), which drives games. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A third basis is strokes, the units of recognition we all hunger for. A stroke is any sign of being noticed; positive or negative, conditional or not. Berne's point is that we need them so badly that we will provoke negative ones rather than be ignored, which shapes how we fill our time. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • A fourth basis is games and rackets. Games are games (repeated hidden-motive exchanges that always end in the same bad feeling), like 'Why don't you, Yes but'. They pay off in a familiar bad 'racket' feeling (such as righteous anger) that quietly props up the life script. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • A fifth basis is the life script (an unconscious life story you decided on as a child, based on messages from your parents, and then act out). It is built from injunctions like 'don't be close', driver messages like 'be perfect', and the child's own early decisions about how life will go. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A sixth basis is the four life positions of the OK Corral. They run from the healthy 'I'm OK, you're OK' down to 'I'm OK, you're not' (blaming), 'I'm not OK, you're OK' (self-doubting) and 'I'm not OK, you're not OK' (hopeless), each colouring a person's whole outlook. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In formulation, problems are traced to contaminated or excluded ego states (for example Parent prejudices leaking into the Adult), destructive games, a negative script and an unhealthy life position. The therapist maps which ego state and which game is driving the complaint. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A vivid formulation tool is Karpman's drama triangle. Clients switch between Persecutor, Victim and Rescuer roles. The healthier exit from each is assertiveness, honest vulnerability and genuine caring. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are autonomy and game-free intimacy: a free, uncontaminated Adult, a revised script (a 'script cure'), better self-control and relief of the presenting symptom. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • The contract is a defining technique, not just a formality. How it is done: at the start, client and therapist agree in plain words on a clear, observable goal and their shared roles. Why it helps: it sets the equal, adult-to-adult, 'you're OK, I'm OK' tone and keeps both accountable. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The four analyses are the main technique set. Structural analysis maps the client's three ego states; transactional analysis studies their exchanges with others; game analysis uncovers the hidden payoff of repeated bad exchanges; and script analysis traces the childhood life-story driving it all. Together they move the problem from vague to visible. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Berne's eight therapeutic operations are the therapist's fine tools, running from interrogation, specification and confrontation, through explanation, illustration, confirmation and interpretation, up to crystallisation (the moment the client can finally hold a new, freeing view of themselves on their own). Each is a graded step from gathering facts to the client's own insight. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Redecision work is the powerful change technique (the Gouldings). How it is done: using chair work, the client goes back to the early scene where they made a limiting decision ('I must never show need') and, from the Adult, makes a new decision now. Supported by the egogram, permissions, stroke work and homework, usually in a group. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the work runs from contracting and assessment, through strengthening and 'decontaminating' the Adult and freeing the Child, to relearning new behaviours and finally autonomy and termination. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is more autonomy, clearer communication, an end to games, a revised script and better self-esteem. The simple language lets clients keep using the ideas after therapy ends. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Indications cover neurotic, relationship and communication problems, personality issues, couples and families, groups, education and organisations. Acute psychosis is a contraindication served only by adapted forms. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status the evidence is modest. Some reviews report good outcomes, but few rigorous trials exist. TA stays widely taught and accredited by the ITAA and EATA, despite criticism of its jargon and limited testing. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Transactional analysis at a glance
  • Origin: Eric Berne (1961 to 1964); extended by Harris, Steiner, Karpman and the Gouldings.
  • Basis: Parent-Adult-Child ego states, transactions, strokes, games, script, OK life positions.
  • Formulation: contaminated ego states, games, negative script; Karpman's drama triangle.
  • Technique: the contract; structural, transactional, game and script analysis; redecision chair work.
  • Process: contract, decontaminate Adult, deconfuse Child, relearn, autonomy; modest evidence base.
Mnemonic
PAC
Parent, Adult and Child — the three ego states at the heart of transactional analysis; the contract and the four analyses (structural, transactional, game and script) work to free the Adult and reach autonomy.

9Reality therapy (and choice theory)

Reality therapy is William Glasser's present-focused approach, built on choice theory. It holds that we choose almost all of our behaviour to meet five basic needs. Within a warm, non-blaming relationship, it uses the WDEP system to help clients judge their own choices and plan better, more responsible ones.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view William Glasser developed reality therapy in the 1960s. He later grounded it in choice theory (the idea that we choose nearly all of our behaviour to try to meet our basic needs), and that we can directly control only our own acting and thinking. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • Theoretical view Glasser held that most psychological problems are relationship problems, rooted in unsatisfying connections. So he rejected diagnostic labels in favour of helping clients make better choices. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • Theoretical view Robert Wubbolding organised the practice into the WDEP system, set inside a cycle of counselling that first builds a trusting, friendly bond. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is present choices and responsibility. Reality therapy holds that we choose almost all of our behaviour to try to meet five basic needs, and that we can directly control only what we do and think — not, directly, how we feel. So the work stays firmly on the present and on better choices, not on the past or on blame.
  • Glasser pictured it as a car with four wheels: thinking and acting are the front wheels we steer with, while feelings and body are the back wheels that follow. Change where you drive (what you do and think) and the back wheels — your feelings — come along behind.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • Change works through self-evaluation inside a caring relationship. The therapist does not scold or diagnose; within a warm bond they keep asking, kindly, 'Is what you are doing actually getting you what you want?'. When the client honestly answers 'no', they become willing to plan something better.
  • The crucial move is that the client judges their own behaviour, not the therapist. A teenager who skips school to feel powerful is led to see for himself that it is costing him the future he wants; owning that judgement is what powers a realistic new plan he will actually keep.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, reality therapy was founded by William Glasser, an American psychiatrist. Unhappy with Freudian therapy, he developed his ideas at the Ventura School for Girls and a veterans' hospital, publishing Reality Therapy in 1965. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • Its origin continued as Glasser's 1980s control theory was renamed choice theory in 1998. Robert Wubbolding then built the WDEP system and the cycle of counselling that organise practice today. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is that all behaviour is chosen, which Glasser grounded in choice theory (the idea that we choose nearly all of our behaviour to try to meet our basic needs). We can directly control only our acting and thinking, which then steer our feelings and body — the four-wheel car picture. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • A second basis is the five genetic basic needs (the five needs that drive all of us: survival, love and belonging, power, freedom and fun). Love and belonging is treated as the most important for mental health, which is why Glasser said most problems are relationship problems. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • A third basis is the quality world (a private mental picture album of the specific people and things you most want in your life). We constantly compare the real world to this album, and a gap between the two is what drives our behaviour to close it. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fourth basis is that relationships are central. The seven deadly habits (criticising, blaming, complaining, nagging, threatening, punishing, bribing) damage connection; the seven caring habits (supporting, encouraging, listening, accepting, trusting, respecting, negotiating differences) rebuild it. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fifth basis is responsibility and a present focus. You meet your needs without stopping others meeting theirs, with no excuses, no blame and no punishment, and almost no attention to the past or to symptoms. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • In formulation, the client is seen as having an unmet need, usually love and belonging. They then choose ineffective behaviours — withdrawing, 'depressing' (Glasser's verb, to stress it is chosen) or blaming — to try to meet it, which builds a failure identity. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are to help clients take responsibility and make effective choices, improve their relationships, and meet needs in ways that are responsible, realistic and right — Glasser's three Rs. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • Building the environment is the first procedure, from Wubbolding's cycle. How it is done: the therapist forms a trusting, friendly bond using warmth, humour and genuine listening, while firmly avoiding arguing, criticising and accepting excuses. Why it matters: a person will only evaluate themselves honestly inside a relationship that feels safe. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • W — exploring Wants is the first WDEP step. How it is done: the therapist asks what the client truly wants — from life, from others, from therapy — drawing out the pictures in their quality world. Why it helps: you cannot steer toward a goal you have never named. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • D — Doing and direction is the second step. How it is done: the therapist asks in concrete detail what the client is actually doing right now and where that road is taking them. Why it helps: it makes the current behaviour visible so it can be weighed. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • E — self-Evaluation is the pivot of the whole method. How it is done: the therapist asks the client to judge, for themselves, whether their current behaviour is getting them what they want and is realistic. Why it is central: change only begins when the person — not the therapist — decides the old way is not working. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • P — Planning is the final step, made memorable as the SAMI2C3 plan (a plan that is Simple, Attainable, Measurable, Immediate, Involving, Controlled, Committed and Consistent). How it is done: client and therapist craft one small, concrete, do-it-now step the client commits to, so success is easy to see and build on. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Further techniques include skilful questioning, metaphor and humour, paradox, role play, written contracts and self-disclosure. The gentle, repeated challenge — 'Is what you are doing helping?' — runs through all of them, always with a firm refusal to accept excuses. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the therapist builds the environment, finds out what the client wants, explores current behaviour, guides self-evaluation, helps form and commit to a plan, and then follows up, revising the plan if it fails. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is more responsibility, a stronger internal locus of control (feeling in charge of your own life), better relationships, higher self-esteem and more satisfying behaviour, in a short and practical course. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications are broad: schools, adolescents and offenders, corrections, addictions, marital and group counselling and the workplace. Severe mental illness or trauma needing exploration is a caution. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status the evidence is moderate but often quasi-experimental. Studies in schools, correctional and cross-cultural settings show gains in self-esteem, locus of control and relationships. Critics attack its rejection of diagnosis and its simple view of choice. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Reality therapy at a glance
  • Origin: William Glasser (Reality Therapy, 1965; Choice Theory, 1998); Wubbolding's WDEP and cycle.
  • Basis: all behaviour is chosen; five basic needs; the quality world; relationships are central.
  • Formulation: an unmet need and ineffective chosen behaviours; a failure identity.
  • Technique: build the environment, then Wants, Doing, Evaluation and Planning (a SAMI2C3 plan).
  • Process: present-focused, no excuses, no punishment; moderate, mostly quasi-experimental evidence.
Mnemonic
WDEP
Wants, Doing (and direction), Evaluation and Planning — Wubbolding's four procedures of reality therapy, applied inside a warm, non-blaming environment to help clients choose more effective, responsible behaviour.

10Supportive psychotherapy

Supportive psychotherapy sits at the ego-strengthening end of therapy. It does not dig into the unconscious. Instead it props up and restores the client's coping, self-esteem and everyday functioning. It does this through a warm, active relationship, using reassurance, advice, praise and practical problem solving.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Supportive psychotherapy sits at the supportive end of Luborsky's supportive-expressive continuum. Think of a scale: one end uncovers hidden conflict, the other end strengthens coping. Supportive work aims to keep up or improve ego functions and self-esteem, not to uncover conflict. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Theoretical view It treats the therapeutic relationship as the main healer. The therapist is a trusted, caring support, or an auxiliary ego (a dependable stand-in who lends the client the mental strength they temporarily lack, like a crutch for a sprained ankle). [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Theoretical view It was described by Wallerstein, Rockland, and Winston, Pinsker and McCullough for patients with limited ego strength who need shoring up rather than insight. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is to strengthen the person, not to dig into them. Supportive therapy does the opposite of uncovering work: instead of opening up hidden conflicts, it reinforces the client's existing strengths, defences and coping so they can get through the day.
  • Picture someone whose house is in a storm. Deep therapy would rebuild the foundations; supportive therapy props up the walls so the house stands through the storm. The aim is to keep the person steady and functioning, raise their self-esteem, and carry them through a crisis or a long illness, using the warm relationship itself as the main support.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • The change engine is a borrowed strength plus relief of pressure. A dependable, warm therapist acts as an auxiliary ego (a stand-in source of steadiness and judgement that the client leans on until their own recovers), so the client borrows the calm and good sense they cannot yet manage alone — much like leaning on a crutch until a sprained ankle heals.
  • At the same time, letting feelings out, getting clear reassurance and advice, and solving one practical problem at a time all lower the pressure on an overwhelmed mind. As the stress drops and small successes build, the client's own coping and confidence slowly come back, and the borrowed support is needed less.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, supportive psychotherapy grows from the earliest practice of psychiatry, its reassurance, advice and moral treatment. It also grows from psychodynamic therapy at the supportive end of Luborsky's continuum. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Its origin was shaped by Wallerstein's Menninger project, by Rockland, and by Winston, Pinsker and McCullough. They formalised it for patients whose fragile personality cannot handle uncovering work. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is a change of aim. The goal is to maintain, restore or improve ego functions and self-esteem, and to shore up existing defences and coping, not to make the unconscious conscious. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A second basis is that the relationship itself heals. A dependable, warm therapist acts as an auxiliary ego, lending the strength and steadiness the client temporarily lacks. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A third basis is a here-and-now focus. The work stays on present stresses, coping and daily functioning, not on childhood reconstruction or deep transference. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fourth basis is how transference is handled. Positive transference is used but not interpreted, and deep interpretation is deliberately avoided, so the client is not destabilised. In short, the therapist uses the client's trust but does not analyse it. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In formulation, symptoms are read as arising when stress overwhelms a person's coping. An overwhelmed ego, or a fragile personality, needs its functioning and adaptation strengthened. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are symptom relief and stabilisation, better adaptation and coping, support through a crisis or illness, prevention of relapse and rehospitalisation, and higher self-esteem. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A defining procedure is the therapist's stance, which is active, warm and flexible, and often directive. The therapist meets the client where they are, rather than waiting in silence for associations. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Core techniques include reassurance, encouragement and praise, empathic listening, and ventilation (letting the client talk out and release pent-up feelings, like letting steam out of a pot). Advice and guidance are given where the client cannot yet decide alone. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Further techniques are psychoeducation, explanation and clarification, anticipatory guidance that readies the client for a stressful event, and practical problem solving and reality testing. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • More techniques include reframing (helping the client see a situation in a more helpful light), changing the environment (such as involving family or arranging services), modelling, limit-setting, strengthening good defences and timely crisis intervention. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The frequency and duration are flexible. Sessions may be weekly or monthly, and the work can be brief and time-limited or long-term maintenance for chronic conditions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the work moves from assessing strengths and ego capacities, through building the alliance and defining problems and goals, to choosing and applying supportive techniques. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The later process is monitoring progress, with a gradual reduction or ongoing maintenance, and an open-ended, flexible ending rather than a sharply scheduled termination. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is better coping and daily functioning, less distress and fewer relapses. Stabilisation is valued above the deeper personality change that expressive therapy pursues. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications are wide: schizophrenia and other severe mental illness, bipolar and personality disorders with low ego strength, crises and bereavement, chronic or terminal medical illness, the elderly and those with low insight. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, Wallerstein's Menninger study found supportive therapy gave more lasting change than expected. Later work shows it comparable to other therapies, yet it is under-researched and often used only as a control condition, and critics warn it may foster dependency. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Supportive psychotherapy at a glance
  • Origin: psychiatry's reassurance and advice; the supportive end of Luborsky's continuum; Wallerstein.
  • Basis: strengthen ego functions and self-esteem; the relationship heals; here-and-now focus.
  • Formulation: stress overwhelms coping; an overwhelmed ego or fragile personality.
  • Technique: reassurance, praise, ventilation, advice, psychoeducation, problem solving, reframing.
  • Indications: severe mental illness, crises, medical illness; comparable but under-researched.
Mnemonic
SUPPORT
Strengthen the ego, Use the relationship, Praise and reassure, Problem-solve, Offer advice and psychoeducation, Reality-test and Reframe, Tackle the crisis — the aims and techniques of supportive psychotherapy, which bolsters coping rather than uncovering the unconscious.

11Clinical hypnotherapy

Clinical hypnotherapy uses hypnosis, a state of narrow, focused attention in which a person responds more readily to suggestion. In that state the therapist helps change how the client perceives, feels and behaves. A session moves through five steps and is mostly used as an add-on to treat pain, anxiety and habits.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The American Psychological Association (Division 30) describes hypnosis (a state of narrowly focused attention, less awareness of surroundings, and a greater readiness to respond to suggestion). It is a state of consciousness, not sleep. [American Psychological Association (Division 30, Society of Psychological Hypnosis)]
  • Theoretical view Milton H. Erickson shaped modern practice with a permissive, indirect style. He used the client's own imagery and words rather than giving bossy commands. [Milton H. Erickson & Ernest L. Rossi, Hypnotherapy: An Exploratory Casebook]
  • Theoretical view Theories split into state views, like Hilgard's neodissociation (hypnosis is a real altered state), and non-state or socio-cognitive views that stress role-taking, expectancy and imagination. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is focused attention put to use. In hypnosis the mind narrows to one thing and the usual critical, doubting part steps back, so helpful suggestions can take hold far more easily than in ordinary waking talk.
  • Think of how a child, lost in a story, really feels afraid or excited — attention is so absorbed that the imagined becomes almost real. Hypnotherapy deliberately creates that absorbed state and then feeds in useful ideas (calm instead of panic, numbness instead of pain). It is not sleep and not loss of control; the client stays aware and could stop any time. It is mostly used as an add-on to other treatment, not a therapy on its own.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • The change engine is heightened suggestibility inside a narrowed focus. When attention is absorbed and the critical, judging mind relaxes, a suggestion meets less resistance and is accepted more like a direct instruction — so 'your hand feels numb' or 'the worry drifts away' can actually reshape how the body and feelings respond.
  • The gains are then made to last by post-hypnotic suggestion and self-hypnosis: the helpful response is linked to a cue the client can use later, and they are taught to re-enter the calm state on their own. In short, hypnosis opens a window; the suggestions and home practice are what carry the change out of the room and into daily life.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, hypnotherapy descends from Mesmer's animal magnetism in the 1770s, through Puysegur's artificial somnambulism, and James Braid, who in 1843 coined 'hypnosis' from the Greek for sleep. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Its origin runs on through the Nancy school of Liebeault and Bernheim (who saw hypnosis as suggestion), Charcot and Janet on hysteria and dissociation, and Breuer and Freud's early catharsis under hypnosis, which Freud later gave up. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The modern origin is dominated by Milton Erickson's permissive, indirect method, by Hilgard's neodissociation theory with the Stanford scales, and by socio-cognitive theorists like Barber, Sarbin, Spanos and Kirsch. [Milton H. Erickson & Ernest L. Rossi, Hypnotherapy: An Exploratory Casebook]
  • The first basis is the nature of the hypnotic state: absorption, focused attention and suggestibility with reduced critical judgement. Importantly, hypnosis is not sleep and not a loss of control. [American Psychological Association (Division 30, Society of Psychological Hypnosis)]
  • A second basis is the debate between state and non-state theories. One camp sees a real altered state of dissociation; the other sees role-taking, expectancy and imagination. Brain imaging shows changes in attention and default-mode networks. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A third basis is hypnotisability (how easily a given person responds to hypnotic suggestion, which stays fairly fixed, like a stable trait). It varies across people and is measured by scales like the Stanford and Harvard group scales. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In formulation, symptoms, habits, pain and anxiety are seen as kept going by automatic learned responses and by negative beliefs and imagery. Focused attention and suggestion can be used to change them. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are symptom control, especially of pain and anxiety, habit change, relaxation, more self-efficacy and ego-strength, and teaching self-hypnosis so the client can manage symptoms alone. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A core procedure is the pre-induction phase. It secures consent, takes a history, explains hypnosis, and clears up the myths of lost control or revealed secrets, while building expectancy and rapport. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The induction technique then narrows attention through eye fixation, progressive relaxation, breathing or imagery. Ericksonian inductions use confusion, storytelling and pacing and leading rather than direct command. [Milton H. Erickson & Ernest L. Rossi, Hypnotherapy: An Exploratory Casebook]
  • The deepening technique follows, using counting down, staircase imagery or fractionation (lifting the person out of trance and taking them back in again, repeatedly, so each dip goes deeper), to intensify the experience before therapeutic work. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In the therapeutic phase the clinician uses direct and indirect suggestions, metaphors and ego-strengthening, plus imagery and rehearsal. Where helpful, hypnotic analgesia such as glove anaesthesia is used for pain. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Deeper techniques in that phase include age regression and hypnoanalysis (uncovering and working through buried memories or feelings while the person is under hypnosis), post-hypnotic suggestions and anchoring, desensitisation and parts therapy, all used with care. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The final technique is termination: a careful re-alerting or dehypnosis by counting up and reorienting the client, followed by discussion and teaching self-hypnosis for ongoing practice. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the session therefore follows the fixed order of pre-induction, induction, deepening, therapeutic phase and termination, each step set up before the next is tried. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is less pain and anxiety, changed habits and greater self-efficacy. Gains are stronger when the client practises self-hypnosis between sessions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications include chronic and procedural pain, labour and dental pain, anxiety and phobias, irritable bowel syndrome, insomnia, habit disorders and psychosomatic problems. Contraindications and cautions cover psychosis, severe personality disorder and the risk of false memory in forensic use. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, meta-analyses find that adding hypnosis to CBT improves outcomes. Evidence is strong for pain, irritable bowel syndrome and procedural distress, moderate for anxiety, and weaker or unclear for smoking and weight loss. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Clinical hypnotherapy at a glance
  • Origin: Mesmer and Braid; the Nancy school; Erickson, Hilgard and the socio-cognitive theorists.
  • Basis: focused attention and suggestibility (APA Division 30); state vs non-state; hypnotisability.
  • Formulation: symptoms kept up by automatic responses and imagery, modified by suggestion.
  • Technique: pre-induction, induction, deepening, therapeutic suggestions, re-alerting; self-hypnosis.
  • Indications: pain, IBS, anxiety, habits; strong for pain and IBS, weak for smoking and weight.
Mnemonic
PIDTT
Pre-induction, Induction, Deepening, Therapeutic phase and Termination — the five stages of a hypnotherapy session; throughout, hypnosis is focused attention with a heightened response to suggestion, used mostly as an adjunct.

12Play therapy

Play therapy treats play as the child's natural language. In it, toys become the child's words and play becomes the child's speech. Using a safe playroom and a trained therapist, in either directive or non-directive form, it helps children express and work through emotions, trauma and relationship difficulties.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Play therapy rests on the idea that play is the child's natural language. As Landreth put it, toys are the child's words and play is the child's speech. Children show in play what they cannot yet say in words. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • Theoretical view Virginia Axline set out the non-directive, child-centred form in 1947, with its eight guiding principles. She applied Rogers' conditions to children. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • Theoretical view Its roots reach back to Anna Freud and Melanie Klein, who first used play in analysis, and forward to Landreth's child-centred play therapy and Knell's cognitive-behavioural play therapy. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is to meet children in their own language, which is play. A young child cannot sit and explain, in words, that they feel scared, angry or unsafe — but they can show it with toys. So play therapy treats toys as words and play as speech.
  • A frightened child may make two dolls fight, or bury a toy in the sand and dig it up again, working through a real worry at a safe, pretend distance. The therapist gives a warm, accepting playroom and follows the child's lead, so the child can express, understand and master feelings and experiences they could never put into a sentence.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • The change engine is safe expression plus mastery through repetition. By acting a frightening or confusing experience out in symbolic play, the child lets the bottled-up feeling out at a safe distance (it is only the dolls, not really them) and, by replaying it with a better ending, moves from feeling helpless to feeling in charge of the story.
  • The accepting relationship does the rest: when the therapist reflects the child's feelings and accepts them without blame, the child learns the feelings are allowed and manageable. Over many sessions this builds self-esteem, emotional control and trust — and when parents are trained to play this way too, as in filial therapy, the healing continues at home.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, play therapy begins with Freud's case of Little Hans in 1909 and Hermine Hug-Hellmuth, the first to use play with children. Then Anna Freud and Melanie Klein made play central to child analysis. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Its origin then runs through Virginia Axline's non-directive play therapy in 1947 and her book Dibs: In Search of Self. It moves on to the Guerneys' filial therapy, Landreth's child-centred play therapy and Schaefer's founding of the Association for Play Therapy. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • The first basis is that play is the child's natural language. Children cannot easily put abstract thoughts and feelings into words, so they express them through symbolic play with toys. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • A second basis is the therapeutic powers of play (the specific ways play actually heals, such as helping a child communicate, release feelings and build relationships), which Schaefer named as communication, emotional wellness, stronger relationships and increased personal strengths. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A third basis is that play therapy draws on several theories: psychoanalytic, humanistic and child-centred, attachment, cognitive-behavioural and developmental. So it can be directive or non-directive. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fourth basis is Axline's eight principles. These include building a warm relationship, accepting the child exactly as they are, reflecting feelings, and respecting the child's own ability to solve problems. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • In formulation, children's problems are read as blocked emotions, trauma, insecure attachment or unmet needs. Play lets the child express and master these safely, at a symbolic distance from the real event. For example, a scared child may make dolls act out a frightening scene. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are emotional expression and regulation, a better self-concept and self-esteem, social skills and coping, the resolution of trauma or conflict, and a stronger parent-child attachment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A core procedure is the playroom and its toys, sorted into three kinds: real-life toys like a doll house, acting-out and aggressive toys like puppets and a bop bag, and creative media like sand, clay and paint. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Non-directive techniques include tracking the child's play aloud, reflecting feelings, returning responsibility with phrases like 'you can decide', and encouragement that follows rather than steers the child. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Limit-setting is a key technique, often taught as ACT (a three-step way to set a limit: Acknowledge the feeling, Communicate the limit, Target an acceptable alternative), which keeps the playroom safe without shaming the child. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Structured techniques include puppets, sandplay, drawing and storytelling, Gardner's mutual storytelling, bibliotherapy, role play, therapeutic games, and cognitive-behavioural play that uses modelling and exposure. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Parent-mediated techniques are distinctive. In filial therapy and child-parent relationship therapy the therapist trains parents to run play sessions at home. Theraplay builds structure, engagement, nurture and challenge. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the work moves from assessment, parent consultation and consent, through relationship building as the child explores the room and tests the therapist, into the appearance of themes in the play. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The later process is a working-through phase of aggression, regression, limit-testing and dramatic play toward mastery. Then comes a termination that the child is prepared for early, usually across about twelve to twenty weekly sessions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is better emotional expression, behaviour and self-esteem, improved social skills and a stronger parent-child relationship, especially when parents take an active part. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications are children of about three to twelve years with anxiety, fears, trauma and abuse, bereavement, parental divorce, adjustment and behaviour problems, and selective mutism. Adolescents may need talk, art or drama instead. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, Bratton and colleagues' 2005 meta-analysis of ninety-three studies reported a large mean effect, larger when parents took part. Later child-centred play therapy reviews show moderate to large effects, though critics note methodological weaknesses. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Play therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Freud's Little Hans; Anna Freud and Klein; Axline (non-directive, 1947); Landreth's CCPT.
  • Basis: play is the child's language; the therapeutic powers of play; Axline's eight principles.
  • Formulation: blocked emotions, trauma, insecure attachment expressed through symbolic play.
  • Technique: playroom and toys, tracking, reflecting, ACT limit-setting, sandplay, filial therapy.
  • Research: Bratton et al. (2005) large effect; moderate to large for CCPT; children about 3 to 12.
Mnemonic
TRACK
Toys are words, Relationship first, Accept the child, Children-centred (non-directive) and Keep safe limits (ACT) — the heart of play therapy, in which play is the child's natural language for expressing and mastering experience.

13Psychodrama

Psychodrama is J. L. Moreno's action-based group therapy. One group member, the protagonist, acts out their conflict instead of just talking about it. A director, helpers playing other people, an audience and a stage make this possible. The work restores spontaneity and new roles through three phases: warm-up, action and sharing.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Jacob L. Moreno created psychodrama out of his Theatre of Spontaneity. He coined the term group psychotherapy and developed sociometry, a way of measuring the relationships within a group. [Jacob L. Moreno, Psychodrama]
  • Theoretical view Its foundation is that spontaneity (the ability to respond freshly and well to a situation, old or new, instead of on autopilot) and creativity are the basis of mental health. So trouble is a loss of spontaneity and getting stuck in rigid roles. [Jacob L. Moreno, Psychodrama]
  • Theoretical view Moreno described five instruments: the protagonist, the director, the auxiliary egos, the audience and the stage. Together they make the enactment possible. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is to act out the problem, rather than just talk about it. Rather than describe a painful argument with a parent, the client (the protagonist) stages it on the spot, with group members stepping in to play the other people, so the conflict is lived again in the room.
  • The guiding belief is that health is spontaneity — being able to respond freshly and flexibly — and that distress comes from getting stuck in rigid, narrow roles. By replaying scenes and trying new responses safely, the person recovers spontaneity and widens their range of roles. Doing it, not only saying it, is what makes the difference.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • The change engine is enactment plus a fresh viewpoint. Acting a scene out brings the real emotion up and releases it (catharsis), far more fully than describing it would. Then techniques like role reversal — where the protagonist steps into the other person's shoes and plays them — let the client literally feel the situation from the other side, which produces sudden empathy and insight.
  • Because the stage is 'surplus reality', the client can also replay what never happened — say what was never said, meet someone who has died, rehearse a feared future — and so finish unfinished business and practise new roles safely. The closing sharing, where others tell their own similar stories, dissolves the shame of being alone with the problem and settles the person back into real life.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, psychodrama was created by Jacob L. Moreno in Vienna. His Theatre of Spontaneity in 1921 led, after he moved to the United States, to the Beacon sanatorium and a systematic method of action-based group therapy. [Jacob L. Moreno, Psychodrama]
  • Its origin also credits Zerka Moreno as a key developer. The method went on to influence Gestalt therapy, drama therapy, role play and the behavioural rehearsal used in CBT. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is that spontaneity and creativity are the foundations of mental health. So distress is understood as lost spontaneity, rigid role patterns and unfinished action. [Jacob L. Moreno, Psychodrama]
  • A second basis is role theory, covering role-taking, role-playing and role-creating. Role conflict and role rigidity are seen as central to psychological problems. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A third basis is tele (a genuine two-way flow of feeling and understanding between people, unlike one-sided transference). Moreno used the word for the real mutual empathy that binds a group. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fourth basis is catharsis, encounter and surplus reality. Surplus reality lets a client act out what never happened, or what is feared or wished, all worked in the here-and-now. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In formulation, distress is traced to a limited or conflicting set of roles, blocked spontaneity and unresolved relationships and events. Enactment brings these into the open to be experienced and changed. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are catharsis and release, insight and a wider range of roles, renewed spontaneity, better interpersonal skills, the resolution of unfinished business, and group cohesion and support. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A defining procedure is the use of the five instruments: the protagonist whose issue is explored, the director who guides, the auxiliary egos who play significant others, the audience who witness, and the stage. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The most powerful technique is role reversal (the protagonist swaps and plays the other person, so as to see the situation through their eyes). It builds empathy and insight by letting the protagonist feel the other's position. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A second key technique is doubling, where a helper voices the protagonist's unspoken feelings. There is also mirroring, where the protagonist watches a helper play them from the outside, like seeing themselves on film. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Further techniques include soliloquy, the empty chair and future projection, plus concretisation, sculpting, the social atom and the magic shop, which make abstract feelings concrete and visible. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A related technique is sociodrama, which turns the same action methods onto shared group or social issues, rather than one person's private conflict. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process, the work opens with a warm-up. The director builds trust and a protagonist emerges, so the group is ready for action. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The action phase then sets and acts out the scene, often moving from the present into the past. It uses role reversal, doubling and mirroring to reach a peak of emotion and new solutions before the scene is closed. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The closing process is sharing and integration. The audience offer their own similar experiences, rather than analysis or advice. This reduces the protagonist's sense of isolation and helps them re-enter daily life. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is emotional release and insight, better relationships and role flexibility, and the support of the group, all reached through doing rather than only talking. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications are group settings for trauma, grief, addictions, family and relationship issues, adolescents, social-skills training and psychiatric inpatients. Acute psychosis, or a very fragile client without containment, is a caution needing a skilled director. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, Kipper and Ritchie's 2003 meta-analysis reported a large effect, with role reversal and doubling the most effective techniques. Later reviews show moderate positive evidence, though rigorous randomised trials remain few. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Psychodrama at a glance
  • Origin: J. L. Moreno (Theatre of Spontaneity, 1921); coined group psychotherapy and sociometry.
  • Basis: spontaneity and creativity; role theory; tele; catharsis, encounter and surplus reality.
  • Formulation: a limited role repertoire and blocked spontaneity; unresolved relationships.
  • Instruments and technique: protagonist, director, auxiliary egos, audience, stage; role reversal.
  • Process: warm-up, action, sharing; Kipper and Ritchie (2003) large effect; few rigorous RCTs.
Mnemonic
PDAAS
Protagonist, Director, Auxiliary egos, Audience and Stage — Moreno's five instruments of psychodrama; the work flows through warm-up, action and sharing, with role reversal the most powerful technique.

14Oriental approaches

Oriental approaches are Asian mind-body practices used for mental health. They include yoga, meditation, shavasana, pranic healing, reiki and tai chi. They all work with attention, breath and relaxation. But their evidence varies a lot: genuine support for mindfulness, yoga and tai chi, down to weak and unclear findings for energy healing.

Characterisations by professional bodies and traditions
  • Theoretical view Patanjali's Yoga Sutras describe yoga (the joining of body, mind and spirit through an eight-part discipline) as calming the restless chatter of the mind. This is reached through the eight limbs, from ethical rules to deep absorption. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Guideline Clinical mindfulness meditation is described as non-judgemental, present-moment awareness. Kabat-Zinn adapted it as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction. It sits within the World Health Organization's wider promotion of self-care for mental health. [World Health Organization, mhGAP Intervention Guide]
  • Theoretical view Energy-healing practices like pranic healing and reiki claim to clear or channel a life energy, prana or ki. Their own traditions describe it this way, but there is no credible scientific evidence that such an energy exists. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Main focus (the central idea)
  • The main focus is to settle the mind through the body, breath and attention. Instead of talking a problem out, these practices quiet the whole nervous system — slow breathing, gentle movement, still postures and focused awareness — so the body shifts from 'alarm' mode into 'rest' mode, and the restless chatter of the mind calms down.
  • They are mind-body methods: change the body (breath, posture, movement) and attention, and the mind follows. The group is honest about range and evidence, though. Mindfulness, yoga and tai chi have a real evidence base; the energy-healing claims of reiki and pranic healing have weak or inconclusive evidence, and must never replace proper medical or psychological care.

How it actually works (the mechanism of change)

  • The main change engine is better self-regulation of the nervous system. Slow breathing, relaxation and steady movement switch on the body's calming (parasympathetic) side — heart rate, blood pressure, muscle tension and the stress hormone cortisol all drop — which is the body's natural opposite of the fight-or-flight stress response.
  • Meditation adds a mental skill called decentring (learning to watch a thought as just a passing mental event, not a fact or a command): by watching worries drift past like clouds instead of being swept along by them, the person ruminates less and accepts more. For the energy-healing practices, any benefit is best explained by this same relaxation, focused attention and positive expectancy — not by a special energy, for which there is no credible proof.

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, techniques, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, these approaches come from Asian philosophical and healing traditions, chiefly Indian, Buddhist, Taoist and Japanese. Many have since been adapted into structured, sometimes evidence-based, interventions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Yoga comes from ancient India and was systematised by Patanjali in the Yoga Sutras as Ashtanga, or eight-limbed yoga. Later came Hatha yoga and types such as Raja, Karma, Bhakti and Jnana yoga. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The basis of yoga is union (the joining of body, mind and spirit, which is the literal meaning of the word yoga). It is reached through the eight limbs: ethical restraints, observances, postures, breath control, sense withdrawal, concentration, meditation and absorption. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In therapy, yoga techniques are gentle asanas, pranayama breathing and relaxation. Well-known studies at NIMHANS and meta-analyses report real benefit as an add-on for mild to moderate depression and anxiety. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Meditation comes from Hindu, Buddhist and Taoist contemplative traditions. It was adapted clinically as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction by Jon Kabat-Zinn in 1979, and as Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy by Segal, Williams and Teasdale. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The basis of meditation is training attention and decentring. Decentring means learning to watch your thoughts as passing mental events, not facts, like watching clouds drift by. This lowers rumination and raises acceptance and emotion control. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The evidence for meditation is genuine. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy is recommended for preventing relapse in recurrent depression. A major review by Goyal and colleagues in 2014 found moderate evidence for anxiety, depression and pain. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Shavasana, the corpse pose, is the final relaxation posture of a yoga session. The related Yoga Nidra (a guided deep relaxation, sometimes called 'yogic sleep', where you stay aware while the body fully rests) was popularised by Swami Satyananda Saraswati. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The basis of shavasana is a relaxation response like Benson's. It lowers sympathetic arousal, heart rate, blood pressure and muscle tension, much like Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation. Small trials show less anxiety and better sleep. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Pranic healing was developed by Master Choa Kok Sui in 1987. It holds that disease comes from depleted or congested prana in an energy body, treated by scanning, cleansing and energising the field, usually without touch. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The evidence for pranic healing is very limited and poorly controlled. There is no credible evidence for a detectable energy field. Any benefit is probably relaxation, attention and expectancy, and it must not replace evidence-based treatment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Reiki comes from Mikao Usui in Japan in 1922. The practitioner channels a universal life energy through the hands to the client, who lies clothed while set hand positions are used. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The evidence for reiki is weak and at high risk of bias. Reviews such as the Cochrane review for anxiety and depression find insufficient evidence. So it is best seen as a complementary comfort measure that should not delay treatment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Tai chi comes from seventeenth-century China, from Taoist philosophy and martial arts. It is often called meditation in motion, using slow, flowing, coordinated movements with deep breathing and focused attention. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The evidence for tai chi is reasonably good. Meta-analyses report less stress, anxiety and depression, benefit as an add-on in late-life depression, and value for fall prevention, chronic pain and cardiac rehabilitation. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Other related approaches, offered under 'etc.', include Morita and Naikan therapy from Japan and qigong. Morita teaches accepting your feelings and doing what needs to be done; Naikan uses structured introspection to build gratitude. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The common basis and mechanism across these practices is better self-regulation: raised parasympathetic and vagal tone, lowered cortisol and arousal, and better attention and emotion control. This explains much of their benefit. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The honest outcome and indications are clear. Mindfulness, yoga and tai chi have a real evidence base as add-ons for stress, anxiety, depression and chronic pain. Reiki and pranic healing have weak or inconclusive evidence and carry the risk of replacing proper care. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, these approaches fit Indian and Asian clients' cultural beliefs and are low in cost and side effects. But their evidence is of variable quality: strongest for mindfulness-based therapies and weakest for energy-healing claims that remain untestable. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Oriental approaches at a glance
  • Named practices: yoga, meditation, shavasana (and Yoga Nidra), pranic healing, reiki, tai chi (etc.).
  • Basis: attention, breath and relaxation; self-regulation, raised vagal tone and lowered arousal.
  • Good evidence: mindfulness (MBCT for relapse), yoga and tai chi as adjuncts for anxiety and depression.
  • Weak or inconclusive: reiki and pranic healing energy healing; benefit likely relaxation and expectancy.
  • Cautions: must not replace medical or psychological care; watch for unqualified practitioners.
Mnemonic
YMSPRT
Yoga, Meditation, Shavasana, Pranic healing, Reiki and Tai chi — the named oriental approaches; mindfulness, yoga and tai chi have a real evidence base, while reiki and pranic healing have weak or inconclusive evidence.

Key sources & references

Key sources: the books used and the primary and online sources consulted for the affective-psychotherapies content.

Books

  • Gerald Corey — Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy (psychodynamic, humanistic, existential, Gestalt, person-centred and Adlerian therapies).
  • Richard S. Sharf — Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling (theory, techniques and process of each approach).
  • Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding — Current Psychotherapies (comparative overview of the major systems).
  • Glen O. Gabbard — Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text (psychodynamic technique, transference and the therapeutic frame).
  • Nancy McWilliams — Psychoanalytic Diagnosis and Psychoanalytic Case Formulation (psychodynamic formulation and defences).
  • Sigmund Freud — The Interpretation of Dreams and Introductory Lectures on Psycho-Analysis (the foundations of psychoanalysis).
  • Carl R. Rogers — Client-Centered Therapy and On Becoming a Person (the person-centred approach and the core conditions).
  • Irvin D. Yalom — Existential Psychotherapy (death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness).
  • Viktor E. Frankl — Man's Search for Meaning (logotherapy, paradoxical intention and dereflection).
  • Frederick S. Perls, Ralph Hefferline & Paul Goodman — Gestalt Therapy (awareness, contact and the here-and-now).
  • Alfred Adler — The Science of Living and Understanding Human Nature (Individual Psychology, inferiority and social interest).
  • Eric Berne — Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy and Games People Play (ego states, transactions, games and scripts).
  • William Glasser — Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry and Choice Theory (choice theory, basic needs and the WDEP system).
  • Milton H. Erickson & Ernest L. Rossi — Hypnotherapy: An Exploratory Casebook (permissive, indirect and utilisation hypnotherapy).
  • Virginia M. Axline — Play Therapy and Dibs: In Search of Self (non-directive, child-centred play therapy and its eight principles).
  • Jacob L. Moreno — Psychodrama (spontaneity, role theory and the five instruments of action-based group therapy).
