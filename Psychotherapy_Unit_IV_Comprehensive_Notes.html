1Psychodynamic psychotherapy

Psychodynamic psychotherapy is the modern, briefer descendant of Freudian psychoanalysis that treats symptoms as meaningful compromises between unconscious wishes, reality and conscience, and uses the relationship, free association and interpretation to make the unconscious conscious and bring lasting change.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology characterises psychodynamic psychotherapy (therapy that works with unconscious conflict and the therapy relationship) as a form of therapy derived from psychoanalysis that focuses on unconscious processes as they show in present behaviour, aiming at self-awareness and insight into the influence of the past on the present. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Theoretical view Freud founded psychoanalysis with Breuer's 'talking cure', moving from hypnosis and catharsis to free association and interpretation, and set its aim in the formula that where id was, there ego shall be. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • Theoretical view Later schools extended the approach into ego psychology, object relations, self psychology and attachment, so that modern psychodynamic therapy is face-to-face and briefer than classical analysis while keeping its core ideas. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin the approach begins with Freud and Breuer and the case of Anna O., the so-called talking cure, out of which Freud built psychoanalysis from hypnosis and catharsis toward free association and dream interpretation. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • Its later origin runs through Jung, Adler, Klein and others including Anna Freud, Winnicott, Kohut and Bowlby, so that object relations, self psychology and attachment all grew from the one psychoanalytic root into today's briefer forms. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • The first basis is psychic determinism (the view that nothing mental is accidental and every symptom has a cause), the idea that behaviour and symptoms have meaning and causes rather than arising by chance, so that even slips of the tongue are read as significant. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • A central basis is the unconscious, described through the topographic model of conscious, preconscious and unconscious and the structural model in which the id, ego and superego pursue pleasure, reality and conscience. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • The developmental basis is the psychosexual stages, oral, anal, phallic, latency and genital, where excessive frustration or indulgence can cause fixation (getting stuck at an early developmental stage) that later shapes personality and symptoms. [Nancy McWilliams, Psychoanalytic Diagnosis]
  • Anxiety and the defence mechanisms form another basis, so that repression, denial, projection, displacement and sublimation keep unacceptable wishes out of awareness while quietly steering conduct. [Nancy McWilliams, Psychoanalytic Diagnosis]
  • In formulation, a symptom is read as a compromise formation (a symptom that partly satisfies a wish and partly defends against it) between a repressed wish, the demands of reality and the superego, so it symbolises an underlying conflict and yields secondary gain. [Nancy McWilliams, Psychoanalytic Diagnosis]
  • The guiding goal is to make the unconscious conscious, giving the client insight into hidden conflicts and patterns, strengthening the ego, maturing the defences and so producing lasting change rather than mere symptom removal. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • The therapist's stance is a key procedure, combining neutrality (not taking sides among the client's wishes, defences and conscience), abstinence and relative anonymity so the therapist acts as a blank screen onto which the client's patterns can be projected. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • The fundamental technique is free association (saying whatever comes to mind without censoring it), in which the client is asked to voice every passing thought, because the gaps and detours reveal where the real conflict lies. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • Dream analysis is a classic technique, treating dreams as the royal road to the unconscious and distinguishing the remembered manifest content from the hidden latent content reached by exploring the dream's distortions. [Sigmund Freud, The Interpretation of Dreams]
  • Interpretation is the therapist's main tool, tactfully and at the right moment explaining the unconscious meaning of associations, dreams and symptoms, so that what was warded off can be put into words and understood. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Two further techniques are the analysis of resistance, noting lateness, silence or intellectualising, and the analysis of transference (exploring the client's transfer of old relationship patterns onto the therapist), which lets old patterns be relived and understood in the room. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • In stages and process the work moves from an opening phase of assessment, contract and alliance, through the emergence and working through of transference and resistance, to a planned termination that reviews and consolidates the gains. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • A distinctive process idea is working through (repeatedly revisiting an insight until it produces real change), the patient and slow repetition of insights across many sessions, since a single interpretation rarely shifts a deeply rooted pattern on its own. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • The outcome sought is reduced symptoms, greater insight, more flexible and mature defences and healthier relationships, and gains often continue to grow after therapy ends in what is called a sleeper effect. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Indications favour clients with good ego strength, psychological-mindedness and motivation facing neurotic, personality or interpersonal problems, while acute psychosis, high suicidality and crisis states are contraindications better met by supportive work. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • In research and current status, well-known reviews report that psychodynamic therapy produces substantial and lasting benefit, with short-term psychodynamic therapy supported for depression and anxiety and specialised forms helping borderline personality disorder, while classical long analysis remains hard to study. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Psychodynamic therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Freud and Breuer, extended by ego psychology, object relations, self psychology and attachment.
  • Basis: psychic determinism, the unconscious, id-ego-superego, psychosexual stages, defences.
  • Formulation: the symptom is a compromise formation that symbolises unconscious conflict.
  • Technique: free association, dream analysis, interpretation, analysis of resistance and transference.
  • Process: opening, transference, working through, termination; indicated for ego-strong neurotic clients.
Mnemonic
FIDO-WT
Free association, Interpretation, Dream analysis, (analysis of) resistance and transferO, Working through and Termination — the techniques and stages of psychodynamic therapy; the aim throughout is to make the unconscious conscious.

2Brief psychotherapy

Brief psychotherapy is a planned, time-limited treatment that works on one agreed focus with an active, directive therapist, using the deadline itself to mobilise motivation, so that symptoms ease and functioning is restored without attempting a full personality overhaul.

Characterisations by classic authors and models
  • Theoretical view Brief therapies share a planned time limit, a single focus and an active therapist, and they rest on the finding that most improvement happens early, with roughly half of clients improved within about eight sessions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Theoretical view Named models include Malan, Sifneos, Mann and Davanloo with their focal, short-term anxiety-provoking, time-limited and intensive short-term dynamic therapies, alongside interpersonal therapy and solution-focused brief therapy. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Guideline The World Health Organization promotes brief, structured psychological interventions in routine care, framing them as effective help delivered through respectful, collaborative conversation within a few sessions. [World Health Organization, mhGAP Intervention Guide]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, brief therapy grows from Ferenczi and Rank's active technique and set time limits in the 1920s and from Alexander and French's idea of a corrective emotional experience in 1946. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Its origin then branches into many models, including Malan, Sifneos, Mann and Davanloo in the psychodynamic stream and de Shazer and Berg's solution-focused therapy in the systemic stream. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A practical origin driver was service pressure, since cost, demand and the evidence that most gains come early pushed clinicians toward shorter, planned treatments. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is that the time limit is therapeutic, because a known end date mobilises motivation, concentrates the work and guards against the regression and dependency that an open-ended course can breed. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A second basis is a tight focus (a single agreed problem or theme that organises the whole treatment), so the therapist is active, directive and selective, steering the sessions back to the one theme rather than following every new concern. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A third basis is optimism about change, emphasising the here-and-now, the client's strengths and the assumption that small changes can set off larger ones, especially in the solution-focused models. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In formulation the therapist builds a focal formulation, naming a recurring interpersonal pattern, a current loss or an unresolved conflict tied to the present problem, which keeps the brief work on target. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A well-known formulation grid is interpersonal therapy's four problem areas, namely grief, role disputes, role transitions and interpersonal deficits, one of which is chosen as the focus. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The goals are modest and clear, to resolve the focal problem, relieve symptoms and restore previous functioning, plus teaching the client skills to use later, rather than reconstructing the whole personality. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A core procedure is careful patient selection, and Sifneos' criteria look for a circumscribed complaint, at least one meaningful past relationship, psychological-mindedness, motivation and the capacity to tolerate anxiety. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The contract is itself a technique, typically 6 to 20 sessions, often around twelve to sixteen, with a fixed end date agreed at the outset so that both parties work against the clock. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Dynamic techniques include focused interpretation, selective attention and inattention, gentle challenge of defences and the deliberate use of positive transference to keep the client engaged with the focus. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Solution-focused techniques are distinctive, among them the miracle question (asking how life would look if the problem vanished overnight), scaling questions, hunting for exceptions and complimenting the client's existing resources. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In stages and process the work runs from assessment and selection in the first few sessions, through an active middle phase on the focus with a mid-point review, to an explicit termination that consolidates gains. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A defining process feature is that termination is discussed from the start, so the ending is planned, the gains are reviewed and a booster or follow-up session may be arranged rather than the therapy simply trailing off. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome is symptom relief and improved functioning, and many comparisons find brief therapy as effective as longer therapy for common disorders such as depression, anxiety and adjustment problems. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Indications are acute and focal problems, adjustment disorders, mild to moderate depression and anxiety, grief and crises in motivated clients, while chronic multi-problem conditions and severe high-risk states are contraindications. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status the evidence is strong for interpersonal therapy and brief cognitive-behavioural therapy and supportive for short-term psychodynamic and solution-focused work, and brief models are now standard in primary care, student counselling and telehealth. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
Brief psychotherapy at a glance
  • Origin: Ferenczi and Rank, Alexander and French; models by Malan, Sifneos, Mann, Davanloo, de Shazer.
  • Basis: the time limit is therapeutic; a single focus; an active, directive therapist.
  • Formulation: a focal theme; interpersonal therapy's grief, disputes, transitions and deficits.
  • Technique: selection criteria, a fixed contract, focused interpretation, solution-focused questions.
  • Process: assessment, focused middle phase, planned termination; strong evidence for IPT and brief CBT.
Mnemonic
FAST
Focus (one theme), Active and directive therapist, Selection (clear criteria) and Time limit (fixed end date) — the four features that define brief psychotherapy; the deadline itself is used to drive the work.

3Humanistic psychotherapy

Humanistic psychotherapy is the third force in psychology, a growth-oriented movement that trusts the person's own actualising tendency, centres subjective experience, free will and the whole person, and makes the genuine therapeutic relationship, rather than technique, the main agent of change.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology characterises humanistic therapy (an approach stressing growth, choice and the whole person) as an approach arising from humanistic psychology that emphasises the person's capacity for growth, self-direction and self-actualisation and the quality of the therapeutic relationship. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Theoretical view The movement began as the third force in the 1950s and 1960s, reacting against the determinism of psychoanalysis and the mechanism of behaviourism, and drew figures such as Maslow, Rogers, May, Frankl and Perls. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Maslow's hierarchy of needs, from physiological and safety needs up to self-actualisation (becoming the fullest version of oneself), frames distress as what happens when growth needs are blocked. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, humanistic therapy emerged in the 1950s and 1960s as the self-styled third force, a deliberate reaction against both psychoanalytic determinism and behaviourist mechanism, with the Association for Humanistic Psychology founded in 1961. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Its origin gathers several figures under one banner, since Maslow, Rogers, May, Frankl and Perls all stressed growth and choice, and its roots lie in phenomenology and existential philosophy. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The first basis is that people are inherently good and growth-oriented, carrying an actualising tendency (an innate drive toward growth and fulfilment) that moves them toward fulfilment when conditions allow. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A second basis is the emphasis on subjective experience, free will, choice and responsibility, treating the person's own point of view and the here-and-now as the proper focus rather than drives or conditioning. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A third basis is holism, the view of the whole person in context, so the approach resists reducing a human being to symptoms, reflexes or diagnostic labels. [Raymond J. Corsini & Danny Wedding, Current Psychotherapies]
  • A fourth basis, from Maslow, is the hierarchy of needs, in which physiological, safety, belonging and esteem needs build toward self-actualisation, and blocked growth needs produce distress. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In formulation, distress is understood as natural growth being blocked by a hostile environment, by conditions of worth (approval given only when a person meets others' standards), or by losing touch with one's own experience. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A key formulation idea is that symptoms reflect disconnection from experience rather than disease, so the aim is to restore awareness and self-direction rather than to correct a defect. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The goals are self-awareness, self-acceptance, authenticity and personal growth, helping the person take responsibility and move toward self-actualisation in their own way. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A defining procedure is that the relationship itself is the main agent of change, so a genuine, accepting and empathic presence matters more than any specific technique. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Core techniques are empathic listening, reflection and unconditional acceptance, offered in a non-directive, client-led manner that follows the person rather than steering them. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Experiential techniques are also used, including focusing on the bodily felt sense and chair work, which help the person contact and process emotion in the present moment. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In stages and process the work is client-led and unfolding rather than staged by the therapist, moving as the person feels safe enough to explore, deepen awareness and reclaim ownership of their experience. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A process hallmark is genuine presence and congruence from the therapist, which lowers the client's defences and frees the actualising tendency to resume its natural course. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is increased self-esteem, congruence and emotional awareness, together with greater relationship satisfaction and a more internal sense of direction. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Indications cover mild to moderate distress, identity and relationship problems, depression and anxiety and clients seeking growth, while severe psychosis or a need for structured skills training are better served as a contraindication by more directive work. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In research and current status, well-known meta-analyses by Elliott and colleagues report that humanistic-experiential therapies produce large, stable gains and are broadly equivalent to cognitive-behavioural therapy in many comparisons. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The approach remains alive in modern forms such as emotion-focused therapy and motivational interviewing, though critics note that its concepts are vague, hard to test and perhaps over-optimistic about human nature. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Humanistic therapy at a glance
  • Origin: the 1950s to 1960s third force; Maslow, Rogers, May, Frankl, Perls; phenomenological roots.
  • Basis: innate goodness and an actualising tendency; subjective experience, free will, the whole person.
  • Formulation: distress is blocked growth, conditions of worth and disconnection from experience.
  • Technique: empathic listening, reflection, unconditional acceptance; relationship over technique.
  • Outcome and research: growth and congruence; Elliott et al. show large gains, equivalent to CBT.
Mnemonic
GROWTH
Growth-oriented view, Relationship as the agent of change, Own actualising tendency, Whole person, Third force and Here-and-now — the humanistic essentials; it trusts the person to move toward self-actualisation.

4Existential psychotherapy

Existential psychotherapy helps people face the unavoidable givens of existence, death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness, so that the anxiety these provoke is met honestly and turned into authentic, responsible and meaningful living rather than avoided through symptoms.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Yalom frames existential therapy around four ultimate concerns (the unavoidable givens of existence that provoke anxiety), namely death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness, and holds that confronting them produces anxiety that avoidance turns into symptoms. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Frankl's logotherapy centres the will to meaning, and his Man's Search for Meaning, formed from his concentration-camp experience, argues that meaning can be found even in unavoidable suffering. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • Theoretical view The approach draws on Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger and Sartre and on clinicians such as Binswanger, Boss and May, giving it a strongly philosophical character. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, existential therapy has philosophical roots in Kierkegaard, Nietzsche, Heidegger and Sartre and clinical roots in Binswanger and Boss's Daseinsanalysis, which applied phenomenology to the suffering person. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Its clinical origin runs through Frankl's logotherapy from the 1930s, May's Existence in 1958 and Yalom's Existential Psychotherapy in 1980, which organised the field for English-speaking clinicians. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • The first basis is that existence involves unavoidable ultimate concerns, so that death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness confront everyone and generate an existential anxiety (the dread that arises from facing the basic conditions of existence) that cannot simply be cured away. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • A second basis is that existence precedes essence, meaning we create ourselves through our choices, so that authenticity (living in accordance with one's own values and choices) stands against living in bad faith. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A third basis, from Heidegger, is being-in-the-world across the physical, social and personal worlds, together with Frankl's conviction that a will to meaning is the deepest human motive. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • In formulation, psychopathology is read as inauthentic living, the avoidance of freedom and responsibility, loss of meaning or failure to cope with death and isolation. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Frankl's distinctive formulation is the existential vacuum (a sense of emptiness and boredom from loss of meaning), an emptiness and boredom arising from lost meaning that he called noogenic neurosis. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • The goals are to help clients face life's givens honestly and to increase awareness, responsibility, authenticity and meaning, committing to action even in the presence of anxiety. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • A core procedure is phenomenological exploration of the client's own worldview, with the therapist bracketing assumptions and using Socratic dialogue to examine values, choices and avoidances. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The here-and-now relationship is used as a laboratory, and encouraging 'I' statements that own experience helps the client take responsibility rather than treating problems as things that merely happen to them. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • A signature logotherapy technique is paradoxical intention (deliberately trying to do the very thing one fears), in which the client intends the feared symptom, which often loosens the anticipatory anxiety behind phobias and insomnia. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • A second logotherapy technique is dereflection (shifting attention away from the self toward meaningful activity), turning the client's focus from anxious self-monitoring onto meaningful activity and other people. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • A third technique is attitudinal modification, helping the client find meaning in unavoidable suffering, which is central when the situation itself cannot be changed. [Viktor E. Frankl, Man’s Search for Meaning]
  • In stages and process the work establishes a genuine relationship and explores the worldview, then identifies patterns of avoidance and inauthenticity, deepens awareness of freedom and responsibility, and ends in committed action. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • The process is collaborative and exploratory rather than technique-driven, so the therapist accompanies the client in facing hard truths rather than fixing a defect. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is greater meaning, purpose and authenticity and an increased ability to tolerate anxiety, so the person lives more deliberately in the face of life's givens. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
  • Indications include existential crises such as life transitions, midlife and bereavement, terminal or chronic illness, and depression marked by emptiness, while acute psychosis and a wish for quick symptom control are relative contraindications. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In research and current status, the evidence is moderate, with meaning-centred interventions showing benefit for meaning, mood and quality of life, especially in people with cancer, while critics note the concepts are abstract and hard to operationalise. [Irvin D. Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy]
Existential therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Kierkegaard to Sartre; Binswanger, Boss, Frankl, May and Yalom.
  • Basis: four ultimate concerns (death, freedom, isolation, meaninglessness); existence precedes essence.
  • Formulation: inauthentic living, avoided responsibility and the existential vacuum.
  • Technique: phenomenological and Socratic dialogue; paradoxical intention, dereflection, attitude change.
  • Outcome and research: meaning and authenticity; moderate evidence, strong in meaning-centred care.
Mnemonic
DFIM
Death, Freedom, Isolation and Meaninglessness — Yalom's four ultimate concerns at the heart of existential therapy; facing them honestly is what turns dread into authentic living.

5Gestalt therapy

Gestalt therapy is an experiential, here-and-now approach developed by Fritz and Laura Perls in which awareness itself is curative, the therapist sets up experiments rather than giving interpretations, and the client completes unfinished business and reowns split-off parts to become more whole.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology characterises gestalt therapy (an experiential therapy centred on present awareness and the whole person) as an experiential approach that emphasises present experience and awareness, integrating feelings, perceptions and actions into the whole person. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Theoretical view It was developed by Fritz Perls, Laura Perls and Paul Goodman from psychoanalysis, Gestalt psychology and field theory, and was made popular in the 1960s at the Esalen Institute. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Its guiding idea is the paradoxical theory of change (change happens when a person accepts what they are, not when they force what they are not), which Beisser stated as change coming when a person fully becomes what they are. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, Gestalt therapy was developed by Fritz and Laura Perls with Paul Goodman in the 1940s and 1950s, drawing on psychoanalysis, Gestalt psychology, Reich's body work and Lewin's field theory. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Its origin reached a wide public in the 1960s at Esalen, where Perls' charismatic demonstrations made the empty-chair style famous, for better and worse. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is holism and field theory, the view that person and environment form a single field, so behaviour is understood in context rather than in isolation. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A central basis is the here-and-now, focusing on the what and how of present experience rather than the why, because awareness in the present moment is held to be curative. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A further basis is figure and ground, in which the dominant need becomes the figure (the need or interest that stands out from the background at a given moment) against the background, guided by organismic self-regulation. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Another basis is unfinished business (unexpressed feelings from the past that intrude on the present), the unexpressed feelings that press for attention and interfere with living fully in the present. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In formulation, disturbance arises from interruptions to contact at the boundary, named as introjection, projection, retroflection (turning back onto oneself what one would like to do to or get from others), deflection and confluence. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A further formulation idea is Perls' layers of neurosis, the phony, phobic, impasse, implosive and explosive layers through which a client passes toward authentic contact. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A vivid formulation is the top-dog versus underdog split, the inner conflict between the demanding shoulds and the resistant wants that keeps a person stuck. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The goals are to increase awareness, take responsibility, integrate split-off parts, complete unfinished business and shift from relying on the environment toward self-support. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The best-known technique is the empty-chair technique (speaking to an imagined person or part of oneself placed in an empty chair), a dialogue with an absent person or a part of the self that brings a conflict into the room. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Further techniques include exaggerating a gesture, reversal and staying with the feeling, each designed as an experiment that heightens awareness rather than as an interpretation. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Language techniques matter too, replacing 'it' and 'you' with 'I' statements and 'I can't' with 'I won't', so the client owns experience and choice in the present tense. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Dream work is distinctive, treating every part of a dream as a projected piece of the dreamer, who then plays each part to reclaim it. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In stages and process the work moves from contact and the I-Thou relationship, through awareness work and experiments that heighten contact, to integration and closure of what was unfinished. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The therapist's process stance is authentic, present and non-interpretative, offering experiments and honest feedback rather than analysing from a distance. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is greater awareness, responsibility and emotional expression, fuller integration and more vital interpersonal functioning. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Indications include anxiety, depression, interpersonal and psychosomatic problems and trauma-related unfinished business, while very confrontational experiments are a contraindication for fragile or psychotic clients who may be overwhelmed. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In research and current status, the empirical base is limited but growing, and the strongest support comes through emotion-focused therapy, where Greenberg and colleagues show that empty-chair work for unfinished business is effective. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Gestalt therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Fritz and Laura Perls with Goodman; Gestalt psychology and field theory; Esalen in the 1960s.
  • Basis: holism, the here-and-now, figure and ground, unfinished business, awareness is curative.
  • Formulation: contact disturbances, layers of neurosis, top-dog versus underdog.
  • Technique: the empty chair, exaggeration, 'I' language, dream work; experiments not interpretations.
  • Outcome and research: awareness and integration; limited base, supported via emotion-focused therapy.
Mnemonic
AWARE
Awareness is curative, Who-and-how not why, Appropriate experiments (empty chair), Responsibility and 'I' language, and Ending unfinished business — the Gestalt essentials in the here-and-now.

6Person-centred (client-centred) therapy

Person-centred therapy is Carl Rogers' non-directive approach in which the therapist's congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathy are held to be the necessary and sufficient conditions that free the client's actualising tendency, so the client, as the expert on themselves, directs their own change.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology characterises person-centered therapy (Rogers' non-directive therapy built on empathy, congruence and acceptance) as the non-directive form of therapy developed by Carl Rogers, in which the therapist's genuineness, unconditional positive regard and empathic understanding foster the client's growth. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Theoretical view Rogers introduced non-directive counselling in 1942 and set out his six necessary and sufficient conditions of therapeutic change in 1957, grounding the approach in the actualising tendency. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • Theoretical view The approach is firmly humanistic yet distinct, since it is Rogers' specific model built on the core conditions, whereas humanistic therapy names the broader third-force movement. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, the approach is the work of Carl Rogers, whose Counseling and Psychotherapy of 1942 introduced non-directive counselling and whose Client-Centered Therapy of 1951 named the model. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • Rogers later broadened it into the person-centred approach applied to education, groups and conflict resolution, and he pioneered recording and studying sessions, which began an empirical tradition in therapy research. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The foundational basis is the actualising tendency (the single inbuilt drive of the organism toward growth and fulfilment), the single motivating force that moves every organism toward growth and fulfilment when conditions allow. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • A second basis is the self-concept and the ideal self, together with the organismic valuing process by which a healthy person trusts their own experiencing as a guide. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • A third basis is conditions of worth (the sense that one is valued only when meeting others' standards), formed when love and approval are conditional, which lead the person to distort or deny experiences that conflict with them. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A fourth basis is incongruence, the gap between self-concept and experience, or between real and ideal self, which produces anxiety and defensiveness, so that restoring congruence is the mark of health. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • In formulation, problems are understood as incongruence caused by conditions of worth, so therapy is not a technical repair but the provision of conditions in which the actualising tendency can resume. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The decisive formulation claim is Rogers' six conditions, of which the three therapist-offered core conditions are congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathy. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • The first core condition is congruence (the therapist being genuine and integrated, not playing a role), the therapist being real and integrated in the relationship rather than hiding behind a professional facade. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • The second core condition is unconditional positive regard (accepting and prizing the client without conditions), a warm, non-judgemental acceptance of the client as a person whatever they bring. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • The third core condition is empathy, an accurate understanding of the client's internal frame of reference that is communicated back so the client feels deeply understood. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The guiding goals are greater congruence and self-acceptance, an internal locus of evaluation and movement toward becoming a fully functioning person who is open to experience. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • The defining procedure is being non-directive, so the therapist does not interpret, diagnose, advise or reassure, but trusts the client as the expert on themselves. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The core techniques, insofar as the model allows them, are active and reflective listening, reflection of feeling, paraphrase, clarification and the acceptance of silences, offered within a genuine relationship. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In stages and process, Rogers described a seven-stage continuum along which the client moves from rigid remoteness from feelings toward fluid, immediate and trusted experiencing. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In practice the process is that the client talks while the therapist reflects, and as the client feels understood and accepted, defences lower, self-exploration deepens and self-directed change emerges. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is increased self-acceptance and congruence, an internal locus of evaluation, reduced anxiety and defensiveness and improved, self-directed relationships. [Carl R. Rogers, Client-Centered Therapy]
  • Indications are wide, covering mild to moderate emotional, identity and relationship problems, grief, adolescents and student counselling, while clients wanting direct advice, severe disorders or acute crises are relative contraindications. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In research and current status, Rogers was a pioneer of therapy research, and meta-analyses by Elliott and colleagues find that empathy, positive regard and congruence correlate modestly but reliably with outcome, though critics challenge the claim that the conditions are sufficient on their own. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Person-centred therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Carl Rogers (1942 non-directive counselling, 1951 client-centred therapy, 1957 six conditions).
  • Basis: the actualising tendency, self-concept, conditions of worth and incongruence.
  • Formulation: problems are incongruence from conditions of worth; provide the core conditions.
  • Technique: non-directive; congruence, unconditional positive regard and empathy; reflective listening.
  • Outcome and research: congruence and self-direction; Rogers pioneered research; conditions aid outcome.
Mnemonic
CUE
Congruence, Unconditional positive regard and Empathy — Rogers' three therapist-offered core conditions in person-centred therapy; given these, the client's own actualising tendency does the healing.

7Adlerian therapy (Individual Psychology)

Adlerian therapy, or Individual Psychology, is Alfred Adler's approach that views the person as a whole, goal-directed and socially embedded, treats problems as discouragement and mistaken lifestyle beliefs rather than illness, and re-educates the client toward courage, social interest and useful contribution.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Adler founded Individual Psychology after breaking from Freud's circle in 1911, stressing that the person is an indivisible whole whose behaviour is goal-directed and shaped by social context. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Theoretical view Its hallmark concept is social interest (an innate capacity and willingness to cooperate and contribute to others), which Adler treated as the very measure of mental health. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Theoretical view The approach was carried forward by Dreikurs, Ansbacher, Mosak and Dinkmeyer into child guidance, family work and parent education. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, Adlerian therapy was founded by Alfred Adler, a Viennese physician and early member of Freud's circle who broke away in 1911 to establish Individual Psychology. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Its origin also includes Rudolf Dreikurs, who developed Adler's ideas for children, families and the democratic classroom, and Adler's founding of the first child-guidance clinics in 1920s Vienna. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The first basis is holism and teleology, the view that the person is indivisible and that behaviour is pulled toward a future goal, a fictional finalism (a guiding self-created goal that directs a person's striving) that organises striving. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A central basis is inferiority and compensation, so that natural feelings of inferiority spur a striving for superiority or significance, which can become either healthy ambition or a self-defeating complex. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • A defining basis is social interest, the innate capacity to cooperate and contribute, which Adler made the yardstick of mental health, so that well-being grows with connection to others. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • A further basis is the lifestyle (the unique pattern of beliefs and goals formed early in childhood), the unique pattern of beliefs and goals formed by about age five from the family constellation and birth order. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Another basis is the contrast between private logic and common sense, where idiosyncratic mistaken beliefs, or basic mistakes, steer a person away from cooperative living. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In formulation, problems arise from discouragement, mistaken beliefs and low social interest, so difficulties are seen as problems of living rather than illness, as a way of life that is not working. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A practical formulation tool is Dreikurs' four mistaken goals of children's misbehaviour, namely attention, power, revenge and a display of inadequacy. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The goals are to increase social interest and belonging, correct mistaken lifestyle beliefs, build courage and self-worth and replace inferiority striving with useful contribution. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • A core procedure is the four-phase structure named by Dreikurs, beginning with establishing an equal, respectful and collaborative relationship. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The second phase is lifestyle assessment, analysing the family constellation, birth order and especially early recollections (the earliest memories, read as clues to current beliefs), which are read as projective clues to present beliefs. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The third phase is insight, in which the therapist discloses and interprets the client's mistaken beliefs, private logic and hidden goals in a warm, tentative way. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The fourth phase is reorientation, turning insight into action through new choices, supported by encouragement, the central Adlerian technique. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Distinctive techniques include acting as if (behaving as though one already had the desired quality), spitting in the client's soup to make a payoff unattractive, catching oneself, the push-button technique and setting tasks and homework. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • In stages and process, the therapist works as an encouraging teacher or coach, treating therapy as an educational and re-educational process rather than a cure of disease. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The outcome sought is reduced inferiority and discouragement, increased courage, greater social interest and a more adaptive lifestyle and relationships. [Alfred Adler, The Science of Living]
  • Indications are broad, covering children and adolescents, parent education and family therapy, school and career counselling and group work, including collectivist cultures whose values fit social interest, while severe psychopathology needs adaptation. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • In research and current status, the evidence is limited but positive, with support for Adlerian parent education such as STEP, Adlerian play therapy and counselling, and Adler's ideas are recognised as anticipating cognitive, humanistic and family-systems therapies. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Adlerian therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Alfred Adler (Individual Psychology, 1911); Dreikurs, Ansbacher, Mosak, Dinkmeyer.
  • Basis: holism and teleology, inferiority and compensation, social interest, lifestyle and birth order.
  • Formulation: discouragement and mistaken beliefs; problems of living, not illness.
  • Technique: four phases (relationship, assessment, insight, reorientation); encouragement, acting as if.
  • Outcome and research: courage and social interest; limited but positive evidence; anticipated CBT.
Mnemonic
RAIR
Relationship, Assessment (lifestyle), Insight and Reorientation — Dreikurs' four Adlerian phases; the engine throughout is encouragement, aiming the client toward social interest.

8Transactional analysis (TA)

Transactional analysis is Eric Berne's social-psychiatry approach that reads personality as three ego states, Parent, Adult and Child, and treats problems as contaminated ego states, repetitive games and a limiting life script, aiming by a clear contract to restore autonomy and game-free intimacy.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Eric Berne founded transactional analysis in the late 1950s, setting out its theory in Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy and popularising it in Games People Play, and he defined the aim of therapy as autonomy through awareness, spontaneity and intimacy. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view A transaction (a single exchange of a stimulus and a response between two people) is the unit of social interaction, and Berne read every exchange as issuing from one of three ego states, the Parent, the Adult and the Child. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view The approach was carried forward by Harris, Steiner, Karpman and the Gouldings, who added the OK life positions, scripts, the drama triangle and redecision therapy. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, transactional analysis was created by Eric Berne, a Canadian-born psychiatrist who broke from psychoanalysis to build a plain-language social psychiatry, publishing Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy in 1961 and Games People Play in 1964. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Its origin then widened through Thomas Harris, whose I'm OK, You're OK spread the life positions, Claude Steiner on scripts, Stephen Karpman on the drama triangle and the Gouldings on redecision therapy. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The core basis is the three ego states (consistent patterns of feeling and behaviour, called Parent, Adult and Child), where the Parent is taught and judging, the Adult is thinking and objective and the Child is feeling and spontaneous. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • A second basis is the analysis of transactions, which may be complementary and so keep talk flowing, crossed and so break it off, or ulterior (carrying a hidden psychological message under the social one) and so drive games. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A third basis is strokes, the units of recognition people hunger for, so that positive and negative strokes, conditional and unconditional, shape how a person structures time from withdrawal through rituals and pastimes to games and intimacy. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • A fourth basis is games and rackets, which are games (repetitive ulterior transactions that end in a predictable bad feeling) such as 'Why don't you, Yes but', paying off in a familiar racket feeling that props up the script. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • A fifth basis is the life script (an unconscious life plan decided in childhood from parental messages), built from injunctions such as 'don't be close', counter-injunction drivers such as 'be perfect' and the child's own early decisions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A sixth basis is the four life positions of the OK Corral, running from the healthy 'I'm OK, you're OK' to the blaming, the depressive and the hopeless positions that colour a person's whole outlook. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In formulation, problems are traced to contaminated or excluded ego states, destructive games, a negative script and an unhealthy life position, so the therapist maps which ego state and game is driving the complaint. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A vivid formulation tool is Karpman's drama triangle, in which clients switch between the roles of Persecutor, Victim and Rescuer, and the healthier exit is assertiveness, vulnerability and genuine caring. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are autonomy and game-free intimacy, a free and uncontaminated Adult, a revised script sometimes called a script cure, better social control and relief of the presenting symptom. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • A central procedure is the contract, a clear and mutual agreement on goals and responsibilities that gives TA its collaborative, adult-to-adult, 'OK, OK' stance from the very first session. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The main technique is the four kinds of analysis, namely structural analysis of ego states, transactional analysis of exchanges, game analysis of hidden payoffs and script analysis of the life plan. [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Berne set out eight therapeutic operations, from interrogation, specification and confrontation through explanation, illustration, confirmation and interpretation to crystallisation (the moment the client can hold a new, freeing position for themselves). [Eric Berne, Transactional Analysis in Psychotherapy]
  • Further techniques include the egogram, permissions and protection, redecision work that re-experiences and changes early decisions through chair work, stroke work, role play and homework, usually in a group format. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the work runs from contracting and assessment, through strengthening and decontaminating the Adult and the deconfusion and redecision of the Child, to relearning new behaviours and finally autonomy and termination. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is greater autonomy, clearer communication, an end to games, a revised script and improved self-esteem, with the simple language letting clients keep using the ideas after therapy. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Indications cover neurotic, relationship and communication problems, personality issues, couples and families, groups, education and organisations, while acute psychosis is a contraindication served only by adapted forms. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status the evidence is modest, with some reviews reporting positive outcomes but few rigorous trials, yet TA stays widely taught and accredited by the ITAA and EATA despite criticism of its jargon and limited validation. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Transactional analysis at a glance
  • Origin: Eric Berne (1961 to 1964); extended by Harris, Steiner, Karpman and the Gouldings.
  • Basis: Parent-Adult-Child ego states, transactions, strokes, games, script, OK life positions.
  • Formulation: contaminated ego states, games, negative script; Karpman's drama triangle.
  • Technique: the contract; structural, transactional, game and script analysis; redecision chair work.
  • Process: contract, decontaminate Adult, deconfuse Child, relearn, autonomy; modest evidence base.
Mnemonic
PAC
Parent, Adult and Child — the three ego states at the heart of transactional analysis; the contract and the four analyses (structural, transactional, game and script) work to free the Adult and reach autonomy.

9Reality therapy (and choice theory)

Reality therapy is William Glasser's present-focused approach built on choice theory, which holds that all behaviour is chosen to meet five basic needs, and uses the WDEP system within a warm, non-blaming relationship to help clients evaluate and plan more effective, responsible choices.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view William Glasser developed reality therapy in the 1960s and later grounded it in choice theory (the view that we choose almost all of our behaviour to meet basic needs), the idea that we can directly control only our own acting and thinking. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • Theoretical view Glasser held that most psychological problems are relationship problems, rooted in unsatisfying connections, and so he rejected diagnostic labels in favour of helping clients make more effective choices. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • Theoretical view Robert Wubbolding systematised the practice as the WDEP system set inside a cycle of counselling that first builds a trusting, friendly environment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, reality therapy was founded by William Glasser, an American psychiatrist who, dissatisfied with Freudian therapy, developed his ideas working at the Ventura School for Girls and a veterans' hospital, publishing Reality Therapy in 1965. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • Its origin continued as Glasser's control theory of the 1980s was renamed choice theory in 1998, and Robert Wubbolding built the WDEP system and the cycle of counselling that organise practice today. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is that all behaviour is chosen, since we can directly control only our acting and thinking, which in turn steer our feeling and physiology, a picture Glasser likened to the four wheels of a car. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • A second basis is the five genetic basic needs (survival, love and belonging, power, freedom and fun), which drive all behaviour, with love and belonging treated as the most important for mental health. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • A third basis is the quality world (an inner picture album of the people and things a person most wants), against which we compare the perceived world, so that a gap between the two produces frustration and behaviour. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fourth basis is that relationships are central, so the seven deadly habits of criticising, blaming and complaining damage connection while the seven caring habits of supporting, listening and respecting rebuild it. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fifth basis is responsibility and a present focus, meeting one's needs without stopping others from meeting theirs, with no excuses, no blame and no punishment, and almost no attention paid to the past or to symptoms. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • In formulation, the client is seen as having an unmet need, usually love and belonging, and choosing ineffective behaviours such as withdrawing, 'depressing' or blaming to try to satisfy it, forming a failure identity. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are to help clients take responsibility and make effective choices, improve their relationships and meet needs in ways that are responsible, realistic and right, Glasser's three Rs. [William Glasser, Reality Therapy: A New Approach to Psychiatry]
  • The core procedure is Wubbolding's cycle of counselling, which first builds the environment, a trusting and friendly bond with humour and good listening while avoiding arguing and criticising. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The main technique is the WDEP system, where W explores wants and needs and D clarifies current total behaviour and direction. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The WDEP technique continues with E, the client's own self-evaluation of whether their behaviour is helping and is realistic, and P, a plan that is committed to and workable. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A memorable planning technique is the SAMI2C3 plan (a plan that is simple, attainable, measurable, immediate, involving, controlled, committed and consistent), which makes the client's commitment concrete and checkable. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Further techniques include skilful questioning, metaphor and humour, paradox, role play, contracts and self-disclosure, with the gentle confrontation 'Is what you are doing helping?' and a firm refusal to accept excuses. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the therapist builds the environment, establishes what the client wants, explores current behaviour, guides self-evaluation, helps form and commit to a plan, and then follows up, revising the plan if it fails. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is greater responsibility, a stronger internal locus of control, better relationships, higher self-esteem and more satisfying behaviour, achieved in a short and practical course of work. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications are broad, covering schools, adolescents and offenders, corrections, addictions, marital and group counselling and the workplace, while severe mental illness or trauma needing exploration is a caution. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status the evidence is moderate but often quasi-experimental, with studies in schools, correctional and cross-cultural settings showing gains in self-esteem, locus of control and relationships, though critics attack its rejection of diagnosis and simple view of choice. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Reality therapy at a glance
  • Origin: William Glasser (Reality Therapy, 1965; Choice Theory, 1998); Wubbolding's WDEP and cycle.
  • Basis: all behaviour is chosen; five basic needs; the quality world; relationships are central.
  • Formulation: an unmet need and ineffective chosen behaviours; a failure identity.
  • Technique: build the environment, then Wants, Doing, Evaluation and Planning (a SAMI2C3 plan).
  • Process: present-focused, no excuses, no punishment; moderate, mostly quasi-experimental evidence.
Mnemonic
WDEP
Wants, Doing (and direction), Evaluation and Planning — Wubbolding's four procedures of reality therapy, applied inside a warm, non-blaming environment to help clients choose more effective, responsible behaviour.

10Supportive psychotherapy

Supportive psychotherapy is the ego-strengthening end of the supportive-expressive continuum, which aims not to uncover the unconscious but to maintain and restore ego functions, self-esteem and coping through a warm, active relationship, using reassurance, advice, praise and problem solving.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Supportive psychotherapy sits at the supportive end of Luborsky's supportive-expressive continuum, aiming to maintain or improve ego functions and self-esteem rather than to uncover unconscious conflict. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Theoretical view It treats the therapeutic relationship as the main agent of change, with the therapist acting as a trusted, caring support or auxiliary ego (a dependable figure who lends ego strength the client temporarily lacks). [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Theoretical view It was described by Wallerstein, Rockland, and Winston, Pinsker and McCullough for patients with limited ego strength who need strengthening rather than insight. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, supportive psychotherapy grows from the earliest practice of psychiatry, its reassurance, advice and moral treatment, and from psychodynamic therapy at the supportive end of Luborsky's supportive-expressive continuum. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Its origin was given shape by Wallerstein's Menninger project, Rockland, and Winston, Pinsker and McCullough, who formalised it for patients whose fragile personality cannot tolerate uncovering work. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is a shift of aim, since the goal is to maintain, restore or improve ego functions and self-esteem and to shore up existing defences and coping rather than to make the unconscious conscious. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A second basis is that the relationship itself heals, so a dependable, warm therapist serves as an auxiliary ego, lending the strength and stability the client temporarily lacks. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A third basis is a here-and-now focus, keeping the work on present stresses, coping and daily functioning rather than on childhood reconstruction or deep transference. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fourth basis is the handling of transference, since positive transference is used but not interpreted, and deep interpretation is deliberately avoided so the client is not destabilised. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In formulation, symptoms are read as arising when stress overwhelms a person's coping resources, so that an overwhelmed ego or a fragile personality needs its functioning and adaptation strengthened. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are symptom relief and stabilisation, improved adaptation and coping, support through crisis or illness, prevention of relapse and rehospitalisation, and enhanced self-esteem. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A defining procedure is the therapist's stance, which is active, warm, flexible and often directive, meeting the client where they are rather than waiting in silence for associations. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Core techniques include reassurance, encouragement and praise, empathic listening and ventilation (letting the client talk out and release pent-up feelings), and advice and guidance where the client cannot yet decide alone. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Further techniques are psychoeducation, explanation and clarification, anticipatory guidance that prepares the client for stressful events, and practical problem solving and reality testing. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Additional techniques include reframing, environmental modification such as involving family or arranging services, modelling, limit-setting, the strengthening of adaptive defences and timely crisis intervention. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The frequency and duration are flexible, ranging from weekly to monthly sessions and from brief and time-limited work to long-term maintenance for chronic conditions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the work moves from assessing strengths and ego capacities, through building the alliance and defining problems and goals, to selecting and applying supportive techniques. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The later process is monitoring progress with a gradual reduction or ongoing maintenance and an open-ended, flexible ending rather than a sharply scheduled termination. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is better coping and daily functioning, reduced distress and fewer relapses, with stabilisation valued above the deeper personality change pursued by expressive therapy. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications are wide, covering schizophrenia and other severe mental illness, bipolar and personality disorders with low ego strength, crises and bereavement, chronic or terminal medical illness, the elderly and those with low insight. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, Wallerstein's Menninger study found supportive therapy produced more lasting change than expected, and later work shows it comparable to other therapies, yet it is under-researched and often used only as a control condition, and critics warn it may foster dependency. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Supportive psychotherapy at a glance
  • Origin: psychiatry's reassurance and advice; the supportive end of Luborsky's continuum; Wallerstein.
  • Basis: strengthen ego functions and self-esteem; the relationship heals; here-and-now focus.
  • Formulation: stress overwhelms coping; an overwhelmed ego or fragile personality.
  • Technique: reassurance, praise, ventilation, advice, psychoeducation, problem solving, reframing.
  • Indications: severe mental illness, crises, medical illness; comparable but under-researched.
Mnemonic
SUPPORT
Strengthen the ego, Use the relationship, Praise and reassure, Problem-solve, Offer advice and psychoeducation, Reality-test and Reframe, Tackle the crisis — the aims and techniques of supportive psychotherapy, which bolsters coping rather than uncovering the unconscious.

11Clinical hypnotherapy

Clinical hypnotherapy uses hypnosis, a state of focused attention and heightened responsiveness to suggestion, to help clients modify perception, emotion and behaviour, moving through pre-induction, induction, deepening, a therapeutic phase and re-alerting to treat pain, anxiety and habit problems, usually as an adjunct.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The American Psychological Association (Division 30) characterises hypnosis (a state of focused attention with reduced peripheral awareness and heightened response to suggestion) as a state of consciousness involving focused attention and reduced peripheral awareness, marked by an enhanced capacity for response to suggestion. [American Psychological Association (Division 30, Society of Psychological Hypnosis)]
  • Theoretical view Milton H. Erickson shaped modern practice with a permissive, indirect and utilisation approach, using the client's own imagery and language rather than authoritarian commands. [Milton H. Erickson & Ernest L. Rossi, Hypnotherapy: An Exploratory Casebook]
  • Theoretical view Theories divide into state views, such as Hilgard's neodissociation, and non-state or socio-cognitive views that stress role-taking, expectancy and imagination. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, hypnotherapy descends from Mesmer's animal magnetism in the 1770s, through Puysegur's artificial somnambulism and James Braid, who in 1843 coined the word hypnosis from the Greek for sleep. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Its origin runs on through the Nancy school of Liebeault and Bernheim, who saw hypnosis as suggestion, Charcot and Janet on hysteria and dissociation, and Breuer and Freud's early catharsis under hypnosis, which Freud later abandoned. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The modern origin is dominated by Milton Erickson's permissive and indirect method and by Hilgard's neodissociation theory with the Stanford scales, alongside socio-cognitive theorists such as Barber, Sarbin, Spanos and Kirsch. [Milton H. Erickson & Ernest L. Rossi, Hypnotherapy: An Exploratory Casebook]
  • The first basis is the nature of the hypnotic state, marked by absorption, focused attention and suggestibility with reduced critical analysis, and it is important that hypnosis is not sleep and not a loss of control. [American Psychological Association (Division 30, Society of Psychological Hypnosis)]
  • A second basis is the debate between state and non-state theories, where one camp sees an altered state of dissociation and the other sees role-taking, expectancy and imagination, with neuroimaging showing changes in attention and default-mode networks. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A third basis is hypnotisability (a stable trait describing how readily a person responds to hypnotic suggestion), a fairly stable trait that varies across people and is measured by scales such as the Stanford and Harvard group scales. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In formulation, symptoms, habits, pain and anxiety are seen as maintained by automatic learned responses and by negative beliefs and imagery, which focused attention and suggestion can be used to modify. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are symptom control, especially of pain and anxiety, habit change, relaxation, increased self-efficacy and ego-strength, and the teaching of self-hypnosis so the client can manage symptoms alone. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A core procedure is the pre-induction phase, which secures consent, takes a history, explains hypnosis and dispels the myths of lost control or revealed secrets, and builds expectancy and rapport. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The induction technique then narrows attention through eye fixation, progressive relaxation, breathing or imagery, with Ericksonian inductions using confusion, storytelling and pacing and leading rather than direct command. [Milton H. Erickson & Ernest L. Rossi, Hypnotherapy: An Exploratory Casebook]
  • The deepening technique follows, using counting down, staircase imagery or fractionation (repeatedly lifting and re-inducing trance to deepen it), which intensify the hypnotic experience before therapeutic work begins. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In the therapeutic phase the clinician uses direct and indirect suggestions, metaphors and ego-strengthening, together with imagery and rehearsal and, where indicated, hypnotic analgesia such as glove anaesthesia. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Deeper techniques in that phase include age regression and hypnoanalysis (uncovering and working through buried material under hypnosis), post-hypnotic suggestions and anchoring, desensitisation and parts therapy, all used with caution. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The final technique is termination, a careful re-alerting or dehypnosis by counting up and reorienting the client, followed by discussion and the teaching of self-hypnosis for ongoing practice. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the session therefore moves through the fixed sequence of pre-induction, induction, deepening, therapeutic phase and termination, each step prepared before the next is attempted. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is reduced pain and anxiety, changed habits and greater self-efficacy, and gains are strengthened when the client practises self-hypnosis between sessions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications include chronic and procedural pain, labour and dental pain, anxiety and phobias, irritable bowel syndrome, insomnia, habit disorders and psychosomatic problems, while contraindications and cautions cover psychosis, severe personality disorder and the risk of false memory in forensic use. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, meta-analyses find that adding hypnosis to cognitive-behavioural therapy improves outcomes, with strong evidence for pain, irritable bowel syndrome and procedural distress, moderate evidence for anxiety and weaker or inconclusive evidence for smoking and weight loss. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Clinical hypnotherapy at a glance
  • Origin: Mesmer and Braid; the Nancy school; Erickson, Hilgard and the socio-cognitive theorists.
  • Basis: focused attention and suggestibility (APA Division 30); state vs non-state; hypnotisability.
  • Formulation: symptoms kept up by automatic responses and imagery, modified by suggestion.
  • Technique: pre-induction, induction, deepening, therapeutic suggestions, re-alerting; self-hypnosis.
  • Indications: pain, IBS, anxiety, habits; strong for pain and IBS, weak for smoking and weight.
Mnemonic
PIDTT
Pre-induction, Induction, Deepening, Therapeutic phase and Termination — the five stages of a hypnotherapy session; throughout, hypnosis is focused attention with a heightened response to suggestion, used mostly as an adjunct.

12Play therapy

Play therapy treats play as the child's natural language, so that toys become words and play becomes speech, and it uses a safe playroom and a trained therapist, in directive or non-directive form, to help children express and master emotions, trauma and relationship difficulties.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Play therapy rests on the idea that play is the child's natural language, so that, in Landreth's phrase, toys are the child's words and play is the child's speech. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • Theoretical view Virginia Axline set out the non-directive, child-centred form in 1947 and its eight guiding principles, applying Rogerian conditions to children. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • Theoretical view Its roots reach back to Anna Freud and Melanie Klein, who first used play analytically, and forward to Landreth's child-centred play therapy and Knell's cognitive-behavioural play therapy. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, play therapy begins with Freud's case of Little Hans in 1909 and Hermine Hug-Hellmuth, the first to use play with children, before Anna Freud and Melanie Klein made play central to child analysis. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Its origin then runs through Virginia Axline's non-directive play therapy in 1947 and her book Dibs: In Search of Self, and on to the Guerneys' filial therapy, Landreth's child-centred play therapy and Schaefer's founding of the Association for Play Therapy. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • The first basis is that play is the child's natural language, since children cannot easily put abstract thoughts and feelings into words and so express them through symbolic play with toys. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • A second basis is the therapeutic powers of play (the specific ways play heals, such as communication, catharsis and relationship building), named by Schaefer as communication, emotional wellness, stronger relationships and increased personal strengths. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A third basis is that play therapy draws on several theories, the psychoanalytic, the humanistic and child-centred, the attachment, the cognitive-behavioural and the developmental, so it can be directive or non-directive. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fourth basis is Axline's eight principles, which include building a warm relationship, accepting the child exactly as they are, reflecting feelings and respecting the child's own capacity to solve problems. [Virginia M. Axline, Play Therapy]
  • In formulation, children's problems are read as blocked emotions, trauma, insecure attachment or unmet needs, which play lets them express and master safely at a symbolic distance from the real event. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are emotional expression and regulation, a better self-concept and self-esteem, social skills and coping, the resolution of trauma or conflict and a strengthened parent-child attachment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A core procedure is the playroom and its toys, sorted into real-life toys such as a doll house, acting-out and aggressive toys such as puppets and a bop bag, and creative media such as sand, clay and paint. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Non-directive techniques include tracking the child's play aloud, reflecting feelings, returning responsibility with phrases such as 'you can decide' and encouragement that follows rather than steers the child. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Limit-setting is a key technique, often taught as ACT (acknowledge the feeling, communicate the limit, target an alternative), which keeps the playroom safe without shaming the child. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Structured techniques include puppets, sandplay, drawing and storytelling, Gardner's mutual storytelling, bibliotherapy, role play, therapeutic games and cognitive-behavioural play that uses modelling and exposure. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Parent-mediated techniques are distinctive, since filial therapy and child-parent relationship therapy train parents to hold play sessions at home, and Theraplay builds structure, engagement, nurture and challenge. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process the work moves from assessment, parent consultation and consent, through relationship building as the child explores the room and tests the therapist, into the appearance of themes in the play. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The later process is a working-through phase of aggression, regression, limit-testing and dramatic play toward mastery, and then a termination for which the child is prepared early, usually across about twelve to twenty weekly sessions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is better emotional expression, behaviour and self-esteem, improved social skills and a stronger parent-child relationship, especially when parents are actively involved. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications are children of about three to twelve years with anxiety, fears, trauma and abuse, bereavement, parental divorce, adjustment and behaviour problems and selective mutism, while adolescents may need talk, art or drama instead. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, Bratton and colleagues' 2005 meta-analysis of ninety-three studies reported a large mean effect, larger when parents took part, and later child-centred play therapy reviews show moderate to large effects, though critics note methodological weaknesses. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Play therapy at a glance
  • Origin: Freud's Little Hans; Anna Freud and Klein; Axline (non-directive, 1947); Landreth's CCPT.
  • Basis: play is the child's language; the therapeutic powers of play; Axline's eight principles.
  • Formulation: blocked emotions, trauma, insecure attachment expressed through symbolic play.
  • Technique: playroom and toys, tracking, reflecting, ACT limit-setting, sandplay, filial therapy.
  • Research: Bratton et al. (2005) large effect; moderate to large for CCPT; children about 3 to 12.
Mnemonic
TRACK
Toys are words, Relationship first, Accept the child, Children-centred (non-directive) and Keep safe limits (ACT) — the heart of play therapy, in which play is the child's natural language for expressing and mastering experience.

13Psychodrama

Psychodrama is J. L. Moreno's action-based group therapy in which a protagonist enacts rather than merely describes their conflicts, using a director, auxiliary egos, an audience and a stage, so that spontaneity and new roles are restored through warm-up, action and sharing.

Characterisations by classic authors
  • Theoretical view Jacob L. Moreno created psychodrama from his Theatre of Spontaneity, coined the term group psychotherapy and developed sociometry, the measurement of relationships within a group. [Jacob L. Moreno, Psychodrama]
  • Theoretical view Its foundation is that spontaneity (a fresh, adequate response to a new or old situation) and creativity are the basis of mental health, so that pathology is a loss of spontaneity and rigid roles. [Jacob L. Moreno, Psychodrama]
  • Theoretical view Moreno described five instruments, the protagonist, the director, the auxiliary egos, the audience and the stage, that together make enactment possible. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, goals, technique, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, psychodrama was created by Jacob L. Moreno in Vienna, whose Theatre of Spontaneity in 1921 led, after he moved to the United States, to the Beacon sanatorium and the systematic method of action-based group therapy. [Jacob L. Moreno, Psychodrama]
  • Its origin also credits Zerka Moreno as a key developer, and the method went on to influence Gestalt therapy, drama therapy, role play and the behavioural rehearsal used in cognitive-behavioural work. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The first basis is that spontaneity and creativity are the foundations of mental health, so that distress is understood as a loss of spontaneity, rigid role patterns and unfinished action. [Jacob L. Moreno, Psychodrama]
  • A second basis is role theory, covering role-taking, role-playing and role-creating, so that role conflict and role rigidity are seen as central to psychological problems. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A third basis is tele (a two-way flow of genuine feeling and understanding between people), Moreno's term for the mutual empathy that binds a group, distinct from one-way transference. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A fourth basis is catharsis, encounter and surplus reality, the last letting a client enact what never happened or what is feared or wished, all worked in the here-and-now moment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In formulation, distress is traced to a limited or conflicting role repertoire, blocked spontaneity and unresolved relationships and events, which enactment brings into the open to be experienced and changed. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The goals are catharsis and release, insight and a wider role repertoire, renewed spontaneity, better interpersonal skills, the resolution of unfinished business and group cohesion and support. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A defining procedure is the use of the five instruments, the protagonist whose issue is explored, the director who guides, the auxiliary egos who play significant others, the audience who witness and the stage. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The most powerful technique is role reversal (the protagonist plays the other person to see through their eyes), which builds empathy and insight by letting the protagonist experience the other's position. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A second key technique is doubling, in which an auxiliary voices the protagonist's unspoken feelings, and mirroring, in which the protagonist watches an auxiliary portray them from the outside. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Further techniques include soliloquy, the empty chair and future projection, together with concretisation, sculpting, the social atom and the magic shop, which make abstract feelings concrete. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • A related technique is sociodrama, which turns the same action methods onto shared group or social issues rather than one person's private conflict. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In stages and process, the work opens with a warm-up in which the director builds trust and a protagonist emerges, so the group is ready for action. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The action phase then sets and enacts the scene, often moving from the present into the past, using role reversal, doubling and mirroring to reach a peak of emotion and new solutions before the scene is closed. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The closing process is sharing and integration, in which the audience offer their own similar experiences rather than analysis or advice, reducing the protagonist's isolation and supporting re-entry. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The outcome sought is emotional release and insight, improved relationships and role flexibility and the support of the group, achieved through doing rather than only talking. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Indications are group settings for trauma, grief, addictions, family and relationship issues, adolescents, social-skills training and psychiatric inpatients, while acute psychosis or a very fragile client without containment is a caution needing a skilled director. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, Kipper and Ritchie's 2003 meta-analysis reported a large effect with role reversal and doubling the most effective techniques, and later reviews show moderate positive evidence, though rigorous randomised trials remain few. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Psychodrama at a glance
  • Origin: J. L. Moreno (Theatre of Spontaneity, 1921); coined group psychotherapy and sociometry.
  • Basis: spontaneity and creativity; role theory; tele; catharsis, encounter and surplus reality.
  • Formulation: a limited role repertoire and blocked spontaneity; unresolved relationships.
  • Instruments and technique: protagonist, director, auxiliary egos, audience, stage; role reversal.
  • Process: warm-up, action, sharing; Kipper and Ritchie (2003) large effect; few rigorous RCTs.
Mnemonic
PDAAS
Protagonist, Director, Auxiliary egos, Audience and Stage — Moreno's five instruments of psychodrama; the work flows through warm-up, action and sharing, with role reversal the most powerful technique.

14Oriental approaches

Oriental approaches are Asian mind-body practices used for mental health, among them yoga, meditation, shavasana, pranic healing, reiki and tai chi, which share a focus on attention, breath and relaxation, but whose evidence ranges from genuine support for mindfulness, yoga and tai chi down to weak and inconclusive findings for energy healing.

Characterisations by professional bodies and traditions
  • Theoretical view Patanjali's Yoga Sutras characterise yoga (the union of body, mind and spirit through an eight-limbed discipline) as the cessation of the fluctuations of the mind, achieved through the eight limbs from ethical restraints to absorption. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Guideline Clinical mindfulness meditation is characterised as non-judgemental, present-moment awareness, adapted by Kabat-Zinn as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction and recommended within the World Health Organization's wider promotion of self-care for mental health. [World Health Organization, mhGAP Intervention Guide]
  • Theoretical view Energy-healing practices such as pranic healing and reiki are characterised by their own traditions as the clearing or channelling of a life energy, prana or ki, a claim for which there is no credible scientific evidence. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]

Exam points (origin, basis, formulation, techniques, process, outcome, indications, research)

  • In origin, these approaches come from Asian philosophical and healing traditions, chiefly the Indian, Buddhist, Taoist and Japanese, and many have since been adapted as structured, sometimes evidence-based, interventions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Yoga originates in ancient India and was systematised by Patanjali in the Yoga Sutras as Ashtanga or eight-limbed yoga, with later Hatha yoga and types such as Raja, Karma, Bhakti and Jnana yoga. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The basis of yoga is union (the joining of body, mind and spirit, the literal meaning of the word yoga), reached through the eight limbs of ethical restraints, observances, postures, breath control, sense withdrawal, concentration, meditation and absorption. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In therapy, yoga techniques are gentle asanas, pranayama breathing and relaxation, and well-known studies at NIMHANS and meta-analyses report real benefit as an adjunct for mild to moderate depression and anxiety. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Meditation originates in Hindu, Buddhist and Taoist contemplative traditions, and was adapted clinically as Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction by Jon Kabat-Zinn in 1979 and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy by Segal, Williams and Teasdale. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The basis of meditation is training attention and decentring, learning to observe thoughts as passing mental events rather than facts, which lowers rumination and increases acceptance and emotion regulation. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The evidence for meditation is genuine, since mindfulness-based cognitive therapy is recommended for preventing relapse in recurrent depression and a major review by Goyal and colleagues in 2014 found moderate evidence for anxiety, depression and pain. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Shavasana, the corpse pose, is the final relaxation posture of a yoga session, and the related Yoga Nidra (a guided deep relaxation also called yogic sleep) was popularised by Swami Satyananda Saraswati. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The basis of shavasana is a relaxation response like Benson's, which lowers sympathetic arousal, heart rate, blood pressure and muscle tension, resembling Jacobson's progressive muscle relaxation, with small trials showing reduced anxiety and better sleep. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Pranic healing was developed by Master Choa Kok Sui in 1987 and holds that disease results from depleted or congested prana in an energy body, treated by scanning, cleansing and energising the field, usually without touch. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The evidence for pranic healing is very limited and poorly controlled, with no credible evidence for a detectable energy field, so any benefit is probably relaxation, attention and expectancy, and it must not replace evidence-based treatment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Reiki originates with Mikao Usui in Japan in 1922 and has the practitioner channel a universal life energy through the hands to the client, who lies clothed while the practitioner uses set hand positions. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The evidence for reiki is weak and at high risk of bias, with reviews such as the Cochrane review for anxiety and depression finding insufficient evidence, so it is best regarded as a complementary comfort measure that should not delay treatment. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Tai chi originates in seventeenth-century China from Taoist philosophy and martial arts and is often called meditation in motion, using slow, flowing, coordinated movements with diaphragmatic breathing and focused attention. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The evidence for tai chi is reasonably good, with meta-analyses reporting reduced stress, anxiety and depression, benefit as an adjunct in late-life depression and value for fall prevention, chronic pain and cardiac rehabilitation. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • Other related approaches, offered under 'etc.', include Morita and Naikan therapy from Japan and qigong, where Morita teaches accepting one's feelings and doing what needs to be done and Naikan uses structured introspection to build gratitude. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The common basis and mechanism across these practices is improved self-regulation, raised parasympathetic and vagal tone, lowered cortisol and arousal and better attention and emotion regulation, which explains much of their benefit. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • The honest outcome and indications are that mindfulness, yoga and tai chi have a real evidence base as adjuncts for stress, anxiety, depression and chronic pain, while reiki and pranic healing have weak or inconclusive evidence and carry the risk of replacing proper care. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
  • In research and current status, these approaches fit Indian and Asian clients' cultural beliefs and are low in cost and side effects, but their evidence is of variable quality, strongest for mindfulness-based therapies and weakest for energy-healing claims that remain untestable. [Affective Therapies (Unit IV compiled reader)]
Oriental approaches at a glance
  • Named practices: yoga, meditation, shavasana (and Yoga Nidra), pranic healing, reiki, tai chi (etc.).
  • Basis: attention, breath and relaxation; self-regulation, raised vagal tone and lowered arousal.
  • Good evidence: mindfulness (MBCT for relapse), yoga and tai chi as adjuncts for anxiety and depression.
  • Weak or inconclusive: reiki and pranic healing energy healing; benefit likely relaxation and expectancy.
  • Cautions: must not replace medical or psychological care; watch for unqualified practitioners.
Mnemonic
YMSPRT
Yoga, Meditation, Shavasana, Pranic healing, Reiki and Tai chi — the named oriental approaches; mindfulness, yoga and tai chi have a real evidence base, while reiki and pranic healing have weak or inconclusive evidence.
