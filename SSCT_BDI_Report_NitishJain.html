Result and Interpretation

BECK DEPRESSION INVENTORY (BDI-II)

The BDI was administered to assess the presence, nature, and severity of depressive symptomatology, given the patient's reported low mood, guilt, reduced motivation, and the emotional disturbance frequently associated with chronic opioid dependence.

On the Beck Depression Inventory, the patient obtained a total score of 47, which falls within the range of severe depression. The elevated score reflects pervasive and clinically significant depressive symptomatology, including persistent low mood, marked feelings of guilt and self-blame, pessimism and hopelessness regarding the future, reduced self-worth, diminished interest and motivation, and a sense of failure and inadequacy. Somatic and vegetative features such as disturbed sleep, reduced appetite, fatigue, and loss of energy are also likely to be present, consistent with the patient's reported biological disturbances. The findings indicate substantial emotional distress with a negative view of the self, the world, and the future (the depressive cognitive triad). These results should, however, be interpreted with caution, as active opioid dependence, withdrawal-related dysphoria, financial strain, and ongoing psychosocial crisis may amplify the reported depressive symptom severity. The overall profile is consistent with the elevated Major Depression scale obtained on the MCMI-III and with the self-directed guilt and pessimism reflected in the projective findings, indicating the need for close monitoring of mood, risk assessment, and integrated management of depression alongside de-addiction treatment.

SACKS SENTENCE COMPLETION TEST (SSCT)

The SSCT was administered to explore the patient's conscious attitudes, conflicts, and emotional concerns across four major areas — family, sexual adjustment, interpersonal relationships, and self-concept. Each attitude area is rated on a 0–2 disturbance scale, and a domain is regarded as an area of significant conflict where the accumulated rating approaches or exceeds the cut-off. In the present protocol, the Self-Concept domain emerged as the most disturbed, whereas the family, sexual, and interpersonal domains reflected relatively better adjustment.

DomainInterpretation
Family Area The family domain reflects relatively adequate adjustment with only mild conflict. The patient's responses convey a largely warm and emotionally connected relationship with family members, particularly the mother, toward whom attachment and a wish for closeness are evident despite some ambivalence. Attitudes toward the father are comparatively neutral, with only mild dissatisfaction. There is a perception of the family as broadly supportive, though colored by an underlying awareness of having strained these relationships through his own behaviour. Overall, the family is experienced as a source of belonging and support, with the disturbance being limited rather than pervasive.
Sex Area The sexual domain indicates broadly adequate adjustment. The patient's attitudes toward women and toward heterosexual/marital relationships are largely conventional and socially appropriate, with an emphasis on companionship and marital life. Only minimal conflict is evident in this area, and the patient's views appear stable though somewhat idealised and simplified, reflecting limited depth in the appraisal of intimate relationships. This domain does not represent a primary area of psychological disturbance.
Interpersonal Relationships This domain suggests only mild interpersonal difficulty. The patient generally perceives friends, colleagues, and those he works with in neutral to positive terms, and reports comfort in cooperative working relationships. Mild conflict emerges chiefly in relation to authority and superior figures, where sensitivity and some wariness are apparent, and in a degree of caution about how he is regarded by others when absent. Nonetheless, interpersonal functioning is comparatively preserved, and this domain reflects situational strain rather than entrenched relational conflict.
Self-Concept The self-concept domain is the most disturbed area in the protocol and dominates the overall profile. The patient's responses reveal a markedly negative self-evaluation with pronounced feelings of inadequacy, low confidence, and doubt regarding his own abilities, especially when faced with adversity or unfavourable circumstances. There is a strong and pervasive presence of guilt, regret, and self-blame, centred on past mistakes and the consequences of his substance use. Attitudes toward the future are conflicted and predominantly pessimistic, with hopes for change coexisting with apprehension and a sense of foreshortened possibility. Prominent fears and anxieties are evident, together with unresolved conflict around unmet goals and aspirations. This constellation of severe guilt, deflated self-worth, pessimism, and fearfulness is highly consistent with the severe depressive symptomatology reflected in the BDI score and points to significant internal distress underlying the patient's maladaptive coping and continued substance dependence.

Overall Impression: The SSCT profile indicates that the patient's principal area of emotional disturbance lies within the self-concept domain — encompassing marked guilt, impaired self-worth and confidence in his own abilities, pessimism about the future, and prominent fears — while the family, sexual, and interpersonal domains reflect comparatively better adjustment. The pattern of intense self-directed guilt and negative self-appraisal converges with the severe depression identified on the BDI (score = 47) and with the depressive and self-defeating features noted on the MCMI-III, suggesting that low self-worth and unresolved guilt are central maintaining factors in the patient's depressive state and substance dependence. Therapeutic intervention focusing on guilt resolution, restructuring of negative self-perceptions, rebuilding of self-efficacy, instillation of realistic hope, and relapse prevention is indicated, alongside integrated treatment of depression and opioid dependence.
