The F40–F49 Block — Overview

The ICD-10 block F40–F48 is titled "Neurotic, stress-related and somatoform disorders". Historically these were the "neuroses" (disorders in which the person experiences distressing symptoms — anxiety, obsessions, physical complaints — but retains contact with reality and insight, in contrast to the "psychoses"). ICD-10 keeps them together largely for historical reasons and because of their frequent association with psychological causation and with stress; it explicitly notes that a mixture of symptoms is common (especially anxiety and depression together). ICD-10

ICD-11 abolished the umbrella term "neurotic" and broke this single block into several separate, mechanism-based groupings: Anxiety or fear-related disorders (6B0x); Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders (6B2x); Disorders specifically associated with stress (6B4x); Dissociative disorders (6B6x); and Disorders of bodily distress or bodily experience (6C2x). ICD-11

Biggest change to state in the exam: ICD-11 dismantled the "neurotic disorders" block and redistributed its members. In particular, OCD moved out of the anxiety disorders into its own grouping, the somatoform disorders were replaced by "bodily distress disorder", hypochondriasis moved to the OCD-related grouping, and two new stress categories (Complex PTSD, Prolonged grief disorder) were added while acute stress reaction was reclassified as a normal reaction.

F40 · Phobic Anxiety Disorders → ICD-11 6B02 / 6B03 / 6B04

ICD-10 F40 (.0 agoraphobia, .1 social phobias, .2 specific phobias, .8 other, .9 unspecified)
ICD-11 6B02 agoraphobia; 6B04 social anxiety disorder; 6B03 specific phobia
Core Anxiety evoked only/predominantly by well-defined situations or objects that are not currently dangerous
Key point The situation is characteristically avoided or endured with dread

1. Introduction

In the phobic anxiety disorders, anxiety is evoked only, or predominantly, by certain well-defined situations or objects (external to the individual) that are not currently dangerous; as a result these are characteristically avoided or endured with dread. Phobic anxiety is subjectively, physiologically, and behaviourally indistinguishable from other anxiety and may range from mild unease to terror. The person's concern may focus on individual symptoms such as palpitations or feeling faint, and is often associated with secondary fears of dying, losing control, or going mad. The anxiety is not relieved by knowing that others do not regard the situation as dangerous. Merely contemplating entry to the phobic situation usually generates anticipatory anxiety (dread in advance of facing the situation). ICD-10

ICD-10 boundary note: because the phobic object/situation must be external, fears of having a disease (nosophobia) or of being disfigured (dysmorphophobia) are classified under hypochondriacal disorder (F45.2), not here — unless the fear relates specifically to catching a disease (e.g. through contamination), in which case a specific phobia (F40.2) applies. Phobic anxiety often coexists with depression, and pre-existing phobic anxiety almost invariably worsens during a depressive episode.

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

DomainDetail
Genetic / temperamentalModerate heritability; the trait of behavioural inhibition (fearful withdrawal from novelty from early childhood) predicts later phobias, especially social anxiety.
NeurobiologicalOver-reactivity of the amygdala (the brain's fear centre) and fear circuitry; serotonin, noradrenaline, and GABA involvement.
EvolutionarySeligman's preparedness theory (we are biologically primed to acquire fears of ancestral dangers — snakes, heights, spiders — more readily than of modern hazards) explains the non-random content of phobias.

Psychological theories

  • Behavioural / Mowrer's two-factor theory: a fear is acquired by classical conditioning (pairing with a frightening experience) and then maintained by operant conditioning — avoidance is negatively reinforced by the relief it brings. This is the direct rationale for exposure therapy.
  • Vicarious learning & information transmission (Bandura): phobias can be learned by watching others' fear or by being told something is dangerous.
  • Cognitive theory: overestimation of danger/probability and underestimation of coping; in social anxiety, heightened self-focused attention and fear of negative evaluation.
  • Psychodynamic (Freud, "Little Hans"): the phobic object symbolically represents an unconscious conflict onto which anxiety is displaced.

3. Epidemiology

  • Among the most common mental disorders: specific phobias lifetime ~7–12%; social anxiety ~7–12%; agoraphobia ~1–2%. Other evidence
  • More common in women (specific and agoraphobia markedly; social anxiety more equal).
  • Onset: specific phobias in childhood; social anxiety in adolescence; agoraphobia in early adulthood.

4. General diagnostic guidelines (ICD-10) & requirements (ICD-11)

ICD-10 General guidelines (all must be met)

  1. The psychological or autonomic symptoms are primary manifestations of anxiety, not secondary to other symptoms such as delusions or obsessional thoughts.
  2. The anxiety is restricted to (or predominates in) the presence of the particular phobic object or situation.
  3. The phobic situation is avoided whenever possible.

ICD-11 Common requirements

Marked and excessive fear or anxiety that consistently occurs on exposure or anticipation of the specific stimulus, is out of proportion to actual danger, and leads to avoidance; the pattern persists for an extended period (typically ≥ several months) and causes significant distress or impairment in functioning.

Detailed subtypes — each with criteria, includes/excludes, and differential diagnosis

F40.0 — Agoraphobia → ICD-11 6B02

Concept: agoraphobia (literally "fear of the marketplace" — a well-defined cluster of fears of situations from which escape might be difficult or embarrassing, or help unavailable, especially if panic-like symptoms occur): fears of crowds, public places, travelling alone, and travelling away from home. It is the most incapacitating of the phobias; avoidance can leave some people entirely housebound.

Clinical detail: depression, depersonalization, obsessional symptoms, and social phobias may also be present but must not dominate. Absence of an escape route is central; some agoraphobics experience little anxiety because they consistently avoid their phobic situations. Panic attacks are a common accompaniment.

ICD-10 criteria

  1. Psychological/autonomic symptoms must be primary manifestations of anxiety, not secondary to other symptoms (e.g. delusions or obsessions).
  2. Anxiety must be restricted to (or occur mainly in) at least two of: crowds; public places; travelling away from home; travelling alone.
  3. Avoidance of the phobic situation must be, or have been, a prominent feature.

Sub-codes: F40.00 without panic disorder; F40.01 with panic disorder.

ICD-11 (6B02)

Marked fear/anxiety in multiple situations (crowds, public places, travelling alone or away from home, using public transport) because escape might be difficult or help unavailable, especially if incapacitating or embarrassing symptoms (e.g. panic) occur; situations are avoided, entered only with a companion, or endured with intense fear, for ≥ several months, with significant impairment. A panic attack specifier may be added.

Includes: agoraphobia with or without panic disorder; panic disorder with agoraphobia.
Excludes: agoraphobia is not diagnosed if the avoidance is better explained by another disorder (e.g. avoidance solely due to fear of humiliation → social phobia; avoidance due to obsessional fear of contamination → OCD).
Differential diagnosis (F40.0)
  • Social phobia — if the fear is specifically of scrutiny/embarrassment rather than of escape being blocked. ICD-10 rule: if the distinction is very difficult, precedence is given to agoraphobia.
  • Depressive disorder — agoraphobia and depression often coexist and both contribute to being housebound; a depressive diagnosis is added only if a full depressive syndrome can be clearly identified (and if the person was already significantly depressed when the phobia first appeared, depression may be primary).
  • Panic disorder — panic without agoraphobic avoidance; if avoidance of agoraphobic situations is prominent, code agoraphobia (with the panic specifier).
  • Psychotic disorder — avoidance driven by delusions rather than fear of escape being difficult.

F40.1 — Social phobias → ICD-11 6B04 (Social anxiety disorder)

Concept: fear of scrutiny by other people in comparatively small groups (as opposed to crowds), leading to avoidance of social situations, with a fear of behaving in a way that will be humiliating or embarrassing. May be discrete (restricted to eating in public, public speaking, or encounters with the opposite sex) or diffuse (almost all social situations outside the family circle).

Clinical detail: usually associated with low self-esteem and fear of criticism. Complaints may be of blushing, hand tremor, nausea, or urgency/fear of micturition, and the person may be convinced that one of these secondary manifestations is the primary problem. May progress to panic attacks. Avoidance is often marked and, in extreme cases, may result in almost complete social isolation.

ICD-10 criteria

  1. Symptoms must be primarily manifestations of anxiety, not secondary to other symptoms (delusions/obsessions).
  2. Anxiety must be restricted to, or predominate in, particular social situations.
  3. Avoidance of the phobic situation must be prominent.

ICD-11 (6B04)

Marked, excessive fear/anxiety in one or more social situations where the person may be scrutinised (social interaction, being observed, performing), with fear of acting in a way (or showing anxiety) that will be negatively evaluated; situations are avoided or endured with intense fear, for ≥ several months, with significant impairment.

Includes: anthropophobia; social neurosis; social anxiety disorder.
Excludes: agoraphobia (F40.0) where the fear is of escape being difficult; body dysmorphic / hypochondriacal preoccupation (F45.2); delusional conviction (psychotic disorders).
Differential diagnosis (F40.1)
  • Agoraphobia — distinguished by the focus of fear (scrutiny/embarrassment vs escape/help unavailable); if very difficult, ICD-10 gives precedence to agoraphobia.
  • Normal shyness — social phobia is more intense, persistent, causes marked distress/impairment, and prompts avoidance.
  • Depressive disorder — social withdrawal secondary to low mood; a depressive diagnosis is made only if a full depressive syndrome is present.
  • Panic disorder / agoraphobia — if panic attacks are cued only by social scrutiny, social phobia is the primary diagnosis.
  • Autism spectrum / avoidant personality disorder — pervasive, lifelong social difficulty rather than situation-specific fear of scrutiny.

F40.2 — Specific (isolated) phobias → ICD-11 6B03

Concept: phobias restricted to highly specific situations such as proximity to particular animals, heights (acrophobia), thunder (astraphobia), darkness, flying, closed spaces (claustrophobia), using public toilets, eating certain foods, dentistry, or the sight of blood or injury. Though the triggering situation is discrete, contact with it can evoke panic as in agoraphobia or social phobia.

Recognised clusters (identifying detail): animal type; natural-environment type (heights, storms, water); blood-injection-injury type; situational type (lifts, flying, enclosed spaces); and other. Specific phobias usually arise in childhood or early adult life and, if untreated, can persist for decades. The degree of resulting disability depends on how easy it is to avoid the trigger.

ICD-10 criteria

  1. Symptoms must be primarily manifestations of anxiety, not secondary to other symptoms (delusions/obsessions).
  2. Anxiety must be restricted to the presence of the particular phobic object or situation.
  3. The phobic situation is avoided whenever possible.

ICD-11 (6B03)

Marked, excessive fear/anxiety that consistently occurs on exposure or anticipation of a specific object or situation, out of proportion to actual danger, leading to avoidance, for ≥ several months, with significant distress/impairment.

Includes: acrophobia; animal phobias; claustrophobia; simple phobia; examination/dental/blood phobia.
Excludes: dysmorphophobia (non-delusional) and nosophobia → hypochondriacal disorder (F45.2); agoraphobia (F40.0); social phobias (F40.1).
Differential diagnosis (F40.2)
  • Distinguishing feature vs other phobias: it is usual for there to be no other psychiatric symptoms, in contrast to agoraphobia and social phobia.
  • Blood-injury phobia is unique — it leads to bradycardia and sometimes syncope (fainting) via a vasovagal response, rather than the tachycardia of other phobias.
  • Hypochondriacal disorder (F45.2) — fears of specific diseases (cancer, heart disease, venereal infection) are classified here unless they relate to a specific situation where the disease might be acquired (then specific phobia).
  • OCD — if avoidance is driven by an obsessional fear of contamination with rituals.

F40.8 other phobic anxiety disorders; F40.9 phobic anxiety disorder, unspecified.

5. Comorbidities (F40 group)

  • Depression (very common, esp. agoraphobia and social anxiety), other anxiety disorders, panic disorder, and alcohol/substance misuse (used to self-medicate social anxiety).

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

Targets: agora = places/escape; social = people/scrutiny; specific = one object/situation. Maintenance = "AA": Avoidance → Anxiety relief → reinforcement (so treatment = exposure). Blood-injury phobia = the one that makes you faint (vasovagal).

7. Treatment (F40 group)

  • Psychological (first-line): graded exposure, systematic desensitisation, and CBT (cognitive restructuring); social skills training for social anxiety; applied tension (tensing muscles to raise blood pressure and prevent fainting) for blood-injury phobia.
  • Pharmacological: SSRIs/SNRIs for social anxiety and agoraphobia; beta-blockers for performance anxiety; short-term benzodiazepines only with caution. Kaplan & Sadock

F41 · Other Anxiety Disorders → ICD-11 6B01 / 6B00 / 6A73

ICD-10 F41 (.0 panic disorder, .1 GAD, .2 mixed anxiety-depressive, .3 other mixed, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B01 panic disorder; 6B00 GAD; 6A73 mixed depressive and anxiety (moved to depressive disorders)
Core Anxiety is the major symptom and is not restricted to any particular situation
Key point "Free-floating" anxiety, in contrast to the phobias

1. Introduction

Here manifestations of anxiety are the major symptoms and are not restricted to any particular environmental situation. Depressive and obsessional symptoms, and even some phobic anxiety, may be present but are clearly secondary or less severe. ICD-10

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

DomainDetail
GeneticModerate heritability; shared vulnerability with depression (a general internalising/neuroticism factor).
NeurobiologicalDysregulated serotonin, noradrenaline, GABA; amygdala hyper-reactivity; for panic, a hypersensitive "suffocation alarm" and locus-coeruleus over-activity; provocation by CO₂ and sodium lactate.

Psychological theories

  • Clark's cognitive model of panic: panic arises from the catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily sensations (reading a racing heart as "I'm having a heart attack"), creating a vicious cycle of escalating fear and arousal.
  • Beck's cognitive theory of anxiety: systematic overestimation of danger and underestimation of coping, with threat-biased attention.
  • Intolerance-of-uncertainty model (GAD): worry driven by low tolerance of uncertainty plus positive beliefs about worry.
  • Borkovec's avoidance theory of worry (GAD): verbal worry suppresses distressing imagery/arousal, negatively reinforcing the worry habit.

3. Epidemiology

  • GAD: lifetime ~5–6%; women ~2:1; often chronic/fluctuating. Panic disorder: lifetime ~2–5%; women more affected; early-adult onset. High comorbidity with depression. Other evidence

Detailed subtypes — each with criteria, includes/excludes, and differential diagnosis

F41.0 — Panic disorder [episodic paroxysmal anxiety] → ICD-11 6B01

Concept: recurrent attacks of severe anxiety (panic) that are not restricted to any particular situation and are therefore unpredictable.

Clinical detail: a panic attack is a discrete episode of intense fear peaking within minutes, with sudden palpitations, chest pain, choking, dizziness, and feelings of unreality (depersonalization/derealization), almost always with a secondary fear of dying, losing control, or going mad. Individual attacks often last only minutes; a crescendo of fear and autonomic symptoms leads to a hurried exit. Between attacks there is anticipatory anxiety ("fear of fear"). Frequently followed by persistent avoidance and secondary agoraphobia.

ICD-10 criteria

Several severe attacks of autonomic anxiety within about one month, occurring in circumstances where there is no objective danger, not confined to known or predictable situations, and with comparative freedom from anxiety symptoms between attacks (though anticipatory anxiety is common).

ICD-11 (6B01)

Recurrent unexpected panic attacks not restricted to particular stimuli/situations, followed by persistent concern about recurrence or their significance, or maladaptive behaviour to avoid recurrence, over ≥ several weeks. (A "panic attack" specifier is used elsewhere when attacks are cued by another disorder.)

Includes: panic attack; panic state.
Excludes: panic disorder is NOT the main diagnosis if panic attacks occur as part of an established phobic disorder (they reflect the phobia), or if the criteria for a depressive disorder are met at the same time (panic is then secondary). Also exclude if attacks are due to a medical condition (e.g. hyperthyroidism) or substance.
Differential diagnosis (F41.0)
  • Phobic disorders — if panic attacks arise only on exposure to a specific phobic situation, the phobia is the diagnosis, not panic disorder.
  • Depressive disorder — panic attacks may be secondary to depression (particularly in men); if depressive criteria are met concurrently, panic disorder should not be given as the main diagnosis.
  • Organic/medical causes — hyperthyroidism, phaeochromocytoma, arrhythmia, hypoglycaemia, and stimulant/withdrawal states can mimic panic.
  • GAD — continuous free-floating anxiety rather than discrete unexpected attacks.

F41.1 — Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) → ICD-11 6B00

Concept: generalised and persistent anxiety that is "free-floating" (not restricted to, or even strongly predominating in, any particular environmental circumstances).

Clinical detail (the three symptom clusters): (1) apprehension (worries about future misfortune, feeling "on edge", difficulty concentrating); (2) motor tension (restless fidgeting, tension headaches, trembling, inability to relax); and (3) autonomic overactivity (light-headedness, sweating, tachycardia or tachypnoea, epigastric discomfort, dizziness, dry mouth). Fears that the sufferer or a relative will shortly become ill or have an accident are common. In children, prominent need for reassurance and recurrent somatic complaints.

ICD-10 criteria

The person must have primary symptoms of anxiety most days for at least several weeks at a time, and usually for several months. These symptoms should usually involve elements of: (a) apprehension; (b) motor tension; and (c) autonomic overactivity.

ICD-11 (6B00)

Marked symptoms of anxiety with either general apprehension ("free-floating anxiety") or excessive worry about multiple everyday events, present most days for at least several months, accompanied by muscle tension, restlessness, autonomic overactivity, difficulty concentrating, irritability, or sleep disturbance, causing significant distress/impairment.

Includes: anxiety neurosis; anxiety reaction; anxiety state.
Excludes: neurasthenia (F48.0); if criteria for a depressive episode, phobic disorder, panic disorder, or OCD are met and dominate, code those instead.
Differential diagnosis (F41.1)
  • Depressive disorder — anxiety and depression frequently coexist; if a full depressive episode is present it usually takes precedence.
  • Panic disorder — discrete unexpected attacks rather than continuous worry.
  • Phobic disorders — anxiety tied to specific triggers.
  • Organic causes — hyperthyroidism, caffeine/stimulant excess, medication effects, and withdrawal states.
  • Adjustment disorder — anxiety clearly tied to a recent identifiable stressor and time-limited.

F41.2 — Mixed anxiety and depressive disorder → ICD-11 6A73

Concept: symptoms of both anxiety and depression are present, but neither set is severe enough to justify a diagnosis on its own. Very common in primary care.

ICD-10 criteria

Symptoms of anxiety and depression are both present, with neither clearly predominating, and neither set of symptoms sufficient to meet the criteria for an anxiety disorder or a depressive episode considered separately; some autonomic symptoms (tremor, palpitations, dry mouth, etc.) must be present at least intermittently.

ICD-11

Moved out of the anxiety grouping to the depressive disorders as 6A73 (mixed depressive and anxiety disorder) — a notable classification change: both depressive and anxiety symptoms present for ≥ 2 weeks, with neither syndrome meeting full criteria alone.

Excludes: if either the anxiety or the depressive syndrome is severe enough to meet its own criteria, that specific diagnosis is used instead; persistent/severe worry alone → GAD.
Differential diagnosis (F41.2)
  • Depressive episode or GAD — diagnosed instead when either syndrome reaches full criteria.
  • Adjustment disorder — if clearly tied to a recent stressor.
  • Dysthymia — chronic sub-threshold depression without the mixed-anxiety pattern.

F41.3 other mixed anxiety disorders; F41.8 other specified anxiety disorders; F41.9 anxiety disorder, unspecified.

Comorbidities, memory aids, and treatment (F41 group)

Comorbidities

  • Depression (very high), other anxiety disorders, alcohol/sedative misuse, and physical conditions that both cause and are worsened by anxiety.

Mnemonics

Panic: "unexpected attacks + fear of the fear". GAD — "WATCHERS": Worry, Anxiety, Tension, Concentration poor, Hyperarousal/irritability, Energy low, Restlessness, Sleep disturbance. Panic model = catastrophic misreading of body sensations.

  • Panic disorder: CBT (interoceptive exposure + cognitive restructuring) is highly effective; SSRIs/SNRIs first-line; avoid long-term benzodiazepines.
  • GAD: CBT (worry management, applied relaxation, problem-solving); SSRIs/SNRIs first-line; pregabalin/buspirone as options. Kaplan & Sadock

F42 · Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder → ICD-11 6B20

ICD-10 F42 (.0 predominantly obsessional thoughts/ruminations, .1 predominantly compulsive acts, .2 mixed, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B20 — heads its own grouping "Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders" (6B2x)
Core Recurrent obsessions and/or compulsions
Key change ICD-11 moved OCD OUT of the anxiety disorders into its own grouping

1. Introduction

The essential feature is recurrent obsessional thoughts or compulsive acts. Obsessional thoughts (ideas, images, or impulses that enter the mind again and again in a stereotyped form) are almost invariably distressing and the person often tries, unsuccessfully, to resist them; they are recognised as the person's own thoughts, even though involuntary and often repugnant. Compulsive acts or rituals (stereotyped behaviours repeated again and again) are not inherently enjoyable nor useful; they function to prevent some objectively unlikely event, often involving harm to or by the person, and are recognised as pointless/ineffectual with repeated attempts to resist. Anxiety is almost always present and worsens if the acts are resisted. ICD-10

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

DomainDetail
GeneticSubstantial heritability; higher in first-degree relatives; early-onset OCD more familial; link with tic disorders.
NeurobiologicalOver-activity of the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit (a frontal–basal-ganglia–thalamus loop); strong serotonin involvement (SSRI responsiveness). PANDAS (abrupt-onset OCD/tics in children after streptococcal infection) in a small subset.

Psychological theories

  • Behavioural (Mowrer two-factor): obsessions raise anxiety; compulsions are negatively reinforced by the relief they bring — the basis of exposure and response prevention (ERP).
  • Cognitive (Salkovskis; Rachman): intrusive thoughts are normal, but people with OCD appraise them as signifying an inflated sense of responsibility for harm, with thought–action fusion (believing that thinking something is morally equivalent to doing it, or makes it more likely); neutralising prevents disconfirmation.
  • Psychodynamic: defence mechanisms of isolation, undoing, reaction formation; regression to the anal stage; conflict over aggressive/sexual impulses.

3. Epidemiology

  • Lifetime ~2–3%; roughly equal in men and women (earlier onset in males); onset usually adolescence/early adulthood; chronic waxing-and-waning course with long treatment delay. Kaplan & Sadock

4. General diagnostic guidelines (ICD-10) & requirements (ICD-11)

ICD-10

Obsessional symptoms and/or compulsive acts must be present most days for at least 2 successive weeks and be a source of distress or interference with activities. The obsessional symptoms should have all of: (a) recognised as the individual's own thoughts/impulses; (b) at least one is unsuccessfully resisted (even if others are no longer resisted); (c) the thought of carrying out the act is not itself pleasurable (relief of anxiety/tension is not "pleasure"); (d) the thoughts/images/impulses are unpleasantly repetitive.

ICD-11 (6B20)

Persistent obsessions and/or compulsions; obsessions are repetitive, intrusive, unwanted thoughts/images/urges; compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed in response to an obsession, according to rigid rules, or to achieve "completeness". Symptoms are time-consuming (e.g. >1 hour/day) or cause significant distress/impairment. Specify insight (fair/good vs poor/absent).

Detailed subtypes — each with criteria and differential diagnosis

F42.0 — Predominantly obsessional thoughts or ruminations

Concept: obsessions dominate — intrusive thoughts, images, or impulses, or ruminations (endless, inconclusive internal debate, often over trivial alternatives, with inability to reach a decision). There are no, or minimal, overt compulsive acts (though covert mental rituals may occur). Content is often distressing: fears of violence, harming others, obscenity, or repeated pointless questioning (e.g. religious/philosophical).

Differential diagnosis (F42.0)
  • Depressive rumination — brooding on realistic negative themes, mood-congruent, without the ego-dystonic, resisted quality; if a depressive episode is present it may take precedence.
  • Generalized anxiety (worry) — worries are about real-life concerns and are experienced as more realistic, not senseless/intrusive.
  • Psychotic disorder — the person with OCD retains insight and resists the thoughts (ego-dystonic), unlike a delusion held with conviction.

F42.1 — Predominantly compulsive acts [obsessional rituals]

Concept: compulsions/rituals dominate. Most concern cleaning (especially handwashing), repeated checking to guard against a potentially dangerous situation, or orderliness/tidiness. The behaviour is an attempt to ward off an objectively unlikely danger (often harm to or caused by the person), is recognised as pointless, and is repeatedly resisted; underlying the behaviour is usually fear and a need to complete the ritual to reduce it. Rituals may occupy many hours daily and be associated with marked indecisiveness and slowness.

Differential diagnosis (F42.1)
  • Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (anankastic, F60.5) — lifelong ego-syntonic traits of perfectionism/rigidity, without true intrusive obsessions or distressing rituals.
  • Schizophrenia — stereotyped behaviour may occur but with other psychotic features and loss of insight.
  • Organic/tic disorders — repetitive movements of Tourette syndrome or basal-ganglia disease; can co-occur with OCD.

F42.2 — Mixed obsessional thoughts and acts

Concept: both obsessional thoughts and compulsive acts are present and roughly equally prominent — the commonest presentation. Most obsessional patients have elements of both.

Differential diagnosis (F42 general)
  • Depressive disorder — OCD and depression frequently coexist. ICD-10 rule: in an acute episode, give precedence to whichever symptoms developed first; when both are present and neither predominates, it is usually best to regard the depression as primary. In chronic disorders, precedence goes to the symptoms that most often persist when the other has subsided.
  • Anxiety disorders — generalized anxiety/phobia lack the intrusive, resisted, senseless obsessions and rituals.
  • Body dysmorphic disorder / hypochondriasis — now grouped with OCD (obsessional preoccupation with appearance/health).

F42.8 other obsessive-compulsive disorders; F42.9 obsessive-compulsive disorder, unspecified.

Includes: anankastic neurosis; obsessive-compulsive neurosis.
Excludes: obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (F60.5); obsessive-compulsive symptoms occurring in depression (code the depression if primary) or schizophrenia.
ICD-11 OCD-related grouping (6B2x): 6B20 OCD; 6B21 body dysmorphic disorder (preoccupation with a perceived appearance defect); 6B22 olfactory reference disorder (preoccupation that one emits a foul odour); 6B23 hypochondriasis (health anxiety) — moved here from the somatoform disorders; 6B24 hoarding disorder; 6B25 body-focused repetitive behaviour disorders (trichotillomania = hair-pulling; excoriation = skin-picking). Each specified by insight.

Comorbidities, memory aids, and treatment (F42)

Comorbidities

  • Depression (very common), other anxiety disorders, tic disorders/Tourette (esp. early-onset), body dysmorphic disorder, eating disorders, OC personality disorder.

Mnemonics

Types — "WCCOH" (Washing, Checking, Counting, Ordering, Hoarding). Cognitive core — inflated responsibility + thought–action fusion. Treatment reflex — ERP + SSRI (high dose, long duration).

  • Psychological (first-line): exposure and response prevention (ERP); cognitive therapy targeting responsibility beliefs.
  • Pharmacological: SSRIs (higher doses, longer duration than in depression); clomipramine; antipsychotic augmentation in resistant cases; neurosurgery/DBS for severe refractory OCD. Kaplan & Sadock

F43 · Reaction to Severe Stress, and Adjustment Disorders → ICD-11 6B4x

ICD-10 F43 (.0 acute stress reaction, .1 PTSD, .2 adjustment disorders [.20–.28], .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B40 PTSD; 6B41 Complex PTSD (new); 6B42 Prolonged grief disorder (new); 6B43 Adjustment disorder; acute stress reaction → QE84 (normal reaction)
Core Disorders arising as a direct consequence of an identifiable stressor/trauma
Key point Uniquely, a stressor is a necessary causal factor

1. Introduction

These disorders are identified not only on symptoms but on the presence of one of two causal influences: an exceptionally stressful/traumatic life event (producing an acute stress reaction or PTSD), or a significant life change leading to continued unpleasant circumstances (producing an adjustment disorder). The stressor is a necessary, though not always sufficient, cause — the disorder would not have arisen without it. Individual vulnerability and coping capacity influence occurrence and form. ICD-10

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

DomainDetail
The stressorNecessary cause; severity, duration, proximity, and interpersonal nature (esp. deliberate human cruelty) raise risk; prolonged/repeated trauma (childhood abuse, captivity) predisposes to Complex PTSD.
Vulnerability / protectivePrior trauma, pre-existing mental disorder, female sex, low social support, and neuroticism raise risk; strong social support is protective.
NeurobiologicalDysregulated HPA-axis and noradrenergic systems; over-active amygdala with reduced prefrontal control and hippocampal changes; failure of fear extinction.

Psychological theories

  • Conditioning (two-factor) model: trauma cues become conditioned fear stimuli; avoidance is negatively reinforced and blocks extinction.
  • Emotional processing theory (Foa & Kozak): PTSD reflects an inadequately processed fear network; recovery needs activation and modification — basis of prolonged exposure.
  • Ehlers & Clark cognitive model: PTSD persists when trauma is processed so as to create a sense of current threat, via negative appraisals and a poorly elaborated, disjointed trauma memory.
  • Dual-representation theory: verbally- vs situationally-accessible memory systems explain intrusive sensory flashbacks.
  • For grief: attachment theory and the dual-process model of grieving (oscillation between loss- and restoration-oriented coping).

3. Epidemiology

  • Trauma exposure is common; only a minority develop PTSD. Lifetime PTSD ~6–8%, higher after interpersonal violence; about twice as common in women. Adjustment disorders are among the most frequent diagnoses in liaison and primary-care settings. Other evidence

Detailed subtypes — each with criteria and differential diagnosis

F43.0 — Acute stress reaction → ICD-11 QE84 (reframed as a normal reaction)

Concept: a transient disorder of significant severity developing in response to exceptional physical or mental stress in an otherwise unaffected person, subsiding within hours to days.

Clinical detail: typically an initial state of "daze" with narrowing of the field of consciousness and attention, inability to comprehend stimuli, and disorientation; this may be followed either by withdrawal (up to dissociative stupor) or by agitation and overactivity (flight reaction, panic). Autonomic signs of panic (tachycardia, sweating, flushing) are common. Symptoms usually appear within minutes and disappear rapidly (within hours if the stressor can be removed; within 2–3 days at most if it continues). Partial or complete amnesia for the episode may occur.

ICD-10 criteria

There must be an immediate and clear temporal connection between the impact of an exceptional stressor and the onset of symptoms (usually within a few minutes); symptoms show a mixed and usually changing picture; and, in addition to the initial daze, depression, anxiety, anger, despair, overactivity, and withdrawal may all be seen, but no one type predominates for long. Symptoms begin to diminish after 24–48 hours and are usually minimal after about 3 days.

ICD-11

Acute stress reaction is NOT a mental disorder in ICD-11 — it is regarded as a normal reaction to severe stress and listed among "factors influencing health status/contact with health services" (QE84). (An important examinable change.)

Includes: acute crisis reaction; combat fatigue; crisis state; psychic shock.
Excludes: symptoms persisting or arising later → PTSD (F43.1); reactions to a non-catastrophic life change → adjustment disorder (F43.2).
Differential diagnosis (F43.0)
  • PTSD — if symptoms persist beyond the acute period or arise after a latency; re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal dominate.
  • Adjustment disorder — slower onset, less severe stressor, longer course.
  • Panic disorder — attacks not tied to an exceptional external stressor.
  • Dissociative disorders — if dissociative stupor/amnesia dominates and persists.

F43.1 — Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) → ICD-11 6B40 (+ 6B41 Complex PTSD)

Concept: a delayed and/or protracted response to a stressful event or situation of an exceptionally threatening or catastrophic nature that would cause pervasive distress in almost anyone (e.g. disaster, combat, serious accident, assault, rape, torture).

Clinical detail (the classic clusters): (1) re-experiencing (repeated reliving of the trauma in intrusive memories ("flashbacks") or dreams), against a background of a sense of "numbness" and emotional blunting; (2) avoidance (detachment from others, unresponsiveness, avoidance of activities/situations reminiscent of the trauma); and (3) a state of autonomic hyperarousal (hypervigilance, enhanced startle, insomnia), often with anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation. Onset follows the trauma after a latency of a few weeks to months (rarely >6 months).

ICD-10 criteria

  1. Exposure to a stressful event or situation of exceptionally threatening or catastrophic nature.
  2. Persistent remembering or "reliving" of the stressor in intrusive flashbacks, vivid memories, recurring dreams, or distress on exposure to reminders.
  3. Avoidance of circumstances resembling or associated with the stressor.
  4. Either (a) inability to recall some important aspects of the period of exposure, or (b) persistent symptoms of increased psychological sensitivity and arousal (difficulty sleeping, irritability, poor concentration, hypervigilance, exaggerated startle).
  5. Criteria 2–4 arise within 6 months of the stressful event (or the end of a period of stress).

ICD-11 (6B40)

Tightened, more specific requirements — all three core elements after exposure to an extremely threatening/horrific event: (1) re-experiencing the trauma in the present (vivid intrusive memories, flashbacks, or nightmares, typically with fear/horror); (2) deliberate avoidance of reminders; and (3) a persistent sense of current threat (hypervigilance, exaggerated startle). Symptoms persist for ≥ several weeks and cause significant impairment.

Includes: traumatic neurosis.
Excludes: acute stress reaction (F43.0); adjustment disorder (F43.2); enduring personality change after catastrophic experience (F62.0) — the ICD-10 forerunner of Complex PTSD.
Differential diagnosis (F43.1)
  • Acute stress reaction — transient, resolves within days; PTSD persists and re-experiences the trauma.
  • Adjustment disorder — the stressor is not of catastrophic/exceptional severity, and re-experiencing is not the core feature.
  • Depressive disorder — may be comorbid; distinguished by the trauma-specific re-experiencing and avoidance.
  • Specific/other phobias, panic disorder, OCD — anxiety not organised around reliving a specific trauma.
  • Organic causes / head injury / substance effects — especially where there is amnesia or arousal changes.
Complex PTSD → ICD-11 6B41 (NEW — no ICD-10 equivalent, but foreshadowed by F62.0): arises after prolonged or repeated trauma from which escape is difficult (childhood abuse, domestic violence, torture, captivity). It requires all three PTSD clusters PLUS three "disturbances in self-organisation" (DSO): (i) affect dysregulation; (ii) negative self-concept (persistent worthlessness, shame, guilt); and (iii) disturbances in relationships (difficulty feeling close to others).
Prolonged grief disorder → ICD-11 6B42 (NEW): a persistent, pervasive grief response — longing/yearning for or preoccupation with the deceased, with intense emotional pain — persisting abnormally long after the loss (at least 6 months and well beyond the expected social/cultural norm), causing significant impairment.

F43.2 — Adjustment disorders → ICD-11 6B43

Concept: states of subjective distress and emotional disturbance, usually interfering with social functioning, arising in the period of adaptation to a significant life change or a stressful life event (bereavement, separation, migration, serious illness, becoming a parent, failure to achieve a goal). Individual predisposition/vulnerability plays a greater role than in the other F43 disorders, but the condition would not have arisen without the stressor.

Clinical detail: manifestations include depressed mood, anxiety, worry, a feeling of inability to cope/plan ahead, and some degree of disability in daily routine; in children, regressive phenomena (bed-wetting, baby-talk, thumb-sucking); in adolescents, conduct disturbance.

ICD-10 criteria

Onset usually within 1 month of the occurrence of the identifiable stressor; symptoms and behaviour are of a type found in mood (F30–F39), neurotic (F40–F48), or conduct disorders, but the criteria for an individual disorder are not met. Duration of symptoms does not usually exceed 6 months (except the prolonged depressive reaction). The subtype is chosen by the predominant symptom pattern:

  • F43.20 Brief depressive reaction: a transient mild depressive state lasting ≤ 1 month.
  • F43.21 Prolonged depressive reaction: a mild depressive state in response to prolonged exposure to a stressful situation, lasting up to 2 years.
  • F43.22 Mixed anxiety and depressive reaction: both anxiety and depressive symptoms prominent.
  • F43.23 With predominant disturbance of other emotions: anxiety, worry, tension, and anger.
  • F43.24 With predominant disturbance of conduct: e.g. aggressive or antisocial behaviour (esp. adolescents).
  • F43.25 With mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct.
  • F43.28 With other specified predominant symptoms.

ICD-11 (6B43)

Redefined around a preoccupation with the stressor or its consequences (excessive worry, recurrent distressing thoughts about the stressor, constant rumination about its implications) together with a failure to adapt (symptoms interfere with functioning). Onset within a month of the stressor; symptoms typically resolve within about 6 months of the stressor (or its consequences) ending.

Includes: culture shock; grief reaction; hospitalism in children.
Excludes: separation anxiety disorder of childhood (F93.0); if criteria are met for a specific mood/anxiety disorder, code that; bereavement that is a normal grief reaction; prolonged grief disorder (ICD-11 6B42).
Differential diagnosis (F43.2)
  • Depressive episode / anxiety disorder — diagnosed instead if the full criteria are met (symptoms exceed those of the adjustment subtype).
  • PTSD / acute stress reaction — if the stressor is of exceptional/catastrophic severity and re-experiencing dominates.
  • Normal grief / normal adaptation — distinguished by severity, duration, and degree of functional impairment.
  • Prolonged grief disorder — grief that is unusually intense and persists beyond expected norms.

F43.8 other reactions to severe stress; F43.9 reaction to severe stress, unspecified.

Comorbidities, memory aids, and treatment (F43)

Comorbidities

  • Depression, other anxiety disorders, substance misuse (esp. alcohol), and (for PTSD) chronic pain, dissociation, and increased suicide risk.

Mnemonics

PTSD — "RAH": Re-experiencing, Avoidance, Hyperarousal (current threat). Complex PTSD = PTSD + "DSO" (affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relationship difficulty). Timeline: acute stress reaction = minutes–days; PTSD = within ~6 months, lasting weeks+; adjustment disorder = within 1 month, resolves by ~6 months.

  • Acute stress reaction: reassurance, safety, practical support; avoid single-session debriefing.
  • PTSD: trauma-focused CBT / prolonged exposure and EMDR (eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing) first-line; SSRIs/SNRIs; prazosin for nightmares. Complex PTSD needs a phased approach.
  • Adjustment / grief: supportive and problem-focused psychotherapy, brief CBT; grief-focused therapy for prolonged grief. Kaplan & Sadock

F44 · Dissociative (Conversion) Disorders → ICD-11 6B6x

ICD-10 F44 (.0 amnesia, .1 fugue, .2 stupor, .3 trance/possession, .4 motor, .5 convulsions, .6 anaesthesia/sensory loss, .7 mixed, .8 [.80 Ganser, .81 multiple personality])
ICD-11 6B60 dissociative neurological symptom disorder; 6B61 dissociative amnesia (± fugue); 6B63 trance/possession; 6B64/6B65 DID/partial DID; 6B66 depersonalization-derealization
Core Partial/complete loss of the normal integration of memories, identity, sensation, and control of movement
Key point Presumed psychogenic; no organic cause; close temporal link to trauma/conflict

1. Introduction

These disorders share a partial or complete loss of the normal integration between memories of the past, awareness of identity and immediate sensations, and control of bodily movements. ICD-10 uses "conversion" for the older concept that unpleasant affect generated by conflicts is transformed into symptoms. Onset and remission are often sudden and closely associated in time with traumatic events, insoluble problems, or disturbed relationships; the symptoms often represent the person's concept of how a physical illness would manifest. ICD-10

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

DomainDetail
Trauma / stressStrong link with acute stress, unresolved conflict, and especially childhood trauma/abuse (dissociative amnesia and DID).
NeurobiologicalAltered connectivity between limbic (emotion) and sensorimotor/prefrontal regions; imaging supports that the symptoms are genuinely involuntary (not feigned).

Psychological theories

  • Psychodynamic: dissociation/conversion as a defence — intolerable memories/impulses are split off. Classic concepts: primary gain (the symptom keeps the conflict out of awareness, reducing anxiety), secondary gain (external advantages — care, avoiding responsibility), and la belle indifférence (surprising lack of concern about a serious apparent disability).
  • Learning/behavioural: the "sick role" and symptoms are reinforced by attention and avoidance of stressors.
  • Autohypnotic / trauma model: a capacity for self-hypnosis; in DID, dissociation is theorised to protect the child from overwhelming abuse.

3. Epidemiology & comorbidities

  • More common in women and in younger people; conversion symptoms common in neurology/emergency settings. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Comorbidities: depression, PTSD/trauma disorders, other anxiety disorders, personality disorders (esp. borderline), and (for conversion) comorbid neurological disease.

4. General diagnostic guidelines (ICD-10) & requirements (ICD-11)

ICD-10

For a definite diagnosis: (a) the clinical features specified for the individual subtype; (b) no evidence of a physical disorder that could explain the symptoms; and (c) evidence of psychological causation, in a clear temporal association with stressful events/problems or disturbed relationships (even if denied).

ICD-11

Involuntary disruption/discontinuity in the normal integration of identity, sensations, perceptions, affect, thoughts, memory, control of movement, or behaviour; not better explained by another disorder, substance, or medical condition, and causing significant distress/impairment. (ICD-11 drops "conversion" and codes the neurological forms as dissociative neurological symptom disorder, 6B60.)

Detailed subtypes — each with criteria and differential diagnosis

F44.0 — Dissociative amnesia → ICD-11 6B61

Concept: loss of memory, usually of important recent events of a traumatic or stressful nature (accidents, unexpected bereavements), too extensive to be ordinary forgetfulness and not due to an organic disorder. Amnesia is usually partial and selective, centred on traumatic material, and variable from day to day; complete/generalised amnesia is rare.

ICD-10 criteria

(a) Amnesia, partial or complete, for recent events of a traumatic/stressful nature; (b) absence of organic brain disorders, intoxication, or excessive fatigue.

Differential diagnosis (F44.0)
  • Organic amnesic states — usually other neurological signs, clouding of consciousness, disorientation, and fluctuating awareness; loss of very recent memory is more typical of organic states regardless of any traumatic events.
  • Alcohol/drug "blackouts" — closely tied to the time of intoxication; the lost memories can never be regained (unlike dissociative amnesia).
  • Post-ictal amnesia (epilepsy); ordinary forgetfulness/fatigue; malingering (deliberate simulation — may be very hard to distinguish).

F44.1 — Dissociative fugue → ICD-11 6B61 (with dissociative fugue)

Concept: all the features of dissociative amnesia PLUS an apparently purposeful journey away from home or the usual place of work/activities, during which self-care is largely maintained. There may be assumption of a new identity (for days, occasionally longer), and to observers the person may appear entirely normal. Amnesia for the fugue period follows recovery.

ICD-10 criteria

(a) The features of dissociative amnesia; (b) a purposeful journey beyond the usual everyday range (differentiation of travel between areas may need local knowledge); (c) maintenance of basic self-care and simple social interaction with strangers.

Differential diagnosis (F44.1)
  • Post-ictal fugue (esp. temporal-lobe epilepsy) — distinguished by a history of epilepsy, lack of stressful events, and less purposeful, more fragmented activity/travel.
  • Conscious simulation (malingering) of a fugue — may be very difficult to distinguish.
  • Organic states / wandering in dementia.

F44.2 — Dissociative stupor

Concept: profound diminution or absence of voluntary movement, speech, and normal responsiveness to light, noise, and touch, but with muscle tone, posture, breathing, and (sometimes) eye movements showing the person is neither asleep nor unconscious. No physical or other psychiatric disorder explains it, and there is positive evidence of psychogenic causation (recent stress/problems).

ICD-10 criteria

(a) Stupor as above; (b) absence of a physical or other psychiatric disorder that might explain it; (c) evidence of recent stressful events or current problems.

Differential diagnosis (F44.2)
  • Catatonic stupor (schizophrenia) — often preceded by symptoms/behaviour suggestive of schizophrenia.
  • Depressive or manic stupor — usually develop comparatively slowly; a history from an informant is decisive.
  • Organic stupor — clouded consciousness, neurological signs, metabolic/structural causes.

F44.3 — Trance and possession disorders → ICD-11 6B63

Concept: disorders with a temporary loss of both the sense of personal identity and full awareness of the surroundings; in some the person acts as if taken over by another personality, spirit, deity, or "force". Only trance states that are involuntary or unwanted, intruding into ordinary activities by occurring outside accepted collective cultural or religious situations, are classified here.

Excludes: trance states in religious/culturally accepted ceremonies; states associated with psychotic disorders, temporal-lobe epilepsy, or head injury.
Differential diagnosis (F44.3)
  • Culturally sanctioned trance/possession — not a disorder if voluntary and within accepted practice.
  • Temporal-lobe epilepsy; psychotic disorders; organic states.

F44.4–F44.7 — Dissociative disorders of movement and sensation (conversion) → ICD-11 6B60

In these, there is a loss of, or interference with, movements or sensations (usually cutaneous), so the person presents as having a physical disorder, though none is found; symptoms often represent the person's concept of physical illness and may be inconsistent with anatomy/physiology. Degree of disability may vary with circumstances and the presence of observers.

  • F44.4 Dissociative motor disorders: loss of ability to move all or part of a limb(s); paralysis may be partial (weak/slow) or complete; ataxia, tremor, or inability to stand unaided (astasia-abasia — inability to stand or walk despite normal leg movements when lying/sitting).
  • F44.5 Dissociative convulsions ("pseudoseizures" / non-epileptic attacks): movements that mimic epileptic seizures, but tongue-biting, serious bruising from falling, and incontinence are rare, and there is no loss of consciousness (rather a state of stupor or trance).
  • F44.6 Dissociative anaesthesia and sensory loss: anaesthetic skin areas often with boundaries reflecting the person's ideas about bodily function rather than medical knowledge (e.g. "glove-and-stocking" loss); differential loss between sensory modalities not attributable to a neurological lesion; may include dissociative visual loss (rarely total; often "tunnel vision"), deafness, or anosmia.
  • F44.7 Mixed dissociative (conversion) disorders: combinations of the above.
Differential diagnosis (F44.4–.7)
  • Early progressive neurological disease — especially multiple sclerosis and systemic lupus erythematosus, which may be confused with conversion; long periods of assessment/observation may be needed before diagnostic probabilities become clear.
  • Epilepsy (vs dissociative convulsions) — EEG, prolactin, and the features above help distinguish.
  • Malingering / factitious disorder — symptoms deliberately produced; hard to prove.
  • Other psychiatric disorders presenting with somatic symptoms.

F44.8 — Other dissociative (conversion) disorders (named syndromes)

  • F44.80 Ganser's syndrome: the characteristic feature is the giving of approximate answers ("vorbeireden") (near-miss but clearly wrong answers to simple questions, e.g. "2+2=5"; "a horse has 5 legs"), usually accompanied by other dissociative symptoms, often in circumstances suggesting a psychogenic origin (frequently forensic settings).
  • F44.81 Multiple personality disorder → ICD-11 6B64 (DID) / 6B65 (partial DID): the apparent existence of two or more distinct personalities/identity states within one individual, with only one evident at a time; each has its own memories, preferences, and behaviour, and each may take full control in turn; there is inability to recall important personal information too extensive for ordinary forgetfulness. Strongly associated with severe childhood trauma.
  • F44.82 transient dissociative disorders occurring in childhood/adolescence; F44.88 other specified.
Differential diagnosis (F44.8)
  • Ganser vs organic confusion / malingering / psychosis — the approximate answers and dissociative context are characteristic; exclude organic states.
  • DID vs schizophrenia — DID involves distinct identities and amnesia, not first-rank psychotic symptoms; vs borderline personality disorder and vs malingering (esp. forensic contexts).

Memory aid & treatment (F44)

Mnemonics

Dissociation of MEMORY/IDENTITY (amnesia, fugue, DID) vs conversion of MOVEMENT/SENSATION (paralysis, pseudoseizures, sensory loss). Conversion clues — la belle indifférence, non-anatomical deficits, secondary gain. Ganser = approximate answers.

  • Psychological (mainstay): safety and rapport, psychoeducation and reassurance, addressing the underlying stressor/conflict, CBT; physiotherapy and a rehabilitative "expectation of recovery" for conversion; phased trauma-focused therapy for DID; hypnosis/abreaction historically for amnesia.
  • Pharmacological: no specific drug; treat comorbid depression/anxiety/PTSD; avoid unnecessary investigations and reinforcement of the sick role. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Prognosis: acute states with a clear stressor usually recover well; chronic states and DID are more persistent.

F45 · Somatoform Disorders → ICD-11 6C20 (Bodily Distress Disorder)

ICD-10 F45 (.0 somatization, .1 undifferentiated, .2 hypochondriacal, .3 autonomic dysfunction [.30–.38], .4 persistent pain, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6C20 bodily distress disorder (mild/moderate/severe); hypochondriasis → 6B23 (OC-related grouping)
Core Repeated presentation of physical symptoms with persistent requests for investigation despite negative findings and reassurance
Key change ICD-11 collapses most somatoform categories into one bodily distress disorder and re-homes hypochondriasis with OCD

1. Introduction

The main feature is repeated presentation of physical symptoms with persistent requests for medical investigations, in spite of repeated negative findings and reassurance that the symptoms have no physical basis. If any physical disorder is present, it does not explain the nature/extent of the symptoms or the distress and preoccupation. The person usually resists attempts to discuss psychological causation, and there is often attention-seeking behaviour. ICD-10

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

Psychological theories

  • Cognitive-perceptual model: heightened somatosensory amplification (noticing, focusing on, and being alarmed by normal/benign bodily sensations) and catastrophic misinterpretation as serious disease.
  • Behavioural: symptom-focus, checking, and reassurance-seeking are reinforced by anxiety reduction and attention (the "sick role"); avoidance prevents disconfirmation.
  • Psychodynamic: physical symptoms as expression of unacceptable emotion (alexithymia — difficulty identifying/describing feelings) and as a means of gaining care.
  • Learning/family: modelling of illness behaviour, early illness experience, and reinforcement within family/health systems.

3. Epidemiology & comorbidities

  • Common in primary/general medical care; more frequent in women; somatization usually begins before age 30. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Comorbidities: depression and anxiety disorders (very high), other functional somatic syndromes, personality disorders; strong association with early adversity.

Detailed subtypes — each with criteria and differential diagnosis

F45.0 — Somatization disorder (Briquet's syndrome) → ICD-11 6C20

Concept: multiple, recurrent, and frequently changing physical symptoms present for at least 2 years, referable to any part of the body/any system (commonly GI sensations, abnormal skin sensations, sexual/menstrual complaints), for which no adequate physical explanation is found; a long, complicated history of contact with medical services and many negative investigations or fruitless operations; persistent refusal to accept reassurance; and some degree of social/family impairment.

ICD-10 criteria

  • (a) At least 2 years of multiple and variable physical symptoms with no adequate physical explanation;
  • (b) persistent refusal to accept the advice/reassurance of several doctors that there is no physical explanation;
  • (c) some degree of impairment of social and family functioning attributable to the symptoms and resulting behaviour.
Differential diagnosis (F45.0)
  • Physical disorders — patients with long-standing somatization have the same chance as anyone of developing an independent physical disorder; consider further investigation if there is a shift in the emphasis or stability of complaints suggesting physical disease.
  • Depressive and anxiety disorders — commonly accompany somatization; treat/diagnose separately if prominent (esp. if depression is moderate/severe).
  • Hypochondriacal disorder — focus on a disease and its consequences rather than the individual symptoms.
  • Delusional disorder (e.g. somatic type) — fixed false belief held with delusional conviction.
  • Factitious disorder / malingering — deliberate production of symptoms.

F45.1 — Undifferentiated somatoform disorder → ICD-11 6C20

Concept: multiple, varying, and persistent physical complaints for which the complete and typical clinical picture of somatization disorder is not fulfilled (e.g. fewer symptoms, less persistent, shorter duration, or less social/family disruption). The commonest somatoform presentation in practice.

Differential diagnosis (F45.1)

As for the full syndrome of somatization disorder (F45.0) — exclude physical disease, depressive/anxiety disorders, hypochondriacal disorder, and delusional/factitious presentations.

F45.2 — Hypochondriacal disorder → ICD-11 6B23 (hypochondriasis, in the OC-related grouping); BDD → 6B21

Concept: a persistent preoccupation with the possibility of having one or more serious and progressive physical disorders. The person manifests persistent somatic complaints or persistent preoccupation with physical appearance; normal/commonplace sensations and appearances are interpreted as abnormal and distressing; attention is usually focused on only one or two organs/systems. Includes body dysmorphic disorder (non-delusional preoccupation with imagined/minor appearance defect) in ICD-10; ICD-11 codes BDD separately (6B21). Marked depression and anxiety are common.

ICD-10 criteria

  • (a) Persistent belief in the presence of at least one serious physical illness underlying the symptom(s), or a persistent preoccupation with a presumed deformity/disfigurement (BDD);
  • (b) persistent refusal to accept the advice/reassurance of several doctors that there is no physical illness/abnormality.
Excludes: delusional disorders (hypochondriacal delusions, F22); dysmorphic delusions.
Differential diagnosis (F45.2)
  • Somatization disorder — the emphasis in hypochondriasis is on the disease and its future consequences (and usually only one or two disorders, named consistently), rather than the multiple, changing individual symptoms of somatization.
  • Depressive disorders — if depressive symptoms are prominent and precede the hypochondriacal ideas, code the depression.
  • Delusional (hypochondriacal) disorder — the belief in hypochondriasis is usually susceptible, to some degree and in the short term, to argument/reassurance/further examination, unlike a fixed delusion.
  • Panic disorder and anxiety disorders — health fears occurring only during panic.

F45.3 — Somatoform autonomic dysfunction → ICD-11 6C20

Concept: symptoms presented by the person as if due to a physical disorder of a system/organ largely under autonomic innervation and control (cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, respiratory, genitourinary). Two symptom types: (1) objective autonomic arousal signs (palpitations, sweating, flushing, tremor); and (2) idiosyncratic, subjective, non-specific symptoms referred by the person to a specific organ/system.

Sub-codes by system: .30 heart and cardiovascular system (cardiac neurosis, Da Costa's syndrome, neurocirculatory asthenia); .31 upper GI tract (gastric neurosis, psychogenic aerophagy, hiccough, dyspepsia, pylorospasm); .32 lower GI tract (psychogenic irritable bowel syndrome, flatulence, diarrhoea); .33 respiratory system (psychogenic hyperventilation, cough); .34 genitourinary system (psychogenic frequency/dysuria); .38 other organ or system.

ICD-10 criteria

  • (a) Symptoms of autonomic arousal attributed by the person to a physical disorder of one/more of the listed systems;
  • (b) additional subjective symptoms referred to a specific organ/system;
  • (c) preoccupation with, and distress about, the possibility of a serious (but often unspecified) disorder of that system, unresponsive to repeated reassurance;
  • (d) no evidence of a significant structural/functional disturbance of that system.
Differential diagnosis (F45.3)
  • Generalized anxiety disorder — if the psychological components of anxiety predominate and are not attributed to a specific organ.
  • Somatization disorder — autonomic symptoms occur but are not the sole/predominant feature.
  • Genuine physical disease of the organ — must be excluded.

F45.4 — Persistent somatoform pain disorder → ICD-11 6C20

Concept: the predominant complaint is persistent, severe, and distressing pain that cannot be explained fully by a physiological process or a physical disorder, and occurs in association with emotional conflict or psychosocial problems that are sufficient to be judged the main causal influences. The result is usually a marked increase in support and attention (personal or medical).

Excludes: pain presumed of psychogenic origin occurring during depressive disorder or schizophrenia; tension headache (F54); back pain of specified organic cause.
Differential diagnosis (F45.4)
  • Organic pain — must be excluded / reassessed if the picture changes.
  • Depressive disorder — if pain occurs in the context of a depressive episode, code the depression.
  • Somatization disorder — pain is one of many symptoms rather than the single predominant complaint.

F45.8 other somatoform disorders (e.g. psychogenic dysmenorrhoea, dysphagia including "globus hystericus", psychogenic pruritus, psychogenic torticollis, teeth-grinding); F45.9 somatoform disorder, unspecified.

ICD-11 (6C20 Bodily distress disorder): bodily symptoms that are distressing and to which excessive attention is directed (with repeated health-care contact), persisting for several months; rated mild/moderate/severe. It replaces most F45 categories by focusing on the distress and preoccupation rather than on whether the symptoms are "medically unexplained".

Memory aid (F45)

Mnemonics

Somatization = many symptoms, many systems, many years. Hypochondriasis = fear of a disease (now grouped with OCD). Pain disorder = one persistent unexplained pain. Cognitive core — somatosensory amplification + catastrophic misinterpretation. ICD-11 → one Bodily Distress Disorder.

  • Treatment: a single consistent clinician, regular fixed (not symptom-driven) appointments, reattribution and CBT, treatment of comorbid depression/anxiety, and avoidance of unnecessary investigations/iatrogenic harm. Kaplan & Sadock

F48 · Other Neurotic Disorders → ICD-11 (redistributed)

ICD-10 F48 (.0 neurasthenia, .1 depersonalization-derealization, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B66 depersonalization-derealization disorder (dissociative grouping); neurasthenia largely deprecated
Core Residual neurotic conditions not classified elsewhere

Detailed subtypes — each with criteria and differential diagnosis

F48.0 — Neurasthenia

Concept: two overlapping types — (1) increased fatigue after mental effort, often with reduced occupational performance and difficulty concentrating; or (2) bodily/physical weakness and exhaustion after minimal effort, with muscular aches and inability to relax. Both share unpleasant physical sensations (dizziness, tension headaches), worry about declining mental/physical wellbeing, irritability, anhedonia, and varying degrees of depression and anxiety, plus sleep disturbance.

ICD-10 criteria

  • (a) Either persistent, distressing complaints of increased fatigue after mental effort, or persistent complaints of bodily weakness/exhaustion after minimal effort;
  • (b) at least two of: muscular aches/pains, dizziness, tension headaches, sleep disturbance, inability to relax, irritability, dyspepsia;
  • (c) any autonomic/depressive symptoms are not sufficiently persistent/severe to fulfil a more specific disorder.
Excludes: post-viral fatigue syndrome (G93.3); psychasthenia; burnout. ICD-11: the category is largely deprecated/omitted (overlaps with fatigue syndromes and bodily distress disorder).
Differential diagnosis (F48.0)
  • Depressive and anxiety disorders — if they meet full criteria, code them instead (they take precedence).
  • Post-viral / chronic fatigue syndrome and organic causes of fatigue (anaemia, hypothyroidism).
  • Bodily distress disorder (ICD-11).

F48.1 — Depersonalization-derealization syndrome → ICD-11 6B66

Concept: depersonalization (a feeling that one's own self, feelings, thoughts, or body are unreal, remote, "not one's own", or as if one is watching oneself from outside) and/or derealization (a feeling that surroundings, objects, or people are unreal, flat, colourless, lifeless, or dream-like), experienced with intact insight that this is a subjective, spontaneous change and not externally imposed.

ICD-10 criteria

Either or both of: (a) depersonalization — the person feels that their feelings and/or experiences are detached, distant, not their own, lost, etc.; (b) derealization — objects, people, and/or surroundings seem unreal, distant, artificial, colourless, or lifeless. The person accepts that this is a subjective and spontaneous change, not imposed by outside forces or other people (i.e. insight is retained — distinguishing it from psychosis).

ICD-11 (6B66)

Persistent or recurrent depersonalization and/or derealization with intact reality testing, causing significant distress/impairment; moved into the dissociative disorders grouping.

Differential diagnosis (F48.1)
  • Psychotic disorders — in depersonalization insight is retained (the person knows it is subjective), unlike delusional misinterpretation.
  • Temporal-lobe epilepsy and other organic causes (migraine, drug intoxication).
  • Anxiety and depressive disorders — depersonalization often occurs as a symptom within these; a separate diagnosis is made only if it is the predominant, persistent feature.

F48.8 other specified neurotic disorders (e.g. Dhat syndrome, occupational neuroses, psychasthenia); F48.9 neurotic disorder, unspecified.

Treatment (F48)

  • Neurasthenia: graded activity, sleep/stress management, CBT, treatment of comorbid depression/anxiety.
  • Depersonalization-derealization: psychoeducation and reassurance, CBT (reducing catastrophic appraisal and self-monitoring), grounding techniques, treatment of comorbid anxiety/depression; SSRIs may help. Kaplan & Sadock

Two golden rules for the block

Rule 1 — ICD-11 dismantled "neurotic disorders": anxiety/fear (6B0x), obsessive-compulsive/related (6B2x, incl. hypochondriasis and BDD), stress-associated (6B4x, incl. new Complex PTSD and Prolonged grief), dissociative (6B6x), and bodily distress (6C2x).

Rule 2 — always exclude organic disease and substances first; insight is characteristically retained (distinguishing these disorders from psychosis), and a stressor is a necessary cause only for the F43 group.
