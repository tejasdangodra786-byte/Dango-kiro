The F40–F49 Block — Overview

The ICD-10 block F40–F49 is titled "Neurotic, stress-related and somatoform disorders". Historically these were the "neuroses" (disorders in which the person experiences distressing symptoms — anxiety, obsessions, physical complaints — but retains contact with reality and insight, in contrast to the "psychoses"). They are grouped together largely for historical reasons and because of their frequent association with psychological causation and with stress. ICD-10

ICD-11 abolished the umbrella term "neurotic" and broke this single block into several separate, more coherent groupings, because the old grouping mixed disorders with quite different mechanisms. The ICD-11 groupings that replace F40–F48 are: Anxiety or fear-related disorders (6B0x); Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders (6B2x); Disorders specifically associated with stress (6B4x); Dissociative disorders (6B6x); and Disorders of bodily distress or bodily experience (6C2x). ICD-11

The single biggest change to state in the exam: ICD-11 dismantled the "neurotic disorders" block and redistributed its members into five distinct, mechanism-based groupings. In particular, OCD moved out of the anxiety disorders into its own "obsessive-compulsive or related disorders" grouping, and the somatoform disorders were largely replaced by a single "bodily distress disorder".

Core Concepts Needed for the Whole Block

TermPlain-language meaning
AnxietyAn unpleasant emotional state of apprehension or dread, with bodily arousal (racing heart, sweating, trembling), in anticipation of a threat — the threat may be unclear (free-floating) or attached to a specific object/situation.
FearThe emotional response to a real or perceived present threat (in contrast to anxiety, which is future-oriented).
PhobiaA persistent, irrational, and excessive fear of a specific object or situation, leading to avoidance, that the person recognises is out of proportion.
Panic attackA discrete episode of sudden, intense fear with prominent physical symptoms (palpitations, chest pain, choking, dizziness) that peaks within minutes.
ObsessionA recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thought, image, or urge that the person recognises as their own and tries to resist (ego-dystonic).
CompulsionA repetitive behaviour or mental act the person feels driven to perform to reduce anxiety or prevent a feared event (e.g. checking, washing).
DissociationA disruption of the normally integrated functions of consciousness, memory, identity, or perception (e.g. amnesia for a trauma, feeling detached from oneself).
ConversionThe historical concept that psychological distress is "converted" into a physical (usually neurological) symptom without an organic cause.
SomatizationThe tendency to experience and express psychological distress as physical symptoms and to seek medical help for them.
Free-floating vs situational anxietyAnxiety that is generalised and not tied to any particular trigger, vs anxiety provoked by a specific situation.
Ego-dystonicExperienced as unwanted, alien, and inconsistent with one's self-image (obsessions are ego-dystonic).

Mnemonic for the physical symptoms of anxiety — "PANICS"

Palpitations · Abdominal distress / Air hunger (breathlessness) · Numbness or tingling · Intense fear (of dying/losing control) · Chest tightness / Choking · Sweating, shaking, dizziness.

F40 · Phobic Anxiety Disorders → ICD-11 6B02 / 6B03 / 6B04

ICD-10 F40 (.0 agoraphobia, .1 social phobias, .2 specific phobias, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B02 agoraphobia; 6B04 social anxiety disorder; 6B03 specific phobia
Core Anxiety evoked only (or predominantly) by specific, well-defined situations/objects that are not currently dangerous
Key point The situation is characteristically avoided or endured with dread

1. Introduction

In the phobic anxiety disorders, anxiety is provoked only, or predominantly, by certain well-defined situations or objects (external to the person) that are not currently dangerous. As a result these situations are characteristically avoided or endured with dread. The anxiety is not relieved by knowing that others do not regard the situation as dangerous. Anticipatory anxiety (dread of the situation in advance) is common. ICD-10

2. Etiology

DomainDetail
Genetic / temperamentalModerate heritability; the temperamental trait of behavioural inhibition (a tendency, seen from early childhood, to react to unfamiliar people/situations with fear and withdrawal) predicts later phobias, especially social anxiety.
NeurobiologicalOver-reactivity of the amygdala (the brain's fear-processing centre) and fear circuitry; involvement of serotonin, noradrenaline, and GABA systems.
Evolutionary ("preparedness")Seligman's preparedness theory (humans are biologically "prepared" to acquire fears of things that threatened ancestral survival — snakes, heights, spiders — far more readily than of modern dangers) explains the non-random content of common phobias.

Psychological theories (highly relevant to phobias)

  • Behavioural / classical conditioning (Watson & Rayner; Mowrer's two-factor theory): a neutral object becomes feared after being paired with a frightening experience (classical conditioning), and the fear is then maintained by avoidance because avoidance is rewarded by immediate relief (operant/negative reinforcement) — the basis of exposure therapy.
  • Vicarious learning & information transmission (Bandura): phobias can be learned by watching others react fearfully, or by being told something is dangerous, without direct experience.
  • Cognitive theory: catastrophic misinterpretation and overestimation of danger/likelihood, plus underestimation of one's ability to cope; in social anxiety, a heightened focus on the self and fear of negative evaluation.
  • Psychodynamic: the phobic object symbolically represents an unconscious conflict, with anxiety displaced onto it (classically illustrated by Freud's "Little Hans").

3. Epidemiology

  • Phobic disorders are among the most common mental disorders. Specific phobias have a lifetime prevalence of roughly 7–12%; social anxiety disorder around 7–12%; agoraphobia around 1–2%. Other evidence
  • More common in women (specific and agoraphobia markedly so; social anxiety more equal).
  • Age of onset: specific phobias in childhood; social anxiety in adolescence; agoraphobia in early adulthood.

4. Clinical features, unique presentations, and comorbidities

Unique identifying features

  • Anxiety is situation-specific and predictable, with prominent avoidance and anticipatory anxiety.
  • Physical symptoms of anxiety (palpitations, sweating, faintness) triggered by exposure to the feared cue.
  • Insight is retained — the person knows the fear is excessive.

Comorbidities

  • Depression (very common, especially with agoraphobia and social anxiety), other anxiety disorders, panic disorder, and alcohol/substance misuse (used to self-medicate social anxiety).

5. Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10

For a definite diagnosis, all of the following: (1) the psychological or autonomic symptoms are primary manifestations of anxiety, not secondary to other symptoms such as delusions or obsessions; (2) the anxiety is restricted to (or predominates in) the presence of the particular phobic object or situation; and (3) the phobic situation is avoided whenever possible.

ICD-11

Marked and excessive fear or anxiety that consistently occurs on exposure or anticipation of the specific object(s)/situation(s), out of proportion to actual danger, leading to avoidance; symptoms persist for several months (typically ≥6 months) and cause significant distress or functional impairment.

Detailed subtypes with their diagnostic criteria

F40.0 — Agoraphobia → ICD-11 6B02

Concept: agoraphobia (literally "fear of the marketplace" — a cluster of fears of situations from which escape might be difficult or embarrassing, or help unavailable, if panic-like symptoms occur): fear of crowds, public places, travelling alone, or travelling away from home. Avoidance can become so severe that the person is housebound.

ICD-10 criteria: (1) psychological/autonomic symptoms are primarily anxiety, not secondary to other symptoms; (2) anxiety is restricted to at least two of — crowds, public places, travelling away from home, travelling alone; (3) avoidance of the phobic situation is a prominent feature. Sub-codes: F40.00 without panic disorder; F40.01 with panic disorder.

ICD-11 (6B02): marked fear/anxiety in multiple situations (crowds, public places, travelling alone or away from home, using public transport) because escape might be difficult or help unavailable, especially if incapacitating or embarrassing symptoms occur; the situations are avoided, entered only with a companion, or endured with intense fear, for ≥ several months. May be specified with or without panic attacks.

F40.1 — Social phobias → ICD-11 6B04 (Social anxiety disorder)

Concept: fear of scrutiny by other people in comparatively small groups, leading to avoidance of social situations; a fear of behaving in a way that will be humiliating or embarrassing. May be discrete (e.g. restricted to eating in public, public speaking) or diffuse (almost all social situations outside the family).

Identifying features: often accompanied by low self-esteem and fear of criticism; complaints may be of blushing, hand tremor, nausea, or urgency of micturition, and the person may be convinced that one of these secondary manifestations is the primary problem; may progress to panic attacks.

ICD-10 criteria: (1) symptoms are primarily anxiety, not secondary to delusions/obsessions; (2) anxiety is restricted to or predominates in particular social situations; (3) avoidance of the phobic situation is prominent.

ICD-11 (6B04): marked and excessive fear/anxiety in one or more social situations in which the person may be scrutinised (social interactions, being observed, performing), with fear of acting in a way (or showing anxiety symptoms) that will be negatively evaluated; situations avoided or endured with intense fear, for ≥ several months, with significant impairment.

F40.2 — Specific (isolated) phobias → ICD-11 6B03

Concept: phobias restricted to highly specific situations such as proximity to particular animals, heights, thunder, darkness, flying, closed spaces, urinating or defecating in public toilets, eating certain foods, dentistry, or the sight of blood or injury.

Recognised clusters (identifying detail): animal type; natural-environment type (heights, storms, water); blood-injection-injury type (uniquely associated with a vasovagal fainting response — the blood pressure drops and the person faints, unlike other phobias where it rises); situational type (lifts, flying, enclosed spaces); and other.

ICD-10 criteria: (1) symptoms are primarily anxiety; (2) anxiety is restricted to the presence of the particular phobic object/situation; (3) the situation is avoided whenever possible.

ICD-11 (6B03): marked, excessive fear/anxiety consistently on exposure/anticipation of a specific object or situation, out of proportion to actual danger, leading to avoidance, for ≥ several months, with significant distress/impairment.

F40.8 other phobic anxiety disorders; F40.9 phobic anxiety disorder, unspecified.

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

The three phobia targets: agora = places/escape; social = people/scrutiny; specific = one object/situation. Maintenance cycle — "AA": Avoidance brings Anxiety relief, which reinforces the phobia (so treatment = stop avoiding = exposure). Blood-injury phobia clue: it makes you faint (vasovagal), unlike other phobias.

7. Treatment

  • Psychological (first-line): exposure therapy (graded, repeated confrontation with the feared situation until anxiety habituates), systematic desensitisation, and CBT (cognitive restructuring of catastrophic beliefs); social skills training for social anxiety; applied tension (a technique of tensing muscles to raise blood pressure and prevent fainting) for blood-injury phobia.
  • Pharmacological: SSRIs/SNRIs for social anxiety and agoraphobia; beta-blockers for performance anxiety (control tremor/palpitations); short-term benzodiazepines only with caution. Kaplan & Sadock

F41 · Other Anxiety Disorders → ICD-11 6B01 / 6B00 / 6A73

ICD-10 F41 (.0 panic disorder, .1 GAD, .2 mixed anxiety-depressive, .3 other mixed, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B01 panic disorder; 6B00 GAD; 6A73 mixed depressive and anxiety (moved to depressive disorders)
Core Anxiety not restricted to any particular situation (free-floating)
Key point Manifestations of anxiety are the major symptoms and are not restricted to a specific environmental cue

1. Introduction

In this group, anxiety is the major symptom but is not restricted to any particular environmental situation (in contrast to the phobias). Depressive and obsessional symptoms, and even some elements of phobic anxiety, may be present but are clearly secondary or less severe. ICD-10

2. Etiology (shared, with psychological theories)

DomainDetail
GeneticModerate heritability; shared genetic vulnerability with depression (a general "internalising"/neuroticism factor).
NeurobiologicalDysregulation of serotonin, noradrenaline, and GABA; amygdala hyper-reactivity; for panic, a hypersensitive "suffocation alarm" and locus-coeruleus/noradrenergic over-activity; provocation by CO₂ and lactate.

Psychological theories

  • Clark's cognitive model of panic: panic results from the catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily sensations (e.g. reading a racing heart as "I'm having a heart attack"), setting up a vicious cycle of escalating fear and arousal — the basis of CBT for panic.
  • Beck's cognitive theory of anxiety: anxious people systematically overestimate danger and underestimate their ability to cope, with threat-biased attention and interpretation.
  • Intolerance of uncertainty model (GAD): worry is driven by a low tolerance for uncertain outcomes, plus positive beliefs about worry ("worrying keeps me prepared").
  • Borkovec's avoidance theory of worry (GAD): verbal worry suppresses distressing imagery and bodily arousal, negatively reinforcing the worry habit.
  • Psychodynamic: anxiety signals the threatened emergence of unacceptable unconscious impulses.

3. Epidemiology

  • GAD: lifetime prevalence around 5–6%; more common in women (~2:1); often chronic and fluctuating. Other evidence
  • Panic disorder: lifetime prevalence around 2–5%; more common in women; onset typically in early adulthood.
  • High comorbidity with depression and with each other.

Detailed subtypes with their diagnostic criteria

F41.0 — Panic disorder [episodic paroxysmal anxiety] → ICD-11 6B01

Concept: recurrent, unexpected panic attacks that are not restricted to any particular situation or set of circumstances and are therefore unpredictable.

Unique identifying features: a panic attack (a discrete episode of intense fear peaking within minutes) with sudden palpitations, chest pain, choking, dizziness, and feelings of unreality (depersonalization/derealization), often with a secondary fear of dying, losing control, or going mad. Between attacks there is anticipatory anxiety ("fear of fear"). Frequently leads to agoraphobic avoidance.

ICD-10 criteria: several severe attacks of autonomic anxiety within about 1 month, occurring in circumstances where there is no objective danger, not confined to known/predictable situations, with comparative freedom from anxiety symptoms between attacks (though anticipatory anxiety is common).

ICD-11 (6B01): recurrent unexpected panic attacks not restricted to particular stimuli/situations, followed by persistent concern about their recurrence or their significance, or behaviours intended to avoid recurrence, over ≥ several weeks. (In ICD-11 a "panic attack" specifier can also be added to other disorders when attacks are cued.)

F41.1 — Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) → ICD-11 6B00

Concept: generalised and persistent anxiety ("free-floating" — not restricted to, or strongly predominating in, any particular environmental circumstances) with excessive worry about everyday events.

Unique identifying features: persistent nervousness; the "3 clusters" — apprehension (worries about future misfortune, feeling on edge, poor concentration); motor tension (restlessness, tension headaches, trembling, inability to relax); and autonomic overactivity (light-headedness, sweating, tachycardia, epigastric discomfort, dizziness).

ICD-10 criteria: the person must have primary symptoms of anxiety most days for at least several weeks at a time, usually several months, involving elements of apprehension, motor tension, and autonomic overactivity.

ICD-11 (6B00): marked symptoms of anxiety with either general apprehension ("free-floating anxiety") or excessive worry about multiple everyday events, present most days for at least several months, accompanied by muscle tension, restlessness, autonomic overactivity, difficulty concentrating, irritability, or sleep disturbance, causing significant distress/impairment.

F41.2 — Mixed anxiety and depressive disorder → ICD-11 6A73

Concept: both anxiety and depressive symptoms are present, but neither set is severe enough to justify a diagnosis on its own. Common in primary care.

ICD-10 criteria: symptoms of both anxiety and depression are present, with neither clearly predominating and neither meeting the full criteria for an anxiety disorder or a depressive episode; some autonomic symptoms must be present.

ICD-11: moved out of the anxiety grouping to the depressive disorders as 6A73 (mixed depressive and anxiety disorder) — a notable classification change.

F41.3 other mixed anxiety disorders; F41.8 other specified anxiety disorders; F41.9 anxiety disorder, unspecified.

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

Panic disorder: "unexpected attacks + fear of the fear (anticipatory anxiety)". GAD — "WATCHERS" for its features: Worry (uncontrollable), Anxiety, Tension (muscle), Concentration poor, Hyperarousal/irritability, Energy low/fatigue, Restlessness, Sleep disturbance. Panic model: catastrophic misreading of body sensations.

7. Treatment

  • Panic disorder: CBT (including interoceptive exposure and cognitive restructuring of catastrophic misinterpretations) is highly effective; SSRIs/SNRIs first-line pharmacologically; avoid long-term benzodiazepines.
  • GAD: CBT (worry management, problem-solving, relaxation) and applied relaxation; SSRIs/SNRIs first-line; pregabalin as an option; buspirone; short-term benzodiazepines only if essential. Kaplan & Sadock

F42 · Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder → ICD-11 6B20

ICD-10 F42 (.0 predominantly obsessional thoughts/ruminations, .1 predominantly compulsive acts, .2 mixed, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B20 — now heads its own grouping "Obsessive-compulsive or related disorders" (6B2x)
Core Recurrent obsessions and/or compulsions
Key change ICD-11 moved OCD OUT of the anxiety disorders into its own grouping (with BDD, hoarding, hypochondriasis, olfactory reference, trichotillomania, excoriation)

1. Introduction

OCD is characterised by recurrent obsessions (intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that cause anxiety and are recognised as the person's own) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts the person feels driven to perform, usually to reduce the anxiety caused by an obsession or to prevent a feared event). The obsessions are ego-dystonic (unwanted and resisted), and performing the compulsion gives only temporary relief. ICD-10

2. Etiology

DomainDetail
GeneticSubstantial heritability; higher in first-degree relatives; early-onset OCD is more familial.
NeurobiologicalDysfunction of the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical (CSTC) circuit (a brain loop connecting the frontal cortex, deep basal ganglia, and thalamus, over-active in OCD); strong serotonin involvement (hence SSRI responsiveness). PANDAS (paediatric autoimmune neuropsychiatric disorder associated with streptococcal infection — abrupt-onset OCD/tics in children after strep infection) in a small subset.

Psychological theories

  • Behavioural (Mowrer's two-factor) model: obsessions raise anxiety; compulsions are negatively reinforced because they reduce that anxiety, so they persist — the rationale for exposure and response prevention (ERP).
  • Cognitive theory (Salkovskis; Rachman): intrusive thoughts are normal, but people with OCD appraise them as signifying personal responsibility for harm; the key process is an inflated sense of responsibility and thought–action fusion (the belief that thinking something is morally equivalent to doing it, or makes it more likely to happen). Neutralising acts prevent disconfirmation.
  • Psychodynamic: use of the defence mechanisms of isolation, undoing, and reaction formation; regression to the anal stage; conflict over aggressive/sexual impulses.

3. Epidemiology

  • Lifetime prevalence around 2–3%; roughly equal in men and women overall (earlier onset in males); onset usually in adolescence/early adulthood. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Tends to be chronic with waxing and waning course; often long delay before treatment.

4. Clinical features, unique presentations, and comorbidities

Unique identifying features

  • Common obsessional themes: contamination; doubt/checking; symmetry/ordering; forbidden aggressive, sexual, or blasphemous thoughts; hoarding (now separate).
  • Common compulsions: washing, checking, counting, ordering, repeating, mental rituals.
  • Obsessions are recognised as the person's own and are resisted (at least initially) — this ego-dystonic quality distinguishes them from delusions and from the ego-syntonic traits of obsessive-compulsive personality disorder.
  • Insight is usually retained (an ICD-11 specifier records fair/good vs poor/absent insight).

Comorbidities

  • Depression (very common), other anxiety disorders, tic disorders/Tourette syndrome (especially early-onset OCD), body dysmorphic disorder, eating disorders, and OC personality disorder.

5. Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10

For a definite diagnosis, obsessional symptoms or compulsive acts (or both) must be present on most days for at least 2 successive weeks and be a source of distress or interference with activities. The obsessional symptoms should have these features: (a) recognised as the individual's own thoughts/impulses; (b) at least one is unsuccessfully resisted; (c) the thought of carrying out the act is not in itself pleasurable (relief of anxiety is not regarded as pleasure); (d) the thoughts/images/impulses are unpleasantly repetitive.

ICD-11 (6B20)

Persistent obsessions and/or compulsions. Obsessions are repetitive, intrusive, unwanted thoughts/images/urges that are typically anxiety-provoking; compulsions are repetitive behaviours or mental acts performed in response to an obsession, according to rigid rules, or to achieve a sense of "completeness". Symptoms are time-consuming (e.g. >1 hour/day) or cause significant distress/impairment. Specify degree of insight (fair/good vs poor/absent).

Detailed subtypes with their diagnostic criteria

F42.0 — Predominantly obsessional thoughts or ruminations

Obsessions dominate: intrusive thoughts, images, or impulses, or ruminations (endless, inconclusive internal debate, often over trivial alternatives). There are no, or minimal, overt compulsive acts (though covert mental rituals may occur). Content is often distressing (violence, obscenity, or pointless questioning).

F42.1 — Predominantly compulsive acts [obsessional rituals]

Compulsive/ritual behaviours dominate — most concerned with cleaning (esp. handwashing), repeated checking to guard against a dangerous situation, or orderliness/tidiness. The behaviour is an attempt to ward off an objectively unlikely danger, is recognised as pointless/ineffectual, and is repeatedly resisted.

F42.2 — Mixed obsessional thoughts and acts

Both obsessional thoughts and compulsive acts are present and roughly equally prominent (the commonest presentation).

F42.8 other obsessive-compulsive disorders; F42.9 obsessive-compulsive disorder, unspecified.

ICD-11 OCD-related grouping (6B2x) — the related disorders that keep OCD company: 6B20 OCD; 6B21 body dysmorphic disorder (preoccupation with a perceived defect in appearance); 6B22 olfactory reference disorder (preoccupation that one emits a foul body odour); 6B23 hypochondriasis (health anxiety) — moved here from the somatoform disorders; 6B24 hoarding disorder; 6B25 body-focused repetitive behaviour disorders (trichotillomania = hair-pulling; excoriation = skin-picking). Each is specified by insight (fair/good vs poor/absent).

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

OCD = Obsessions (ego-dystonic, resisted thoughts) drive Compulsions (relief-seeking acts). Common types — "Washing, Checking, Counting, Ordering, Hoarding" (WCCOH). Cognitive core — inflated responsibility + thought–action fusion. Treatment reflex — ERP + SSRI.

7. Treatment

  • Psychological (first-line): Exposure and response prevention (ERP) (the person is exposed to the anxiety-provoking obsessional trigger and helped to refrain from performing the compulsion, allowing the anxiety to fall on its own); cognitive therapy targeting responsibility beliefs and thought–action fusion.
  • Pharmacological: SSRIs at higher doses and for longer than in depression; clomipramine (a serotonergic tricyclic) as an alternative; augmentation with an antipsychotic in resistant cases; deep brain stimulation/neurosurgery reserved for severe, refractory OCD. Kaplan & Sadock

F43 · Reaction to Severe Stress, and Adjustment Disorders → ICD-11 6B4x

ICD-10 F43 (.0 acute stress reaction, .1 PTSD, .2 adjustment disorders [.20–.28], .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B40 PTSD; 6B41 Complex PTSD (new); 6B42 Prolonged grief disorder (new); 6B43 Adjustment disorder; acute stress reaction reframed as a normal reaction (QE84)
Core Disorders arising as a direct consequence of an identifiable stressor or trauma
Key point Uniquely, a stressor is a necessary causal factor — the disorder would not have occurred without it

1. Introduction

Unlike other disorders, F43 disorders are identified not only on symptoms but on the presence of a causal stressor — either an exceptionally stressful/traumatic life event (producing acute stress reaction or PTSD) or a significant life change/ongoing difficulty (producing an adjustment disorder). The stressor is a necessary, though not sufficient, cause. ICD-10

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

DomainDetail
The stressorNecessary cause — severity, duration, proximity, and interpersonal nature of the trauma (esp. deliberate human cruelty) raise risk; repeated/prolonged trauma (childhood abuse, captivity) predisposes to Complex PTSD.
Vulnerability / protectivePrior trauma, pre-existing mental disorder, female sex, low social support, and neuroticism raise risk; strong social support is protective.
NeurobiologicalDysregulated HPA-axis and noradrenergic systems; over-active amygdala with reduced prefrontal control and hippocampal changes; failure of fear extinction.

Psychological theories (PTSD)

  • Conditioning (two-factor) model: trauma cues become conditioned fear stimuli; avoidance is negatively reinforced and prevents extinction.
  • Emotional processing theory (Foa & Kozak): PTSD reflects a fear network that has not been adequately processed; recovery requires activating and modifying it — the basis of prolonged exposure.
  • Ehlers & Clark cognitive model: PTSD persists when the trauma is processed in a way that creates a sense of current threat — through excessively negative appraisals and a poorly elaborated, disjointed trauma memory.
  • Dual-representation theory: two memory systems (verbally accessible vs situationally accessible) explain intrusive, sensory flashbacks.
  • For grief: attachment theory and the dual-process model of grieving (oscillation between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented coping).

3. Epidemiology

  • Trauma exposure is common; only a minority develop PTSD. Lifetime PTSD prevalence roughly 6–8%, higher after interpersonal violence; about twice as common in women. Other evidence
  • Adjustment disorders are among the most frequently diagnosed conditions in liaison and primary-care settings.

Detailed subtypes with their diagnostic criteria

F43.0 — Acute stress reaction

Concept: a transient disorder developing in response to exceptional physical or mental stress, usually subsiding within hours to days.

Identifying features: an initial state of "daze" with narrowing of attention, disorientation, then either withdrawal (up to dissociative stupor) or agitation/overactivity; autonomic signs of panic; onset within minutes of the stressor and rapid resolution (within hours if the stressor is removed, or 2–3 days at most).

ICD-10 criteria: immediate and clear temporal connection between an exceptional stressor and onset (usually within minutes); symptoms show a mixed and usually changing picture; they begin to diminish after 24–48 hours and are usually minimal after about 3 days.

ICD-11: acute stress reaction is NOT classified as a mental disorder — it is regarded as a normal reaction to severe stress and listed among "factors influencing health status" (QE84). This is an important examinable change.

F43.1 — Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) → ICD-11 6B40

Concept: a delayed and/or protracted response to an exceptionally threatening or catastrophic event that would cause pervasive distress in almost anyone.

The three symptom clusters (identifying features): (1) re-experiencing (intrusive memories, flashbacks, or nightmares in which the trauma is relived in the present); (2) avoidance (of reminders, thoughts, places, or people associated with the trauma); and (3) a sense of current threat (persistent hyperarousal — hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, irritability, sleep disturbance). Onset usually within 6 months of the trauma.

ICD-10 criteria: exposure to a stressor of exceptional severity; persistent remembering/reliving in intrusive memories, flashbacks, or dreams; avoidance of reminders; and either inability to recall aspects of the trauma or persistent symptoms of increased arousal. Onset within 6 months of the event.

ICD-11 (6B40): tightened, more specific criteria requiring all three core elements — (1) re-experiencing the trauma in the present (not just remembering it); (2) deliberate avoidance of reminders; and (3) a persistent sense of current threat (hypervigilance/startle) — persisting for several weeks and causing significant impairment.

Complex PTSD → ICD-11 6B41 (NEW — no ICD-10 equivalent)

Concept: arises after prolonged or repeated trauma from which escape is difficult (childhood abuse, domestic violence, torture, captivity). It includes all three PTSD clusters PLUS three "disturbances in self-organisation": (i) affect dysregulation (difficulty regulating emotions); (ii) negative self-concept (persistent beliefs of worthlessness, shame, guilt); and (iii) disturbances in relationships (difficulty sustaining relationships and feeling close to others).

Prolonged grief disorder → ICD-11 6B42 (NEW — no ICD-10 equivalent)

Concept: a persistent and pervasive grief response (longing/yearning for, or preoccupation with, the deceased) accompanied by intense emotional pain, persisting abnormally long after the loss (at least 6 months, and well beyond the expected social/cultural/religious norm) and causing significant impairment.

F43.2 — Adjustment disorders → ICD-11 6B43

Concept: states of subjective distress and emotional disturbance arising in the period of adaptation to a significant life change or stressful life event (bereavement, migration, illness, separation), that would not have arisen without the stressor.

ICD-10 criteria: onset usually within 1 month of the stressor; symptoms (depressed mood, anxiety, worry, inability to cope) do not exceed those of the specified subtypes; duration usually does not exceed 6 months (except prolonged depressive reaction). Sub-codes:

  • F43.20 brief depressive reaction (transient mild depression, ≤ 1 month).
  • F43.21 prolonged depressive reaction (mild depression in response to prolonged stress, up to 2 years).
  • F43.22 mixed anxiety and depressive reaction.
  • F43.23 with predominant disturbance of other emotions (anxiety, worry, anger).
  • F43.24 with predominant disturbance of conduct (e.g. aggressive or antisocial behaviour).
  • F43.25 with mixed disturbance of emotions and conduct.
  • F43.28 with other specified predominant symptoms.

ICD-11 (6B43): redefined around a preoccupation with the stressor or its consequences (excessive worry, recurrent distressing thoughts) plus failure to adapt, arising within a month of the stressor and typically resolving within 6 months of the stressor ending.

F43.8 other reactions to severe stress; F43.9 reaction to severe stress, unspecified.

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

PTSD core — the "3 clusters": Re-experiencing (flashbacks/nightmares), Avoidance, Hyperarousal/current threat — "RAH". Complex PTSD = PTSD + "DSO" (Disturbances in Self-Organisation): affect dysregulation, negative self-concept, relationship difficulties. Timeline: acute stress reaction = minutes-days; PTSD = usually within 6 months, lasting weeks+; adjustment disorder = within 1 month, resolves by ~6 months.

7. Treatment

  • Acute stress reaction: reassurance, safety, practical support, brief symptomatic help; most resolve without formal treatment (avoid single-session psychological debriefing, which can be harmful).
  • PTSD: trauma-focused psychological therapies first-line — trauma-focused CBT / prolonged exposure and EMDR (eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing) (a structured trauma therapy using guided eye movements while processing the memory); SSRIs/SNRIs pharmacologically; prazosin for nightmares. Complex PTSD needs a phased approach (stabilisation, trauma processing, reintegration).
  • Adjustment / grief: supportive and problem-focused psychotherapy, brief CBT; treat significant depression/anxiety; grief-focused therapy for prolonged grief disorder. Kaplan & Sadock

F44 · Dissociative (Conversion) Disorders → ICD-11 6B6x

ICD-10 F44 (.0 amnesia, .1 fugue, .2 stupor, .3 trance/possession, .4 motor, .5 convulsions, .6 anaesthesia/sensory loss, .7 mixed, .8 [incl. .80 Ganser, .81 multiple personality])
ICD-11 6B60 dissociative neurological symptom disorder; 6B61 dissociative amnesia (± fugue); 6B63 trance/possession; 6B64/6B65 dissociative identity (full/partial); 6B66 depersonalization-derealization
Core Partial or complete loss of the normal integration of memories, identity, sensation, and control of movement
Key point Presumed to be psychogenic (linked to trauma/conflict) with no organic cause

1. Introduction

These disorders share a partial or complete loss of the normal integration between memories of the past, awareness of identity and immediate sensations, and control of bodily movements. ICD-10 groups "conversion" with "dissociative" because the older term "conversion" (physical symptoms without organic cause) and dissociation (of memory/identity) are presumed to share a psychogenic mechanism. Onset and remission are often sudden and closely linked in time to traumatic events, insoluble problems, or disturbed relationships. ICD-10

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

DomainDetail
Trauma / stressStrong association with acute stress, unresolved conflict, and especially childhood trauma/abuse (particularly for dissociative amnesia and dissociative identity disorder).
NeurobiologicalAltered connectivity between limbic (emotion) and sensorimotor/prefrontal regions; functional imaging shows the symptoms are genuinely involuntary (not feigned).

Psychological theories

  • Psychodynamic: dissociation/conversion as a defence — intolerable memories or impulses are split off from consciousness (repression/dissociation). Classic concepts: primary gain (the symptom keeps the internal conflict out of awareness, reducing anxiety) and secondary gain (external advantages the symptom brings, e.g. care, avoiding responsibility), and la belle indifférence (a surprising lack of concern about a serious apparent disability).
  • Learning/behavioural: the "sick role" and symptoms are reinforced by attention and avoidance of stressors.
  • Cognitive / autohypnotic model: a capacity for self-hypnosis and altered self-representation; the trauma model of dissociative identity disorder holds that dissociation protects the child from overwhelming abuse.

3. Epidemiology & comorbidities

  • More common in women, in younger people, and where medical/psychological sophistication is lower; conversion symptoms are common in neurology and emergency settings. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Comorbidities: depression, PTSD and other trauma-related disorders, other anxiety disorders, personality disorders (esp. borderline), and (for conversion) comorbid neurological disease.

5. General diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10

For a definite diagnosis: (a) the clinical features specified for the individual subtype; (b) no evidence of a physical disorder that could explain the symptoms; and (c) evidence of psychological causation, in the form of a clear association in time with stressful events, problems, or disturbed relationships (even if denied by the individual).

ICD-11

Involuntary disruption or discontinuity in the normal integration of identity, sensations, perceptions, affect, thoughts, memory, control of movement, or behaviour. Symptoms are not better explained by another disorder, substance, or medical condition, and cause significant distress or impairment. (ICD-11 drops the word "conversion" and codes the neurological-symptom forms as dissociative neurological symptom disorder, 6B60.)

Detailed subtypes with their diagnostic criteria

F44.0 — Dissociative amnesia → ICD-11 6B61

Amnesia, usually for recent events of a traumatic/stressful nature (e.g. accidents, bereavement), that is too extensive to be ordinary forgetfulness and is not due to an organic mental disorder. It is typically partial and selective, and centred on traumatic material. ICD-11 codes dissociative fugue as a specifier of 6B61 (with/without dissociative fugue).

F44.1 — Dissociative fugue → ICD-11 6B61 (with dissociative fugue)

All the features of dissociative amnesia PLUS an apparently purposeful journey away from home or work, during which self-care is maintained; there may be assumption of a new identity, and the person appears normal to observers. Amnesia for the fugue period follows recovery.

F44.2 — Dissociative stupor

Profound diminution or absence of voluntary movement, speech, and normal responsiveness to light, noise, and touch, but with muscle tone, posture, breathing, and eye movements indicating the person is neither asleep nor unconscious; no physical or other psychiatric cause is found, and there is positive evidence of psychogenic causation.

F44.3 — Trance and possession disorders → ICD-11 6B63

Temporary loss of the sense of personal identity and full awareness of surroundings; in possession states the person is convinced of being taken over by a spirit, power, deity, or other person. Only involuntary and unwanted states, occurring outside accepted cultural/religious situations, are classified here.

F44.4–.7 — Dissociative disorders of movement and sensation (conversion) → ICD-11 6B60

  • F44.4 Dissociative motor disorders: loss of ability to move a limb or part of a limb, or of coordination (ataxia), or inability to stand unaided (astasia-abasia); paralysis may be partial or complete.
  • F44.5 Dissociative convulsions ("pseudoseizures" / non-epileptic attacks): movements mimicking epileptic seizures, but with rarely any tongue-biting, serious injury, or incontinence, and no loss of consciousness (rather stupor or trance).
  • F44.6 Dissociative anaesthesia and sensory loss: areas of anaesthesia often with boundaries that follow the person's ideas about the body rather than anatomy (e.g. "glove-and-stocking" loss); may include dissociative visual loss, deafness, or anosmia.
  • F44.7 Mixed dissociative (conversion) disorders: combinations of the above.
  • ICD-11 codes all of these as 6B60 dissociative neurological symptom disorder, with specifiers for the symptom type (with visual/auditory/sensory disturbance, non-epileptic seizures, movement/gait/weakness, speech, etc.).

F44.8 — Other dissociative (conversion) disorders (named syndromes)

  • F44.80 Ganser's syndrome: the characteristic feature is the giving of approximate answers ("vorbeireden" — near-miss but clearly wrong answers to simple questions, e.g. "2 + 2 = 5"; a cow has "3 legs"), usually accompanied by other dissociative symptoms, often in a forensic setting.
  • F44.81 Multiple personality disorder → ICD-11 6B64 (dissociative identity disorder) / 6B65 (partial DID): the apparent existence of two or more distinct personalities/identity states within one individual, only one evident at a time, each with its own memories, preferences, and behaviour, with an inability to recall important personal information too extensive for ordinary forgetfulness. Strongly associated with severe childhood trauma.
  • F44.82 transient dissociative disorders occurring in childhood/adolescence; F44.88 other.

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

Dissociation of MEMORY/IDENTITY (amnesia, fugue, DID) vs conversion of MOVEMENT/SENSATION (paralysis, pseudoseizures, sensory loss). Conversion clues — "la belle indifférence", non-anatomical deficits, and secondary gain. Ganser = approximate answers. DID = ≥2 identities + amnesia + childhood trauma.

7. Treatment

  • Psychological (mainstay): establish safety and rapport; psychoeducation and reassurance; address the underlying stressor/conflict; CBT; for conversion, physiotherapy and a rehabilitative "expectation of recovery" approach; trauma-focused, phased therapy for DID; hypnosis and abreaction historically used for amnesia.
  • Pharmacological: no specific drug; treat comorbid depression/anxiety/PTSD. Avoid unnecessary investigations and reinforcement of the sick role. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Prognosis: acute conversion/dissociative states of recent onset with a clear stressor usually have a good prognosis; chronic states and DID are more persistent.

F45 · Somatoform Disorders → ICD-11 6C20 (Bodily Distress Disorder)

ICD-10 F45 (.0 somatization, .1 undifferentiated, .2 hypochondriacal, .3 somatoform autonomic dysfunction [.30–.38], .4 persistent somatoform pain, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6C20 bodily distress disorder (mild/moderate/severe); hypochondriasis moved to 6B23 (OCD-related grouping)
Core Repeated presentation of physical symptoms with persistent requests for medical investigation, despite negative findings and reassurance
Key change ICD-11 collapses most somatoform disorders into a single "bodily distress disorder", and re-homes hypochondriasis with OCD

1. Introduction

The main feature is repeated presentation of physical symptoms together with persistent requests for medical investigations, in spite of repeated negative findings and reassurance that the symptoms have no physical basis. The symptoms are distressing, and the person's attention is focused on them, causing repeated help-seeking. ICD-10

2. Etiology (with psychological theories)

Psychological theories

  • Cognitive-perceptual model: heightened somatosensory amplification (a tendency to notice, focus on, and be alarmed by normal or benign bodily sensations) and catastrophic misinterpretation of these sensations as evidence of serious disease.
  • Behavioural: symptom-focused behaviour, checking, and reassurance-seeking are reinforced by anxiety reduction and by attention (the "sick role"); avoidance prevents disconfirmation.
  • Psychodynamic: physical symptoms as a means of expressing unacceptable emotions (alexithymia — difficulty identifying and describing one's own feelings), and of obtaining care.
  • Learning / family: modelling of illness behaviour, early experience of illness, and reinforcement within the family/health system.

3. Epidemiology & comorbidities

  • Common in primary and general medical care; more frequent in women; onset usually before age 30 for somatization. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Comorbidities: depression and anxiety disorders (very high), other functional somatic syndromes, and personality disorders; strong association with early adversity.

Detailed subtypes with their diagnostic criteria

F45.0 — Somatization disorder (Briquet's syndrome)

Multiple, recurrent, and frequently changing physical symptoms of at least 2 years' duration, referable to any body system, for which no adequate physical explanation is found; persistent refusal to accept reassurance; and some degree of impairment. There is a long, complicated history of contact with medical services and many negative investigations or operations.

F45.1 — Undifferentiated somatoform disorder

Multiple, varying, persistent physical complaints, but the complete and typical clinical picture of somatization disorder is not fulfilled (e.g. fewer symptoms, shorter duration).

F45.2 — Hypochondriacal disorder → ICD-11 6B23 (Hypochondriasis, in the OCD-related grouping)

A persistent preoccupation with the possibility of having one or more serious and progressive physical disorders, shown by persistent bodily complaints or preoccupation with physical appearance; normal sensations are interpreted as abnormal and distressing; attention is focused on one or two organs/systems. Includes body dysmorphic disorder in ICD-10 (preoccupation with imagined deformity) — which ICD-11 codes separately as 6B21. ICD-11 reclassifies hypochondriasis (health anxiety) into the obsessive-compulsive or related disorders, reflecting its obsessional, checking, and reassurance-seeking quality.

F45.3 — Somatoform autonomic dysfunction

Symptoms presented as if due to a physical disorder of a system largely under autonomic control, with two symptom types: autonomic arousal signs (palpitations, sweating, tremor, flushing) and idiosyncratic, subjective symptoms referred to a specific organ. Sub-codes specify the system: .30 heart and cardiovascular system (e.g. "cardiac neurosis", Da Costa's syndrome); .31 upper gastrointestinal tract (e.g. "gastric neurosis", psychogenic aerophagy); .32 lower gastrointestinal tract (e.g. irritable bowel syndrome, psychogenic diarrhoea); .33 respiratory system (psychogenic hyperventilation); .34 genitourinary system (psychogenic frequency/dysuria); .38 other organ or system.

F45.4 — Persistent somatoform pain disorder

Persistent, severe, and distressing pain that cannot be fully explained by a physiological process or physical disorder, occurring in association with emotional conflict or psychosocial problems sufficient to be judged the main causal influence, and resulting in increased support and attention.

F45.8 other somatoform disorders (e.g. psychogenic dysmenorrhoea, dysphagia/"globus hystericus", pruritus, torticollis); F45.9 somatoform disorder, unspecified.

ICD-11 (6C20 Bodily distress disorder): the presence of bodily symptoms that are distressing and to which excessive attention is directed (not explained by the person's focus being on the fear of illness itself), with repeated contact with health services, persisting for several months; rated mild, moderate, or severe. It replaces most F45 categories by focusing on the distress/preoccupation rather than on whether the symptoms are "medically unexplained".

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

Somatization = many symptoms, many systems, many years (the "many"s). Hypochondriasis = fear of having a disease (now grouped with OCD — health anxiety). Pain disorder = one persistent unexplained pain. Cognitive core — somatosensory amplification + catastrophic misinterpretation. ICD-11 → one Bodily Distress Disorder.

7. Treatment

  • Psychological (mainstay): a single, consistent clinician; regular fixed appointments (not symptom-driven); reattribution and CBT (linking symptoms to stress, reducing checking/reassurance-seeking); mindfulness; treat the underlying depression/anxiety.
  • Pharmacological: antidepressants (SSRIs/tricyclics) help comorbid depression/anxiety and some pain; avoid unnecessary investigations, referrals, and iatrogenic harm. Kaplan & Sadock

F48 · Other Neurotic Disorders → ICD-11 (redistributed)

ICD-10 F48 (.0 neurasthenia, .1 depersonalization-derealization, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6B66 depersonalization-derealization disorder (dissociative grouping); neurasthenia largely deprecated
Core Residual neurotic conditions not classified elsewhere

Detailed subtypes with their diagnostic criteria

F48.0 — Neurasthenia

Concept: two overlapping types — (1) persistent, distressing fatigue after mental effort (with poor concentration and inefficiency); or (2) fatigue and bodily weakness after minimal physical effort, with muscular aches and inability to relax. Both include unpleasant physical sensations (dizziness, tension headaches), worry about declining wellbeing, irritability, anhedonia, and sleep disturbance.

ICD-10 criteria: either persistent complaint of increased fatigue after mental effort, or persistent complaint of bodily weakness/exhaustion after minimal effort; accompanied by at least two of — muscular aches, dizziness, tension headaches, sleep disturbance, inability to relax, irritability; not explained by another disorder. ICD-11: the category is largely deprecated/omitted (overlaps with fatigue syndromes and bodily distress disorder); considered culturally variable and controversial.

F48.1 — Depersonalization-derealization syndrome → ICD-11 6B66

Concept: depersonalization (a feeling that one's own self, feelings, or body are unreal, remote, or "not one's own" — as if watching oneself from outside) and/or derealization (a feeling that the surroundings, objects, or other people are unreal, flat, or dream-like), experienced with intact insight that this is a subjective change and not externally imposed.

ICD-10 criteria: either (or both) depersonalization (the individual feels their feelings/experiences are detached, distant, not their own, lost) or derealization (objects, people, surroundings seem unreal, distant, artificial, colourless, lifeless); the person accepts this is a subjective and spontaneous change, not imposed by outside forces (i.e. insight is retained — distinguishing it from psychosis). ICD-11 (6B66): persistent or recurrent depersonalization and/or derealization with intact reality testing, causing significant distress/impairment, and moved into the dissociative disorders grouping.

F48.8 other specified neurotic disorders (e.g. Dhat syndrome, occupational neuroses, "psychasthenia"); F48.9 neurotic disorder, unspecified.

Treatment (F48)

  • Neurasthenia: graded activity, sleep and stress management, CBT, and treatment of comorbid depression/anxiety.
  • Depersonalization-derealization: psychoeducation and reassurance, CBT (reducing catastrophic appraisal of the experience and self-monitoring), treatment of comorbid anxiety/depression; grounding techniques; SSRIs may help. Kaplan & Sadock

Two golden rules for the block

Rule 1 — ICD-11 dismantled "neurotic disorders": anxiety/fear (6B0x), obsessive-compulsive/related (6B2x, incl. hypochondriasis and BDD), stress-associated (6B4x, incl. new Complex PTSD and Prolonged grief), dissociative (6B6x), and bodily distress (6C2x).

Rule 2 — always exclude organic disease and substances first; in this block insight is characteristically retained (distinguishing these disorders from psychosis), and a stressor is a necessary cause only for the F43 group.
