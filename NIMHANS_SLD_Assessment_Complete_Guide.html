Part I — Foundations

1 · What is Specific Learning Disability?

Specific Learning Disability (SLD) is a neurodevelopmental condition in which a child shows persistent difficulty acquiring and using academic skills — reading, written expression/spelling, and/or mathematics — that is unexpected given the child's age, intelligence, schooling, and effort, and that is not primarily explained by intellectual disability, uncorrected sensory impairment, neurological disorder, psychosocial adversity, inadequate instruction, or lack of proficiency in the language of instruction. Per DSM-5, the difficulties persist for at least 6 months despite the provision of targeted intervention (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; as cited in the NIMHANS 2020 monograph).

Core characteristics (as described in the source)

Discrepancy

A gap between the child's ability and academic performance in one or more skill areas.

Academic learning difficulty

Problems concentrated in reading, writing, spelling and/or mathematics.

Perceptual difficulty

Difficulty recognising, discriminating or interpreting auditory and/or visual information.

Metacognitive deficits

Weak self-regulation — planning, monitoring, checking and correcting one's own work.

Memory problems

Children often fail to use the memory strategies that non-disabled peers use readily.

Phonological deficits

Poor phonological skills frequently accompany reading & spelling difficulty.

Attention / motor / social-emotional

Short attention span, clumsiness/fine-motor difficulty, and higher rates of low self-esteem and emotional disturbance from repeated academic failure.

Typical presentation & impact

  • Age of identification: usually in the early school years, once formal literacy/numeracy demands begin; often first flagged by a teacher or parent.
  • Academic impact: underachievement relative to grade placement, slow/laboured work, avoidance of reading/writing/maths.
  • Emotional impact: frustration, anxiety, shame, low academic self-concept; the source notes a higher prevalence of psychological disturbance than in the general population.
  • Social & family impact: peer comparison, avoidance, and parent–child conflict around homework and marks.

Causes / correlates (per the source)

The source states there is no single cause; a variety of factors are linked: genetic (LD runs in families), language (poor phonological processing in reading/spelling disability), biochemical (chemical imbalance affecting brain function), biological (head trauma, foetal distress at birth), unfavourable uterine environment (drugs, smoking, alcohol), and environmental deprivation (lack of stimulation at critical developmental periods). Other correlates mentioned include laterality and left-hemisphere structural factors.

2 · Key Distinctions

A central skill is separating a true SLD from conditions it can resemble. Keep these distinctions explicit in every formulation.

The guiding principle.SLD is a diagnosis of unexpected, specific underachievement in a child of adequate intelligence, adequate sensory function, adequate schooling, and adequate opportunity. Every alternative explanation must be considered before the label is applied.

3 · SLD Domains

The exact subtests and terminology follow the NIMHANS source (§26–31). Broadly, three academic domains are assessed:

Reading

  • Letter/word recognition & decoding
  • Reading accuracy
  • Reading fluency & speed
  • Reading comprehension
  • Listening comprehension (when reading is severe)
  • Phonological processing (where assessed)

Written Expression

  • Handwriting (letter formation, size, spacing, alignment)
  • Copying
  • Spelling (Schonell graded)
  • Sentence construction & grammar
  • Written expression / organisation
  • Writing speed & fluency

Mathematics

  • Number recognition & concepts
  • Counting & quantity
  • Calculation (+ − × ÷, fractions)
  • Place value, carrying/borrowing
  • Mathematical reasoning
  • Word (statement) problems
Follow the source for terminology.The NIMHANS Index organises these as Level I (younger) and Level II (older) subtests with specific names (e.g. "Reading of English passages of I–IX grade levels", "Spelling of English words of I–X Std", Schonell Graded Spelling Test). Use the manual's exact subtest names in reports (§27, §30).
Part II — The Clinical Interview

4 · The Central Clinical Spine

Every section of this handbook returns to one repeating structure. Hold it in mind through the whole assessment:

ASK→ POSSIBLE ANSWER→ PROBE→ OBSERVE→ ASSESS→ SCORE→ INTERPRET→ DIFFERENTIATE→ FORMULATE→ DOCUMENT→ RECOMMEND
Why a spine, not a checklist.SLD assessment is not just test administration. The interview generates hypotheses; the tests provide objective data; observation and records triangulate; and the formulation integrates all of it. The source stresses that test findings are one source of information and should never be the end-point — they are interpreted within the whole clinical picture.
Part II — The Clinical Interview

5 · Interview Question Bank

Each card below follows the spine: ASK → POSSIBLE ANSWERS → PROBE → CLINICAL SIGNIFICANCE → DOCUMENTATION. Possible answers are colour-coded: unremarkable mild concern significant concern severe concern uncertain / "don't know".

"What made you seek an assessment for your child now?"
Possible answers "School counsellor suggested a routine check" "He's slower than classmates in reading" "The teacher says he may fail; he still can't read Std-II words" "He has been held back a year and refuses to go to school" "We're not sure, the school just told us to come"
ProbeWho first raised the concern — parent, teacher, school, or the child? When exactly? Has it worsened, stayed the same, or fluctuated? Which subjects most?
SignificanceClarifies the referral question, onset, trajectory, and whether the concern is corroborated across settings. Vague or externally-driven referrals need careful history before any test is chosen.
Documentation"The child was brought for assessment on the recommendation of the class teacher, who reported persistent difficulty with grade-level reading over the past academic year."
"Which subjects or tasks are hardest, and how do they look at home?"
Possible answers "Only a bit weak in one subject" "Reading aloud and spelling — he dreads them" "Maths word problems; he can't start" "Everything written; copying takes hours" "We don't sit with homework, so we're not sure"
ProbeHome vs school? With help vs independent? Oral vs written performance? Examples from notebooks?
SignificanceLocalises the domain(s); the oral-vs-written gap is a classic SLD pointer; "improves with help" suggests the skill is attainable with support.
Documentation"Caregiver reported difficulty with reading aloud and spelling, more marked in written than in oral tasks."
"When did you first notice the difficulty, and has it changed over time?"
Possible answers "Only this term, after a long illness" "Since Std I, he struggled to learn letters" "Always — and it is getting worse relative to the class" "Can't say exactly"
ProbeAge/class at onset; any skill once learned then lost (regression — see §18); any preceding illness, school change, or psychosocial event?
SignificanceInsidious, long-standing, skill-specific difficulty fits SLD; abrupt onset after an event raises other explanations (illness, emotional, educational disruption).
Documentation"Difficulty with reading was reportedly present since Std I and has been persistent."
"Has your child had any help so far, and did it make a difference?"
Possible answers "Regular tuition — doing much better now" "Some extra classes, slight improvement" "Tutor for two years, little change in reading" "No one has ever worked on it specifically"
ProbeWhat kind of help (general tuition vs structured remediation)? Duration, intensity, response? Previous assessments or reports?
SignificanceResponse-to-intervention is central: persistent difficulty despite adequate, targeted help strengthens the SLD picture; absence of any instruction points toward educational deprivation first.
Documentation"The child reportedly received private tuition for approximately two years with minimal reported change in reading accuracy."
Interview stance.Ask open questions first, then probe. Record the informant's own words as chief complaints before translating them into clinical language (§58). Note disagreements between child and parent rather than resolving them prematurely (§39).

6 · Identification Data

Record

  • Name, age, date of birth, sex
  • Class / grade and school
  • Medium of instruction & type of school (govt / private / aided)
  • Residence; socioeconomic background
  • Birth order; family structure (nuclear/joint)
  • Informant & relationship
  • Referral source & reason for referral

Example documentation

Master A, a 9-year-old boy studying in Std IV in an English-medium private school, is the second of two siblings from a nuclear family of middle socioeconomic status, residing in [city]. He was brought by his mother (informant) on referral from his class teacher for assessment of academic difficulties.

Why medium of instruction matters.The NIMHANS Index norms were collected on English-medium school children in Bengaluru. Document the medium and the child's language exposure explicitly (§41) — difficulty in the language of instruction is not automatically SLD.
Part II — The Clinical Interview

7 · Informant & Reliability

Ask

  • Who accompanied the child, and what is their relationship?
  • How much time does the informant spend with the child daily?
  • Does the informant supervise homework / know school performance?
  • Has the informant seen the child's notebooks and report cards?
  • Are teacher reports or multiple informants available?

Documenting reliability

Reliable Informant spends substantial time with the child, supervises school work, and gives consistent, corroborated information.

Partially reliable Knows the child well in some domains but not others (e.g. not involved in homework).

Limited reliability Limited contact time / limited knowledge of academic functioning; information needs corroboration.

Requires corroboration Where accounts conflict or key facts are uncertain, note that corroboration (teacher report, records) is required.

Do not over-label.Do not call an informant unreliable merely because the parent is distressed or has limited formal education. Judge reliability by contact, consistency, and corroboration — not by affect or education.

  • "What made you seek an assessment for your child?"
  • "What difficulties are you noticing in studies?"
  • "Which subjects are most difficult?"
  • "When did you first notice these difficulties?"
  • "Who first raised the concern — parents, teacher, school, or child?"

9 · Chief Complaints

Write chief complaints concisely in the informant's own words, with duration — not as a premature diagnosis.

Common areas (parent-reported)

  • Difficulty reading / reads slowly
  • Difficulty writing / poor spelling
  • Difficulty in mathematics / forgets tables
  • Slow completion of homework
  • Poor academic performance
  • Difficulty understanding written material
  • Difficulty remembering lessons

Example

Chief complaints (as reported by mother):

  • "Reads like a much younger child" — since Std I
  • "Spells the same word differently each time" — ~2 years
  • "Takes very long to finish written homework" — ongoing
Rule.Use simple, parent-reported language. Convert to clinical terminology only in the interpretation/formulation (§58), never at the complaint stage.
Part II — The Clinical Interview

10 · History of Presenting Difficulty

A typical opening formulation: "The index child was reportedly functioning adequately until…" — or an onset-from-start formulation where the difficulty was present from the beginning of formal schooling.

Cover systematically

  • When first noticed; age/class at onset
  • Subject affected first; progression
  • Current severity & frequency
  • Situations in which it occurs (home vs school)
  • Teacher observations
  • Homework & examination performance
  • Remedial teaching tried & response
  • Previous assessments / interventions
  • Current academic functioning
  • Impact on mood, motivation, attendance

Example documentation

Difficulty with reading was reportedly present from the time the child began formal schooling. He was slow to learn letter–sound correspondences and continues to read below grade level, more so with unfamiliar words. The difficulty has been persistent and is reported both at home and at school. Private tuition over two years produced minimal change. No prior formal assessment had been carried out.

Part III — Subject-Wise History

11 · Reading / Language History

Ask

  • Does the child recognise letters? Confuse or reverse letters?
  • Difficulty sounding out words (decoding)?
  • Reads slowly? Skips words? Adds words?
  • Repeats words/lines? Loses place while reading?
  • Guesses unfamiliar words from the first letter/picture?
  • Difficulty understanding what was read (comprehension)?
  • Can the child read aloud? How does it compare to peers?
  • Does reading improve with assistance?
  • Is listening comprehension better than reading comprehension?
"When your child reads aloud, what exactly happens?"
Possible answers "Reads fine, just a little slow when tired" "Guesses words, skips lines, reads very slowly" "Can't read Std-level words at all; refuses to read aloud" "I've never actually heard him read"
ProbeAccuracy vs speed vs comprehension? Error types (omission/addition/substitution/reversal/repetition/loss of place)? Better with familiar text?
SignificanceSeparates decoding from comprehension; error pattern guides later error analysis (§35). Preserved listening comprehension with poor reading comprehension points to a reading (decoding) problem.
Documentation"Caregiver reported slow, effortful reading with frequent word substitutions and loss of place; reading accuracy appeared below the expected level for current grade."
Do not over-interpret.Do not treat every letter reversal as proof of dyslexia — some reversals are developmentally normal in early readers. Weigh the whole pattern, persistence, and age.

Ask about

Possible answers "Just a bit untidy" "Spells the same word differently; very slow to copy" "Illegible, laborious; gives up on written answers" "I haven't compared with others"

SignificanceDissociates the components of written expression — crucial because the remedy differs (§33).

Part III — Subject-Wise History

13 · Mathematics History

Separate the levels: basic numerical knowledge → calculation → mathematical reasoning → word problems.

Basic numerical knowledge

  • Recognises numbers? Counts correctly?
  • Understands quantity? Compares numbers?
  • Understands place value & mathematical symbols?

Calculation

  • Recalls number facts / multiplication tables?
  • Addition, subtraction, multiplication, division?
  • Carrying / borrowing? Follows procedures/steps?

Reasoning

  • Estimates quantities?
  • Applies the right operation?

Word (statement) problems

  • Understands what the problem asks?
  • Translates words into operations?
"What happens when your child does maths — basic sums vs word problems?"
Possible answers "Fine with sums, slips in tables" "Knows tables but can't start a word problem" "Confuses + and −, can't do place value or carrying" "Not sure where exactly it breaks down"
ProbeWhich level fails first — number sense, calculation, reasoning, or word problems? Careless vs conceptual errors?
SignificanceLocalises the mathematical difficulty and guides targeted remediation (§34, §49).
Documentation"Caregiver reported adequate recall of number facts but marked difficulty interpreting and solving statement (word) problems."

Part IV — Educational & Developmental

18 · Developmental Regression

"Did your child ever learn a skill and later lose it?"
Differentiate Never acquired · Delayed acquisition · Temporary difficulty (e.g. during illness, then recovered) · Actual regression (true loss of a previously mastered skill).
SignificanceSLD is a difficulty acquiring skills, not losing them. Genuine regression requires further clinical/neurological evaluation — it is a red flag that points away from uncomplicated SLD.
Documentation"No history of loss of previously acquired skills was reported" (negative) vs "Caregiver reported loss of previously acquired reading skill, warranting neurological evaluation."

19 · Medical History

  • Neurological illness; seizures
  • Head injury; loss of consciousness
  • CNS infection; high fever
  • Hearing problems; visual problems
  • Chronic illness; endocrine/metabolic conditions

20 · Family History

Ask about: learning difficulties, reading/academic difficulties, speech/language difficulties, ADHD, autism, intellectual disability, developmental disorders, neurological illness, psychiatric disorders, seizures, and family members who required special education.

Externalising

Internalising

Developmental & other

Part VI — Cognitive & Language

24 · Intellectual Functioning

Intellectual assessment is a required pre-requisite in the SLD certification pathway. Per the Gazette of India document, an intelligence test (MISIC / WISC) is administered before the NIMHANS SLD Index, and the child must obtain an IQ of 85 and above for the SLD pathway.

Instruments named in the source

  • MISIC — Malin's Intelligence Scale for Indian Children (Malin, 1969)
  • WISC-III UK (Indian adaptation); WISC-IV if WISC-III Indian not available
  • WAPIS (Wechsler Adult Performance Intelligence Scale) for older children, since MISIC lacks norms above 15 years

What to look at

  • Overall cognitive functioning (Full-Scale IQ)
  • Verbal vs non-verbal abilities
  • Working memory; processing speed; reasoning
  • Adaptive functioning where relevant
SLD vs Intellectual Disability.Do not diagnose SLD solely because IQ is low or high. SLD requires adequate general intelligence (certification: IQ ≥ 85) with specific academic impairment; ID is a global deficit in intellectual and adaptive functioning. Follow the exact diagnostic framework of the classification system you are using (§46).

Level I subtests — administration & cut-offs

Attention — Colour Cancellation

PurposeAbility to focus/sustain attention on a given task. MaterialsColour cancellation sheet (printed on the back cover of the test book). AdministrationSimple: cancel any one colour within 1 minute. Double: cancel any two colours. Instruction"I am going to show you a sheet which has different coloured dots. I want you to cancel out all the black and yellow colours as fast as you can." Demonstrate if needed. ScoringNo. of correct, errors, time (record form: simple 60 s, double 120 s). ObserveSystematic vs haphazard approach; impulsivity; omissions.

Visual Discrimination

PurposeVisual perception / discrimination of similar forms & words. Instruction"Mark the one that looks the same as the first one." InterpretationThree or more errors suggests difficulty with visual discrimination (children aged 5 or older). QualitativeDifficulty matching shapes/forms; loses place; recognises objects poorly when parts are missing; poorly formed/reversed letters (corroborate with observation & parent report).

Visual Memory

Instruction"I am going to show you a row of pictures. After I cover the pictures, tell me each one exactly as you saw it; try to tell me in the same order." Exposure timeRow 1 & 2 = 2 s · Row 3 = 3 s · Row 4 = 4 s · Row 5 = 5 s. InterpretationAge 5: recalling 3 items in any order within a row = adequate. Age 6+: recalling 4 items in any order within a row = adequate. QualitativeRecognises when shown but cannot recall; remembers parts but in the wrong sequence.

Auditory Discrimination

Set-upChild should not see the examiner's face (prevents lip-reading). Instruction"I am going to tell you some words and you tell me if they are same or different." 20 word-pairs. A second presentation may be made; if the child fails again it counts as an error. InterpretationFive or more errors (age 6 or older) suggests difficulty. QualitativeCannot tell when sounds are the same, or when pitch/intensity changes.

Auditory Memory

Instruction"I am going to read some sentences to you. When I finish, just repeat what I said." (8 sentences; record responses verbatim.) InterpretationAge 5: one or more errors in each of the 8 sentences suggests difficulty. Age 6: can repeat at least 5 sentences without error. QualitativeDifficulty following a sequence of directions; understands/recognises words but difficulty retrieving them; parents report "told yesterday, forgotten today".

Speech & Language — Verbal Expression

InstructionPoint to the picture: "Tell me all you can about this. Tell me what it is, and what it does or what you can do with it." (Record stimuli e.g. Car, Ball, Bucket.) List the number of responses. InterpretationAges 4–6: fewer than 4 total responses (at least one per item) suggests a problem. Age 7+: under 6 responses suggests difficulty. QualitativeDoesn't speak; monosyllables only; articulation problems; can't describe persons/places; doesn't comprehend simple sentences.

Visuo-Motor Skills — Copying Shapes

AdministrationChild copies a design exactly as seen; 3 chances, only the best effort counted. Normative dataSingle circle = age 3 · Square = age 4 · Triangle = 5½–6 yrs · Diamond = 6–7 yrs. InterpretationDifficulty after age 7 indicates a need for a comprehensive visual-motor programme (eye–hand readiness). QualitativePoor in games/clumsy; cannot catch/throw a ball; loses balance; unsteady gait.

Writing Skills · Phonic Skills · Knowledge of Sounds

WritingWrite capital letters, small letters, the letter before/after a given letter, numbers 1–20, and the number before/after a given number. Phonic skillsCheck whether the child can articulate the sound corresponding to each alphabet. Note (source)Additional tests — DIBELS (copyrighted; Dynamic Measurement Group) phoneme segmentation fluency and nonsense-word fluency — may be administered. Children aged 6–7 (Std I) who can read & write simple words → administer SLD Level II (Std-I tests: reading, comprehension, spelling, written expression, copying, single-digit addition & subtraction).

Level I Profile sheet (record form)

The SLD Level-I record form (Dept. of Clinical Psychology, Child & Adolescent Mental Health Unit, NIMHANS; "for 5–7 years") records: Attention (single/double cancellation — correct/errors/time), Visual Discrimination (total correct), Visual Memory (exposure times & figures recalled), Auditory Discrimination (20 items), Auditory Memory (8 verbatim), Visual-Motor Screen, Speech & Language, Alphabets, Phonic skills, and Numbers. 30 · Level II — Older Children (8–16 yrs)

Attention — Number Cancellation

PurposeSustained attention. Instruction"Of the several numbers given below, I want you to cancel all 3s and 4s. Do it as quickly as possible." (If the child finds two difficult, instruct to cancel any one number.) RecordTime taken, number of right numbers cancelled, number of wrong numbers cancelled. ScoreScore = (no. of right numbers cancelled) − (no. of wrong numbers cancelled). ObserveComment on the approach: systematic or haphazard.

Reading — English passages (I–IX grade)

Instruction"I want you to read this passage loudly and clearly." After the child reads (e.g. Std-I paragraph), ask comprehension questions from that standard. If read adequately, progress to the next standard, and so on. Scoring/InterpretationNote the highest standard the child reads adequately and compare to grade placement → feeds the severity logic (§28). Record error types for analysis (§35). Content noteThe graded passages are secure test content — not reproduced here (§61).

Reading Comprehension & Listening Comprehension (I–IX)

Reading comp.Comprehension questions matched to each standard the child read. Listening comp.When reading is severe (so reading comprehension would be unfairly poor), the examiner reads the paragraph aloud and asks the questions orally. CautionDo not rate the severity of reading comprehension if the child has a reading disability (§28).

Spelling — Schonell Graded Spelling Test

ProcedureCan be given to a whole group at once (paper & pencil). On foolscap write: child's name, date, A-or-B form, score. Words given & marked in groups of ten, written in a column (space between groups of 10). Each word given only three times. A child continues until making ten mistakes in succession. Giving each wordSay the word → use it in a sentence so meaning is clear → say the word again → child writes it. InterpretationSpelling level vs grade placement; qualitative error analysis (§33, §35).

Copying & Written Expression (I–IX)

Copying"I want you to copy this passage. Copy as neatly as possible." Ruled sheets provided; time limit 10 minutes. Graded passages by standard (secure content). Written expression"I want you to write an essay / a few sentences on the topic 'My School'. Write all that you know." Time limit 15 minutes. ScoringWriting (copying & written expression) rated on a 0/1 severity scale; qualitative analysis of handwriting, speed, spelling, organisation.

Arithmetic & Mathematics

Arithmetic (diagnostic)Simple & graded addition, subtraction, multiplication, division; addition/subtraction/multiplication/division of fractions. "I want you to carry out the following problems." Time 30–40 min. Mathematics TestStatement (word) sums for VI standard and above: "I want you to carry out the list of statement sums given below." Time 60 min. RuleIf the child expresses inability to carry out, the test can be discontinued. RecordTime taken, the procedure used to solve, examples of mistakes, and total score. (Item lists & answer keys are secure content — §61.)
Standard X note.No norms were collected for Std X in the source; 10 Std-X children read, comprehended & copied 3 paragraphs from their English textbook without mistakes. 54 · Case Vignettes

All four cases are Fictional — for teaching only

Case 1 — Reading Difficulty Fictional

History: A 9-year-old boy, Std IV, English-medium, referred by his teacher for persistent reading difficulty since Std I; private tuition for 2 years with little change. Milestones broadly normal; family history of a maternal uncle who "could never read well". Vision & hearing normal.

Questions asked: onset, trajectory, oral vs written, response to help (see §5, §11). Possible answers: "guesses words, loses place, dreads reading aloud".

Test selection: MISIC first (FSIQ average, ≥85) → NIMHANS Level II: Number Cancellation, graded reading + comprehension, Schonell spelling, copying/written expression, arithmetic, BGT.

Findings: Reading adequate only to Std-II level (≈3 standards below) with substitutions & loss of place; spelling below grade; comprehension of orally-read text intact; maths at grade level; attention adequate.

Interpretation: Severity 1 reading disability; preserved listening comprehension localises the problem to decoding, not comprehension. Differential: ID excluded (IQ ≥ 85), sensory normal, schooling adequate, no ADHD/emotional driver. Formulation: predisposing — family history, phonological vulnerability; perpetuating — repeated failure. Recommendations: structured literacy / phonological-decoding intervention, extra time, reduced reading load, re-assess.

Case 2 — Writing Difficulty Fictional

History: A 10-year-old girl, Std V, with slow, illegible handwriting, inconsistent spelling, and difficulty organising written answers; oral performance good. Fine-motor milestones slightly late.

Test selection: IQ (average) → Level II copying (10 min) & "My School" essay (15 min), Schonell spelling, BGT/DTVMI, reading & maths to exclude broader involvement.

Findings: Copying slow & laboured; spelling inconsistent on dictation but better on copying; essay thin & disorganised; reading & maths at grade level; BGT shows mild visuo-motor immaturity.

Interpretation: Impairment on the writing scale, combining a handwriting (motor) component and a spelling/language-formulation component (§33). Differential: poor handwriting alone ≠ dysgraphia — the spelling & composition components confirm written-expression difficulty. Recommendations: handwriting & spelling intervention, written-expression scaffolds, reduced copying burden, alternative response formats.

Case 3 — Mathematics Difficulty Fictional

History: An 11-year-old boy, Std VI, who "knows tables" but fails calculations and cannot start word problems. Reading & writing reportedly fine.

Test selection: IQ (average) → Level II arithmetic (simple/graded + − × ÷, fractions; 30–40 min) & Mathematics statement sums (60 min); reading & spelling to exclude broader involvement.

Findings: Place-value & carrying/borrowing errors, wrong-operation errors, cannot translate statement problems into operations; reading & spelling at grade level.

Interpretation: Specific mathematics disability, below grade placement, with both calculation and reasoning components. Differential: careless/attention errors excluded by consistency & approach; schooling adequate. Recommendations: concrete-to-abstract teaching, number-line strategies, explicit calculation instruction, step-wise problem solving.

Case 4 — Mixed Difficulty with ADHD / Emotional Factors Fictional

History: A 9-year-old boy with underachievement across subjects, restless and inattentive both at home and at school, increasingly anxious about exams and avoiding homework.

Test selection: IQ → Level II across domains; Number Cancellation; careful cross-setting attention & emotional screen; teacher report.

Findings: Cancellation shows haphazard approach & many omissions; academic errors are careless & inconsistent rather than a stable skill deficit; cross-setting inattention/hyperactivity; situational anxiety.

Interpretation: Academic underachievement appears secondary to pervasive inattention and anxiety rather than a primary SLD; performance may underestimate ability. Why differentiate: ADHD can impair output without a specific skill deficit (§23) — labelling this "SLD" would misdirect remediation. Recommendations: address attention & anxiety first, re-assess academic skills once attention is managed; liaise with school.

55 · Viva / Practical Bank

Each: Question → Model answer → Key point → Common mistake.

What is SLD?
A neurodevelopmental condition of persistent, unexpected difficulty acquiring/using a specific academic skill (reading, writing, maths), not explained by low IQ, sensory/neurological problems, inadequate schooling, language, or emotional factors.Key point: "specific" & "unexpected" — intelligence is adequate.Mistake: equating SLD with general poor marks.
What are the domains of SLD?
Reading, written expression (spelling/handwriting/composition), and mathematics.Key point: a child may be impaired in one or several.Mistake: forgetting written expression as a distinct domain.
What is the NIMHANS SLD battery?
The NIMHANS Index for Specific Learning Disabilities — originally John (1989), compiled by Kapur et al (1991), revised 2019 (publ. 2020). Organised as Level I (5–7 yrs) and Level II (8–16 yrs), covering attention, language/reading/writing/spelling/comprehension, arithmetic/maths, visuo-motor and memory, with Brigance readiness screening.Key point: criterion-measure test used to confirm SLD and plan remediation.Mistake: calling it a norm-referenced test yielding % disability.
Why assess IQ before the Index?
Per the Gazette of India, MISIC/WISC is administered first and the child must obtain IQ ≥ 85 — this excludes intellectual disability and establishes that underachievement is "unexpected".Key point: IQ ≥ 85 is the certification pre-requisite.Mistake: diagnosing SLD without an intelligence test.
How is severity rated on the NIMHANS Index?
Severity 1 = functioning three standards below current standard; Severity 2 = four or more standards below (for reading, reading comprehension, listening comprehension, spelling, arithmetic). Writing is rated 0/1. One–two standards below = learning difficulty.Key point: it's a criterion measure — no % disability from the test.Mistake: rating reading-comprehension severity when reading is impaired.
Why is developmental & school history important?
To establish neurodevelopmental onset, rule out regression/ID/GDD, and confirm that schooling & opportunity were adequate (an exclusion).Key point: history rules in and rules out.Mistake: jumping to tests without history.
Why assess sensory functioning?
Uncorrected vision/hearing deficits can mimic learning difficulty and are an exclusion criterion.Key point: screen vision & hearing before interpreting.Mistake: ignoring sensory status.
What are dyslexia, dysgraphia, dyscalculia?
Lay/clinical terms for SLD with impairment in reading (dyslexia), written expression (dysgraphia), and mathematics (dyscalculia) respectively.Key point: use the classification system's formal specifiers in the report.Mistake: using the "-dys" terms loosely without evidence.
SLD vs Intellectual Disability?
ID = global deficit in intellectual and adaptive functioning (developmental onset); SLD = specific skill deficit with adequate intelligence (IQ ≥ 85).Key point: specificity & IQ threshold.Mistake: diagnosing SLD when IQ is below 85.
How do you differentiate SLD from ADHD?
ADHD = cross-setting attention/impulse problem that impairs output without a specific skill deficit; SLD = task-specific skill deficit. They can co-occur.Key point: setting-specificity and error quality.Mistake: labelling careless, cross-setting underperformance as SLD.
Why are multiple informants & teacher reports required?
To triangulate across settings and corroborate parent report, child report, records and direct assessment.Key point: no single informant decides.Mistake: relying on one distressed parent's account.
Why can low marks alone not establish SLD?
Low marks have many causes (illness, absenteeism, schooling, language, motivation, emotional factors) — SLD requires specific, unexpected impairment after exclusions.Key point: marks are an outcome, not a diagnosis.Mistake: equating report-card dips with SLD.
What is error analysis & the role of qualitative observation?
Systematic classification of error types (omission, substitution, reversal, place-value, procedural, etc.) and session behaviour that supports interpretation and shapes the remedial plan.Key point: errors + behaviour contextualise the scores.Mistake: over-interpreting a single error or behaviour as diagnostic.
How do you give recommendations & counsel parents?
Map interventions to the assessed profile (school accommodations + domain-specific remediation); counsel without blame, explain strengths & weaknesses, set realistic expectations, collaborate with school.Key point: never call the child lazy.Mistake: generic recommendations unmatched to the profile.
