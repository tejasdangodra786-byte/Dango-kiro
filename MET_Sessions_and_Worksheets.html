Part A · How to Use This Guide

This book turns the theory and techniques of Book One into action. Each session lists the techniques used (with their Book-One codes, e.g. A3 = reflective listening, C2 = rulers) so you can see exactly which skill to apply when.

Technique code key (full detail in Book One §9). A = OARS (A1 open Qs, A2 affirmations, A3 reflections, A4 summaries); B = reflections (B1 simple…B5 feeling); C = evoking (C1 evocative Qs, C2 rulers, C3 extremes, C4 look back/forward, C5 values, C6 elaborate, C7 key question, C8 bouquet); D = sustain talk/discord (D1 roll, D2 autonomy, D3 reframe, D4 agree-with-twist, D5 shift, D6 come alongside, D7 repair); E = info (E1 E-P-E, E2 feedback, E3 permission, E4 menu); F = planning/relapse (F1 decisional balance … F13 boosters).

Assessment before MET

A structured assessment precedes MET; its results feed the Session-2 feedback. Cover: present use (type/amount/frequency/route/duration), last use & cravings, triggers, consequences (physical/psychological/family/work/legal), previous attempts, withdrawal & overdose risk, patient's own understanding, readiness/importance/confidence, strengths & supports, barriers, and goals/preferences. Instruments: WHO ASSIST, AUDIT, Fagerström, timeline follow-back, SOCRATES.

⚠ Flag immediately for medical/psychiatric care: suicidal ideation, overdose risk, severe alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal risk (seizure/DT history), pregnancy, acute illness. MET complements — never replaces — medical treatment.

Phase I · Engagement & Assessment Feedback (S1–3)

Session 1 · Engagement & Rapport
Session 1
Techniques used: A1 open questions · A3 reflective listening · A2 affirmations · A4 summaries · D2 autonomy · B2 complex reflection  |  Worksheet: #1

Objectives: establish a trusting, collaborative alliance; orient the patient to MET; understand their world; instil hope. Rationale: engagement predicts retention — nothing works without it. Preparation: private space; review referral; set a non-rushed tone; prepare consent/confidentiality points.

Step-by-step

  1. Warm welcome; introduce MET collaboratively.
  2. Explain confidentiality & its limits.
  3. Open-ended invitation to tell their story (A1).
  4. Listen with OARS; reflect far more than you ask (A3).
  5. Elicit hopes/concerns; affirm attending (A2, D2).
  6. Agree a loose agenda for the sessions ahead.
T: “Thanks for coming in. Before anything else — what's been going on in your life lately, in your own words?”
P: “Honestly, I'm only here because my wife pushed me.”
T (D2 + B2): “You came partly for her — and whatever we do here is still your choice. While you're here, what would make this time worth it for you?”
P: “I suppose I'm tired of the arguments.”

Common mistakes: rushing to forms before rapport; siding with family against the patient; correcting denial; filling silences. Homework: notice one thing about use they'd want different. Documentation: engagement quality, stated reason for attending, early change/sustain talk, agenda, risk screen. Move on when: a working alliance exists & the patient will continue.

Session 2 · Personalised Assessment Feedback
Session 2
Techniques used: E3 ask permission · E2 personalised feedback · E1 Elicit-Provide-Elicit · B3 double-sided reflection · A4 summary · C2 rulers  |  Worksheets: #2, #4, #5, #6

Objectives: deliver structured, personalised assessment feedback in MI style (the “enhancement”); let the patient draw their own conclusions. Rationale: objective feedback given without argument raises motivation while protecting autonomy. Preparation: a one-page feedback sheet (levels vs norms, risk markers, consequences); rulers ready.

Step-by-step (E-P-E)

  1. Ask permission to share results (E3).
  2. Elicit what the patient already knows/expects.
  3. Provide one finding at a time, neutrally; pause (E2).
  4. Reflect their reaction; avoid defending the data (B3).
  5. Summarise; ask “What do you make of all this?” (A4).
T (E2): “Your reported use is in the high-risk range compared with most men your age. How does that land?”
P: “I don't use more than my friends.”
T (B3): “Among your circle this feels normal — and seeing it in the high-risk range is a surprise.”
P: “The liver result did scare me a bit.”

Mistakes: lecturing from the sheet; dumping all findings at once; pushing a goal too early. Homework: reflect on which feedback felt most relevant. Move on when: the patient engages with the feedback and voices some concern.

Session 3 · Developing Discrepancy & Mapping Readiness
Session 3
Techniques used: C2 rulers · C5 values/discrepancy · A3 reflection · C8 summarise change talk · B2 complex reflection  |  Worksheets: #4, #5, #6, #8

Objectives: develop discrepancy between use and the patient's goals/values; map importance, confidence & readiness. Rationale: discrepancy felt in the patient's own terms is the motor of change. Preparation: ruler & “why this number” prompts.

Step-by-step

  1. Review the week & any homework.
  2. Importance ruler → “why that number?” → “why not lower?” (C2).
  3. Confidence ruler the same way (C2).
  4. Reflect/summarise all change talk (C8).
  5. Gently hold up the gap: current use vs stated hopes (C5).
T: “On 0–10, how important is changing right now?” → P: “Maybe a 6.” → T: “Why a 6 and not a 3?” → P: “My health scares me and my wife's at her limit.” → T: “What would move it from a 6 to an 8?”

Mistakes: creating discrepancy by lecturing; treating ruler numbers as diagnoses. Move on to Phase II when: the patient voices meaningful discrepancy and some desire/reason to change.

Phase II · Evoking Motivation (S4–6)

Session 4 · Exploring Ambivalence & Decisional Balance
Session 4
Techniques used: F1 decisional balance · C1 evocative questions · B3 double-sided reflection · D2 autonomy · C7 key question  |  Worksheets: #7, #11

Objectives: explore the real benefits and costs of using and of changing; let the balance tip in the patient's own words. Rationale: honouring perceived benefits first lowers defensiveness. Preparation: decisional-balance worksheet; resist arguing the pro-change side.

Step-by-step

  1. Review week.
  2. Explore “good things / things you'd miss” about use.
  3. Explore “less-good things” about use.
  4. Explore benefits & worries about changing.
  5. Summarise all four quadrants; ask the key question (C7).
T: “What are the good things about using — the things you'd miss?” → P: “It calms me after work.” → T: “And the not-so-good side?” → P: “I'm broke, and we fight about it.” → T (C7): “Given both sides, where does that leave you?”

Mistakes: listing only cons of use; taking the pro-change side (patient then argues for use). Move on when: costs of using and benefits of change feature prominently in the patient's speech.

Session 5 · Personal Values & Developing Discrepancy
Session 5
Techniques used: C5 values/discrepancy · C4 looking back/forward · A3 reflection · C6 elaborate · C8 bouquet  |  Worksheets: #8, #9

Objectives: connect change to core values & life goals; deepen discrepancy; strengthen intrinsic motivation. Rationale: change rooted in values is more durable than change driven by fear or pressure. Preparation: values worksheet; allow silences to work.

Step-by-step

  1. Elicit top values (“What matters most to you?”).
  2. Explore how the patient wants to be seen/remembered (C4).
  3. Explore how use fits — or clashes — with those values (C5).
  4. Reflect the discrepancy; let them name it (A3).
T: “Being a good father is everything to you. How does using fit with the father you want to be?” → P: “It doesn't. That's not who I want to be.” → T: “There's a real gap between how you're living and who you want to be — and you want to close it.”

Mistakes: imposing your values/moralising; rushing past emotion. Move on when: the patient articulates a values-based reason for change.

Session 6 · Building Confidence & Self-Efficacy
Session 6
Techniques used: C2 confidence ruler · F10 supporting self-efficacy · A2 affirmations · C4 looking back · C6 elaborate  |  Worksheets: #5, #9, #12

Objectives: raise confidence that change is possible; address the “I want to but I can't” gap; surface strengths, supports & past successes. Rationale: importance and confidence are independent — high importance + low confidence needs efficacy-building, not more reasons. Preparation: confidence ruler; strengths worksheet.

Step-by-step

  1. Confidence ruler → “why that number?” → “why not lower?” (C2).
  2. Review past successes (any domain) & how they were achieved (C4, F10).
  3. Elicit strengths, skills & supports.
  4. Ask “What would raise your confidence by one point?”.
  5. Affirm; summarise ability change talk (A2).
T: “Confidence 0–10 that you could change if you decided?” → P: “About a 3.” → T: “Why a 3 and not a 0?” → P: “I did stop for three weeks once.” → T (A2+F10): “You've already proven you can — that's a strength. What would nudge the 3 toward a 5?”

Mistakes: hollow reassurance; jumping to planning before confidence is adequate. Move on to Phase III when: importance and confidence are both reasonably present and the patient signals readiness.

Phase III · Planning & Action (S7–9)

Session 7 · Negotiating the Change Plan
Session 7
Techniques used: C7 key question · F2 change-plan negotiation · F3 SMART goals · E4 menu of options · C8 bouquet  |  Worksheets: #14, #15, ★#22, ★#23

Objectives: confirm readiness & the chosen goal; co-create a concrete, patient-authored change plan. Rationale: a plan the patient writes carries commitment; a prescribed plan does not. Preparation: change-plan & goal worksheets; know local treatment options.

Step-by-step

  1. Recap motivation (“Last time you said… where are you now?”).
  2. Clarify the goal in the patient's words (abstinence or reduction).
  3. Offer a menu of options; the patient chooses (E4).
  4. Draft steps: who/what/when (F2, F3).
  5. Elicit commitment language; summarise the plan back (C8).
T (C7): “Given all we've discussed, what do you think you'll do?” → P: “I want to stop the smack. I'll ask the doctor about the medicine.” → T: “What's the very first step this week — the appointment, telling your wife, or changing your evenings?”

Mistakes: writing the plan for the patient; over-ambitious goals. Move on when: a written, patient-owned plan with a concrete first step exists.

Session 8 · Triggers, High-Risk Situations & Coping
Session 8
Techniques used: F4 functional analysis · F5 if-then plans · F6 urge-surfing · F7 play-the-tape · F8 refusal rehearsal  |  Worksheets: #10, #12, #13, #11

Objectives: identify triggers & high-risk situations; build a personalised coping toolkit; begin craving management. Rationale: most lapses occur in predictable high-risk situations; planning for them protects the change. Preparation: triggers & coping worksheets.

Step-by-step

  1. Map triggers (people/places/feelings/times) using a recent episode (F4).
  2. Rank the top high-risk situations.
  3. Brainstorm coping options per trigger; the patient selects.
  4. Rehearse one (refusal, delay, distraction, call support) (F8).
  5. Introduce craving skills: urge-surfing & play-the-tape (F6, F7).
T: “When a craving hits in the evening, what tends to happen?” → P: “I message my old group.” → T (F5): “That message is the risky moment. What could you do in the 10 minutes before you'd normally send it?” → P: “Call my brother or go for a walk.”

Mistakes: generic advice not tied to real triggers; skipping rehearsal. Move on when: the patient has a concrete coping plan for their main triggers.

Session 9 · Social Support & Pharmacotherapy Coordination
Session 9
Techniques used: F11 social support · F12 pharmacotherapy coordination · E1 E-P-E · E4 menu · D2 autonomy  |  Worksheets: #12, #16

Objectives: strengthen the support network; address treatment adherence; coordinate medical & pharmacological care; involve family where helpful. Rationale: social support and appropriate medication (MOUD, naltrexone, NRT) markedly improve outcomes. Preparation: referral pathways; optional family session.

Step-by-step

  1. Map who supports the patient & how (F11).
  2. Discuss medication neutrally via E-P-E; coordinate with the doctor (F12, E1).
  3. Address adherence barriers — cost, transport, stigma, side-effects.
  4. If appropriate, plan a supportive family session.
  5. For opioids: naloxone counselling & overdose-prevention plan.
T (E1): “What do you know about the medicine for opioid cravings? … May I add a little? … Buprenorphine can ease withdrawal so the counselling has room to work. What do you make of that?” → P: “If it helps the withdrawal, I'd try it.”

Mistakes: pushing medication rather than offering via E-P-E; ignoring access barriers. Move on to Phase IV when: supports & treatment are in place and the patient is enacting the plan.

Phase IV · Maintenance & Consolidation (S10–12 + boosters)

Session 10 · Reviewing Early Change & Reinforcing Self-Efficacy
Session 10
Techniques used: A2 affirmations · F10 self-efficacy · A3 reflection · C6 elaborate  |  Worksheet: #17

Objectives: review progress & setbacks without judgement; reinforce self-efficacy; adjust the plan. Rationale: early change is fragile; affirming genuine gains builds durable confidence.

Step-by-step

  1. “What's gone well? What's been hard?”
  2. Affirm concrete successes; attribute to effort (A2).
  3. Explore any slips with curiosity, not blame.
  4. Adjust steps/goals as needed.
T: “Two weeks with just one slip — real progress. What helped most?” → P: “Avoiding the market lane and calling my brother.” → T (A2): “You used your plan at the exact right moment — a skill you can repeat.”

Mistakes: focusing on slips over successes; generic praise.

Session 11 · Relapse Prevention & Learning from Lapses
Session 11
Techniques used: F9 lapse-vs-relapse reframing · F5 if-then plans · F8 rehearsal · A2 affirmations  |  Worksheets: #18, #19

Objectives: strengthen relapse-prevention; distinguish a lapse from a relapse; create an emergency “if-then” plan; de-shame slips. Rationale: treating a lapse as total failure (abstinence-violation effect) is a key relapse driver.

Step-by-step

  1. Identify remaining high-risk situations & early warning signs.
  2. Build “if-then” coping plans for each (F5).
  3. Normalise lapses; separate lapse from relapse (F9).
  4. Create an emergency plan (who to call, what to do).
  5. Rehearse a difficult scenario (F8).
T (F9): “You used once last week and then stopped yourself. That slip doesn't erase three good weeks — what helped you get back on track?” → P: “I called my brother instead of my old friends.”

Mistakes: treating a lapse as failure; vague unrehearsed plans.

Session 12 · Consolidation, Continuing Care & Discharge
Session 12
Techniques used: C8 bouquet/summary · A2 affirmations · F13 booster planning · C5 values  |  Worksheets: #20, #21, ★#22, ★#23

Objectives: consolidate gains; review the whole journey; arrange continuing care & follow-up; close respectfully & hopefully. Rationale: structured continuing care protects against drop-off after active treatment ends.

Step-by-step

  1. Review progress from Session 1 to now; highlight growth (C8).
  2. Surface remaining ambivalence; revisit the plan.
  3. Agree a continuing-care pathway (clinic, group, follow-up dates, family role).
  4. Affirm agency; leave the door open (A2).
  5. Set booster/follow-up schedule (F13).
T: “Twelve weeks ago you were unsure change was even possible. Look at what you've built. What are you proudest of?” → P: “That my daughter trusts me again.”
Booster sessions (F13): brief contacts at 1, 3, 6 & 12 months — review progress → re-rate rulers → re-affirm change talk → update plan → reinforce continuing care. Especially valuable for opioids (relapse-overdose risk) and adolescents.

Adolescents & young adults ADOLESCENT

Same spirit & skills, adapted delivery: autonomy first and loudest; present-focused motivators (money, image, sport, school) over distant health risks; longer engagement, shorter sessions; confidentiality balanced with safeguarding and age-appropriate family involvement.

Worksheet 1 · My Substance-Use Story

Purpose: to build an honest, shared, non-judgemental picture of your substance use so you and your therapist understand the full situation. Best used in: assessment / Session 1.
How to fill this in:
  1. Work through each box slowly — there are no right or wrong answers.
  2. Write what you can; “not sure” or “don't remember” is completely fine.
  3. Think about a typical week, not your best or worst day.
  4. Include tobacco, alcohol and any medicines you use to feel different — not just the “main” drug.
Worked example: Main substance: heroin (“smack”), inhaled. Started at 25, using about 4 years. Typical use: 0.4–0.6 g/day, mostly evenings. Also: 20 cigarettes/day; occasional alcohol at weekends. Last use: this morning. Longest period not using: 3 weeks, after a detox last year. How I take it: foil (chasing), never injected.

Age you first used · how many years you've been using:

Typical amount & how often now (a usual week):

Other substances you use (tobacco, alcohol, medicines, others):

When you last used · the longest you've ever gone without:

Anything about your use you'd like your therapist to understand:

Clinician review: reflect patterns and affirm honesty; note route, injecting and overdose markers for safety. If use seems under-reported, don't challenge — ask “roughly, on a heavy day?” and revisit once trust grows.

Worksheet 2 · How Substance Use Affects My Life

Purpose: to notice, across every area of life, how substance use is affecting you — the good and the costly. This often surfaces reasons to change in your own words. Best used in: Session 2.
How to fill this in:
  1. Go area by area. For each, write any effect you've noticed — even small ones.
  2. Be honest about helpful effects too; this isn't about judging yourself.
  3. If you can't think of an effect in an area, imagine what someone close to you might write there.
Worked example (one row): Family/relationships — “My wife and I argue about money most nights; my daughter asked why I'm always tired.”

Worksheet 3 · My Reasons for Considering Change

Purpose: to gather your own reasons for thinking about change — the motivators that will matter most to you. Best used in: Sessions 1–3.
How to fill this in:

  2. Write in your own words; these are your reasons, not anyone else's.
  3. Then rank your top three by circling or numbering them.

Worksheet 4 · Importance Ruler

Purpose: to measure, in your own view, how important it feels to change your substance use right now. Best used in: Sessions 2–3 and whenever motivation is being reviewed.
How to fill this in:
  1. Read the question, then circle the number that fits you today (0 = not important at all, 10 = the most important thing in my life right now).
  2. Answer the two questions below the ruler — these are the important part.
  3. There is no “correct” number; it can change week to week.

“How important is it to you to change your substance use right now?”

012345678910
Worked example: circled 6. “Why a 6 and not a 3? Because my health scares me and my wife is at her limit.” “What would move it to an 8? If the money problems got worse, or if my daughter said something again.”

Worksheet 5 · Confidence Ruler

Purpose: to measure how confident you feel that you could change if you decided to. Importance and confidence are different — you can badly want change yet doubt you can do it. Best used in: Session 6 and throughout.
How to fill this in:
  1. Circle the number for how confident you feel today (0 = not at all confident, 10 = completely confident).
  2. Answer the questions below — especially what would raise your confidence.
  3. Low confidence is normal and workable; it tells us where to build skills and support.

“If you decided to change, how confident are you that you could?”

Worksheet 6 · Why This Number? / Why Not Lower?

Purpose: to draw out, in detail, the thinking behind your ruler scores — because the reasons you give are exactly the motivation that drives change. Best used in: straight after the rulers (Worksheets 4 & 5).
How to fill this in:
  1. Write your importance number and your confidence number at the top.
  2. For each, explain why it isn't lower — list every reason you can.
  3. Then write what a higher number would look like in real life.
Worked example: Importance 6 — “A 3 would mean I don't care, but I do, especially about my daughter and my health.” A higher number would look like “me actually booking the clinic and telling my wife”.

Worksheet 7 · Decisional Balance (Four-Quadrant)

Purpose: to weigh the full picture — the pros and cons of continuing use and of changing — so you can see where the balance really sits for you. Best used in: Session 4 (contemplation).
How to fill this in:

Purpose: to understand a slip without shame, and turn it into useful learning — so one lapse doesn't become a full relapse. Best used in: after any lapse.

  1. Remember: a lapse is a single slip; it does not erase your progress or mean failure.

★ Worksheet 22 · My SHORT-TERM Goals (MET)

Purpose: to set small, clear, achievable goals for the next 1–4 weeks that build momentum and early success toward your bigger aims. In MET, short-term goals turn motivation into movement. Best used in: Session 7 onward, alongside the Change Plan (Worksheet 14).
How to fill this in (step by step):
  1. Pick 2–4 goals you can realistically reach soon — stepping stones, not the whole mountain.
  2. Make each goal SMART: Specific (exactly what), Measurable (how you'll know), Achievable (within reach), Relevant (matters to you), Time-bound (by when).
  3. For each, write how you'll measure it and the first support/step.
  4. Rate your confidence (0–10) for each goal. If a goal is under 7, make it smaller so you're likely to succeed.
  5. Remember: aiming to reduce or stabilise is a valid short-term goal if that's where you are — it doesn't have to be full abstinence yet.

★ Worksheet 23 · My LONG-TERM Goals (MET)

Purpose: to connect your day-to-day efforts to the bigger life you want (3 months to a year or more) — the deeper “why” that keeps you going when motivation dips. In MET, long-term goals give short-term goals their meaning. Best used in: Sessions 7 & 12 and continuing care; revisit regularly.
How to fill this in (step by step):
  1. Picture yourself 3, 6 and 12 months from now. What do you want your life to look like — not just your substance use, but relationships, work, health, self-respect?
  2. Write each long-term goal, with a rough timeframe.
  3. For each, write why it matters — the value behind it (link to Worksheet 8). This is the engine of your motivation.
  4. List the short-term steps (from Worksheet 22) that build toward each long-term goal, so the path is visible.
  5. Finish the single sentence at the bottom — your overall vision.
