What the F10–F19 disorders are — and what “cause” means here

F10–F19 covers the mental and behavioural disorders caused by psychoactive substances (chemicals that change how the brain works — alcohol, opioids, cannabis, stimulants, etc.). ICD-10 codes the substance in the middle digit (F1x) and the clinical picture in the last digit (.x): acute intoxication (.0), harmful use (.1), dependence syndrome (.2), withdrawal state (.3), withdrawal with delirium (.4), psychotic disorder (.5), amnesic syndrome (.6), and more.

“Cause” here is more layered than for a simple infection. Everyone who takes a drug does not become addicted, so the models must explain three linked questions:

  • Why is the substance rewarding at all? (the pharmacology — how it hijacks the brain's reward system).
  • Why do some people become dependent while others don't? (the vulnerability — genes, psychology, environment).
  • Why is it so hard to stop, and why do people relapse? (the neuroadaptation — how the brain changes with repeated use).
Key terms in plain language. Dependence / addiction = a compulsive pattern of use with loss of control, despite harm; tolerance = needing more of the drug to get the same effect (the brain adapts); withdrawal = unpleasant symptoms when the drug is stopped (the adapted brain rebounds); craving = an intense urge to use; reinforcement = anything that makes a behaviour more likely to be repeated (drugs are powerful reinforcers); neuroadaptation = long-lasting changes the brain makes in response to repeated drug exposure.

Plain-language promise & scope. Difficult terms are explained in brackets in the sentence. This is an educational synthesis of research for study — not medical, diagnostic or treatment advice. It focuses on etiology/models; ICD classification detail lives in the separate F10–F19 handbook.

The biopsychosocial frame — addiction is never one thing

No single cause explains addiction. The mainstream view (Kaplan & Sadock; Frontiers integrative review 2026 latest) is that it emerges from the interaction of biological, psychological and social factors — the biopsychosocial model. The drug supplies the pharmacology; the person and their world decide who gets trapped.

BIOLOGICAL
  • Drug's action on the reward pathway (dopamine)
  • Genes (heritability ~50%)
  • Drug-metabolising enzymes (how fast you break it down)
  • Neuroadaptation, tolerance, withdrawal
  • Co-existing mental illness (brain vulnerability)
  • Adolescent brain still developing
PSYCHOLOGICAL
  • Learning & conditioning (cues trigger craving)
  • Positive & negative reinforcement
  • “Self-medication” of distress
  • Personality (impulsivity, sensation-seeking)
  • Expectancies & beliefs about the drug
  • Trauma, depression, anxiety
SOCIAL
  • Availability & price of the drug
  • Peer group & family use
  • Poverty, stress, disadvantage
  • Culture & advertising
  • Childhood adversity / abuse
  • Law, policy & stigma

The one-line takeaway

Genes and the drug load the gun; psychology and environment pull the trigger; neuroadaptation welds the finger to it. Addiction is a developmental, biopsychosocial process, not a single event or a simple moral failing.

The brain reward pathway — the common target of every drug

latest The Neuroscience of Drug Reward and Addiction PMC 2019; NIDA reviews

Almost all addictive drugs converge on one circuit: the mesolimbic dopamine pathway (the brain's “reward highway”), running from the ventral tegmental area (VTA) — a dopamine-making hub in the midbrain — to the nucleus accumbens (the “pleasure/motivation centre”), with connections to the prefrontal cortex (the “boss” that plans and controls impulses) and the amygdala/hippocampus (emotion and memory).

  • Why drugs are so powerful: normal rewards (food, company) raise dopamine modestly and briefly. Addictive drugs cause a much larger, faster dopamine surge — the brain's learning system reads this as “hugely important, repeat it”, stamping in drug-seeking.
  • Different drugs, same finish: they reach the dopamine surge by different routes — cocaine/stimulants block dopamine reuptake (so it lingers), opioids and alcohol disinhibit VTA dopamine neurons, nicotine directly stimulates them — but the reward endpoint is shared.
  • Dopamine is about “wanting”, not just “liking”: a crucial modern refinement (see incentive-sensitization, below).

The three-stage addiction cycle (Koob & Volkow) — the master model

latest Koob & Volkow, Neurobiology of addiction: a neurocircuitry analysis, PMC/Lancet Psychiatry; review PMC 2022–2024

The dominant modern framework describes addiction as a vicious cycle of three stages, each driven by a different brain circuit, that spirals downward with repeated use. This one model ties together reward, withdrawal and relapse.

1Binge / intoxication — the drug floods the basal ganglia (reward area) with dopamine; the “high” teaches the brain to seek the drug (positive reinforcement — using because it feels good).
2Withdrawal / negative affect — when the drug wears off, the extended amygdala (stress circuit) drives a crash: anxiety, low mood, irritability. Now the person uses to escape feeling bad (negative reinforcement).
3Preoccupation / anticipation (craving) — the prefrontal cortex (self-control) weakens while cues trigger intense craving via glutamate; planning and willpower fail, leading back to stage 1.
Tolerance & the “reward deficit”. With repeated use the brain turns down its own dopamine system to compensate (down-regulation). So the person needs more drug for the same effect (tolerance), and normal pleasures feel flat (anhedonia — inability to feel pleasure) — a “reward deficit” that fuels continued use just to feel normal. This is the biological engine of dependence.

Remember — the cycle “B-W-C”

Binge (dopamine reward, basal ganglia) → Withdrawal (stress/negative affect, extended amygdala) → Craving (loss of control, prefrontal cortex) → back to B. Use shifts from “feel good” → “avoid feeling bad”.

Remember the brain rule

“Dopamine wants it, glutamate remembers & craves it, GABA/opioids calm it, the amygdala stresses when it's gone, and the prefrontal cortex loses the brakes.”

Genetic models — why vulnerability runs in families

latest Genetics of substance use disorders PMC (h²≈50%); Genetic landscape of SUDs 2024; Genetics of Alcohol Use Disorder PMC

Twin and family studies consistently show substance use disorders are about 50% heritable (roughly half the variation in who becomes dependent is explained by inherited genes) — comparable to many other psychiatric disorders. But there is no single “addiction gene”: it is polygenic (many genes, each of small effect, adding up) and interacts strongly with environment.

Genetic mechanismWhat it does (plain)
Drug-metabolising enzyme genes — strongest, best-provenADH1B and ALDH2 control how the body breaks down alcohol. The common East-Asian ALDH2*2 variant causes a build-up of acetaldehyde (a toxic by-product) → flushing, nausea — a protective variant that lowers alcoholism risk. A powerful example of genes shaping risk. latest
Neurotransmitter-system genes (e.g. GABRA2, dopamine & opioid receptor genes)Small variations in reward/inhibition chemistry alter how rewarding or calming a drug feels, and how strong the urge is.
Polygenic risk & shared “externalizing” geneticsMany small-effect variants combine; large parts overlap with impulsivity and sensation-seeking and with a general “externalizing” (acting-out) tendency — a shared vulnerability across substances (Nature 2023–2024 latest).
Nicotine metabolism (CYP2A6) & nicotinic receptor genes (CHRNA5)Affect how fast nicotine is cleared and how strongly it acts — influencing who becomes a heavy smoker.
Gene × environment (the GINA model)Genes act through the environment: the same risk genes matter more with early exposure, stress or availability. Epigenetics (drugs/stress switching genes on/off without changing DNA) links the two (Frontiers 2026 latest).

The genetics takeaway

You do not inherit “addiction” — you inherit a degree of vulnerability (how rewarding a drug feels, how impulsive you are, how you metabolise it). Whether that vulnerability becomes disorder depends heavily on exposure, age of first use, stress and environment.

Psychological models — learning, wanting, coping

These explain how repeated use turns into compulsive use at the level of the mind and behaviour — the models that most directly guide psychological treatment (Kaplan & Sadock; Oxford; Neeraj Ahuja).

1. Positive & negative reinforcement (operant conditioning) STRONG

classic Skinner / behavioural learning theory

Positive reinforcement = the drug produces pleasure, so the behaviour is repeated (using to feel good). Negative reinforcement = the drug removes something unpleasant — withdrawal, anxiety, pain — so it is repeated (using to stop feeling bad). As dependence deepens, use shifts from positive to negative reinforcement (exactly matching stages 1→2 of the brain cycle).

2. Classical conditioning & cue-reactivity STRONG

classic Pavlov · latest cue-reactivity research

Neutral cues repeatedly paired with the drug (a pub, a needle, a certain street, a mood) become conditioned triggers that by themselves fire dopamine and produce craving and withdrawal-like feelings — explaining why relapse happens on returning to old places/people. Basis of cue-exposure therapy and relapse-prevention.

3. Incentive-sensitization: “wanting” ≠ “liking” STRONG / influential

classic-modern Robinson & Berridge 1993

A landmark model: with repeated use the brain's dopamine system becomes sensitized (hyper-reactive) to the drug and its cues, producing intense “wanting” (craving) — even as the actual “liking” (pleasure) fades. This explains the tragic gap where addicts crave a drug they no longer even enjoy.

4. Self-medication hypothesis MODERATE

classic Khantzian 1985

People use substances to relieve painful emotional states — anxiety, depression, trauma, low self-esteem — choosing the drug whose effect best soothes their particular distress (e.g. alcohol/opioids for anxiety and pain, stimulants for low energy/depression). Explains the high overlap of addiction with other mental illness (“dual diagnosis”).

5. Expectancy & social-learning theory MODERATE

classic Bandura; Marlatt

What a person expects a drug to do (“it will relax me / make me confident”) strongly shapes their use, and behaviour is learned by watching others (family, peers, media). Underpins the importance of beliefs, modelling and self-efficacy in both starting and stopping.

6. Personality & impulsivity model MODERATE

High impulsivity (acting without thinking) and sensation-seeking (craving novel, intense experiences) — partly heritable temperament traits — predict earlier and heavier use and poorer control. This links personality directly to the weakened-prefrontal brain model.

Social models — the world around the person

  • Availability & economics: the single biggest environmental driver — where a drug is cheap, legal and easy to get, use and dependence rise (e.g. alcohol pricing, tobacco taxation, opioid over-prescription and the opioid epidemic).
  • Family & peer influence: substance use in the family (modelling + genetics + environment) and a using peer group are strong predictors, especially in adolescence.
  • Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs): abuse, neglect and household dysfunction sharply raise later addiction risk — a dose-response relationship — likely via chronic stress, altered brain development and self-medication.
  • Poverty, disadvantage & “deaths of despair”: economic hardship, unemployment and lack of opportunity increase both use and harm.
  • Culture, advertising & norms: shape which substances are used, how, and how acceptably.
  • Stigma & policy: stigma and punitive responses can worsen outcomes by blocking help-seeking; supportive, health-based policy improves them.
Social × biological loop: social adversity is not “just environment” — chronic stress and trauma physically change the stress and reward circuits (and even gene expression via epigenetics), so the social world becomes written into the biology.

The big debate: “brain disease” vs learning/choice models

latest Conflicting theories on addiction aetiology PMC 2023; Neurobiologic Advances from the Brain Disease Model PMC

Brain-disease model of addiction (NIDA / Volkow) DOMINANT

Addiction is a chronic, relapsing brain disorder: repeated drug use produces lasting neuroadaptations in reward, stress and control circuits, so compulsive use is driven by a changed brain, not weak willpower. Reduces stigma; supports medical treatment (medications, relapse as part of a chronic illness).

Learning / choice / behavioural-economic models CONTESTING

Critics argue the brain-disease view can over-medicalise and understate the role of learning, environment, incentives and choice (people often reduce use when incentives change — e.g. jobs, family, contingency rewards). They stress addiction as an extreme of normal learning and decision-making shaped by context.

The balanced modern position

These are not either/or. The best current view is integrative: real neurobiological changes are true and learning, environment and choice remain powerful — which is exactly why effective treatment combines medication + psychological therapy + social change (Frontiers integrative review 2026 latest).

F10 · Alcohol — models of causation

How it works (pharmacology): alcohol is a CNS depressant (it slows brain activity). It boosts GABA (the brain's main calming messenger → relaxation, sedation) and blocks glutamate/NMDA receptors (the main activating messenger → memory impairment, “blackouts”), while raising dopamine and the brain's own opioids in the reward pathway (→ the pleasant, reinforcing “buzz”).

  • Neuroadaptation & withdrawal (dangerous): with chronic use the brain turns down GABA and turns up glutamate to compensate. Stopping suddenly leaves an over-excited brain → tremor, sweating, seizures and, at worst, delirium tremens (F10.4 — a life-threatening confusional state with hallucinations and autonomic storm). Alcohol withdrawal is a medical emergency.
  • Genetic model: ~50% heritable; the clearest genes are the alcohol-metabolising enzymes ADH1B and ALDH2 — the East-Asian ALDH2*2 variant is protective (causes unpleasant flushing). GABRA2 and reward-system genes add risk. latest
  • Nutritional/organic consequence: heavy use + poor diet → thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency → Wernicke-Korsakoff (F10.6 amnesic syndrome) — linking to the F00–F09 memory-circuit model.
  • Psychological/social: classic self-medication of anxiety; heavily shaped by availability, price and cultural norms.

Remember F10

“GABA up, glutamate down → buzz; chronic use flips it → dangerous withdrawal (delirium tremens); ALDH2 gene protects; thiamine lack → Korsakoff.”

F11 · Opioids (heroin, morphine, prescription opioids) — models

How it works: opioids directly stimulate the brain's mu-opioid receptors (the docking sites for the body's own painkiller/pleasure chemicals, endorphins), producing intense pain relief, calm and euphoria, and driving dopamine in the reward pathway. They are among the most powerfully reinforcing drugs.

  • Neuroadaptation: rapid, strong tolerance (needing ever-higher doses) and physical dependence; withdrawal (F11.3) is intensely unpleasant (flu-like: aches, sweating, diarrhoea, agitation) though rarely fatal in itself — but the drive to escape it powerfully sustains use (negative reinforcement).
  • Overdose mechanism: opioids suppress the brainstem breathing centre → respiratory depression is the cause of death; reversed by naloxone.
  • Social driver: the modern opioid epidemic is a textbook case of availability as cause — over-prescription seeded widespread dependence, then a shift to heroin/fentanyl.
  • Genetic/psychological: opioid-receptor gene variants (OPRM1) modulate response; strong self-medication of pain (physical and emotional) and trauma.

Remember F11

“Hijacks the body's own endorphin system → euphoria + huge reinforcement; fast tolerance; withdrawal drives use; overdose kills by stopping breathing.”

F12 · Cannabis (cannabinoids) — models

How it works: THC (the main active chemical) acts on the brain's cannabinoid (CB1) receptors — part of the natural endocannabinoid system that fine-tunes mood, appetite, memory and reward — indirectly raising dopamine. Effects: relaxation, altered perception, but also anxiety and, in vulnerable people, psychosis.

  • Dependence: once doubted, now well-established — a real withdrawal syndrome (irritability, sleep and appetite disturbance) and dependence occur, especially with high-potency products and early, heavy use.
  • Cannabis & psychosis (key etiological link): heavy, early, high-potency use is a risk factor for psychotic disorders (F12.5), especially in the genetically vulnerable — a major gene×environment example connecting F12 to the F20–F29 (schizophrenia) models.
  • Adolescent-brain vulnerability: the developing teenage brain is more susceptible to lasting effects on memory and motivation.
  • Psychosocial: availability, peer use, self-medication of anxiety/insomnia.

Remember F12

“Acts on the endocannabinoid (CB1) system; real dependence & withdrawal; heavy early high-potency use raises psychosis risk in the vulnerable.”

F13 · Sedatives & hypnotics (benzodiazepines, barbiturates, “Z-drugs”) — models

How it works: like alcohol, these boost GABA (the calming messenger) → anxiety relief, sedation, sleep. Because the mechanism overlaps with alcohol, they show cross-tolerance (tolerance to one confers tolerance to the other) and are used to treat alcohol withdrawal.

  • Iatrogenic (treatment-caused) dependence: a defining feature — dependence commonly develops from prescribed use for anxiety/insomnia, even at therapeutic doses, especially with long-term use. A key medical/social cause.
  • Dangerous withdrawal: like alcohol, sudden stopping after dependence can cause seizures and delirium (F13.4) — must be tapered slowly. Barbiturates are especially dangerous (narrow safety margin).
  • High overdose risk when combined with alcohol or opioids (additive breathing suppression).
  • Psychological: strong negative-reinforcement/self-medication pattern (using to switch off anxiety), with rebound anxiety/insomnia perpetuating use.

Remember F13

“GABA-boosters like alcohol; often prescription-caused dependence; withdrawal can seize/delirium — taper slowly; deadly mixed with opioids/alcohol.”

F14 · Cocaine — models

How it works: cocaine blocks the reuptake (re-absorption) of dopamine (and noradrenaline and serotonin) at the synapse — so dopamine floods and lingers in the reward pathway, producing an intense but short euphoria, energy and confidence. The short high drives repeated “bingeing”.

  • Crash & craving: after the binge the depleted dopamine system produces a severe “crash” (exhaustion, depression, intense craving) — a vivid example of the withdrawal/negative-affect stage driving relapse.
  • No major physical withdrawal, but powerful psychological dependence: shows that dependence is about brain reward learning and craving, not just physical withdrawal.
  • Medical/psychiatric harms: cardiovascular (heart attack, stroke from vasoconstriction), and cocaine-induced psychosis (F14.5) with paranoia and formication (“coke bugs” — feeling of insects on the skin).
  • Incentive-sensitization is especially clear here: escalating “wanting” despite diminishing pleasure.

Remember F14

“Blocks dopamine reuptake → dopamine floods → brief intense high → crash & craving; strong psychological dependence; risk of psychosis & heart/brain damage.”

F15 · Other stimulants (amphetamines, methamphetamine, MDMA, caffeine) — models

How it works: amphetamines force the release of dopamine and noradrenaline and block their reuptake — a bigger, longer dopamine surge than cocaine, giving prolonged euphoria, energy, wakefulness and reduced appetite. MDMA also floods serotonin (empathy/warmth effects). Caffeine (a milder stimulant) blocks adenosine (a sleep-promoting chemical).

  • Neurotoxicity: heavy methamphetamine and MDMA use can damage dopamine and serotonin neurons — a lasting biological injury contributing to depression and cognitive problems.
  • Stimulant psychosis (F15.5): prolonged high-dose use commonly causes paranoid psychosis resembling schizophrenia — direct evidence that excess dopamine can produce psychotic symptoms (linking to the dopamine hypothesis of F20–F29).
  • Crash & craving pattern like cocaine; strong psychological dependence.
  • Genetic/psychological: reward and impulsivity genetics; self-medication of fatigue, low mood, ADHD; performance/appetite motives socially driven.

Remember F15

“Release dopamine/noradrenaline → big surge, wakefulness; heavy use is neurotoxic & causes paranoid psychosis.”

F16 · Hallucinogens (LSD, psilocybin, ketamine, PCP) — models

How it works: classic hallucinogens (LSD, psilocybin) act mainly on serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, profoundly altering perception, thought and mood. Dissociatives (ketamine, PCP) block NMDA/glutamate receptors, producing detachment and dissociation.

  • Distinctive dependence pattern: classic hallucinogens cause little physical dependence or withdrawal and rapid tolerance — the reward-pathway/dopamine model applies less strongly here, which is itself informative about how varied these disorders are.
  • Main harms are psychological: “bad trips” (F16.0 with acute anxiety/panic), hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (“flashbacks”), and precipitation of psychosis (F16.5) in the vulnerable.
  • PCP/ketamine can cause agitation, dissociative states and dependence; ketamine has notable urinary and cognitive harms with heavy use.

Remember F16

“Serotonin (LSD/psilocybin) or NMDA-blocking (ketamine/PCP); little physical dependence; harms mainly psychological — bad trips, flashbacks, psychosis.”

F17 · Tobacco / nicotine — models

How it works: nicotine directly stimulates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on dopamine neurons → a fast, reliable dopamine reward, plus improved attention and mood — delivered by inhalation in seconds, making it extremely reinforcing and habit-forming.

  • Powerful conditioning: because each cigarette pairs the cue (routine, coffee, stress) with a rapid reward many times a day, tobacco builds some of the strongest conditioned habits of any drug — a prime example of the learning models.
  • Withdrawal: irritability, anxiety, poor concentration, craving — drives the negative-reinforcement cycle.
  • Genetics: CHRNA5 (nicotinic receptor) and CYP2A6 (nicotine metabolism) genes shape how heavily people smoke and how hard it is to quit. latest
  • Social: historically driven by advertising, price and norms; the archetype of a socially/commercially shaped epidemic.

Remember F17

“Nicotine → fast dopamine reward + attention; ultra-strong conditioning (many daily pairings); genes (CHRNA5/CYP2A6) shape heaviness of use.”

F18 · Volatile solvents (inhalants: glue, petrol, aerosols) — models

How it works: inhaled solvents are CNS depressants that act broadly (partly GABA/NMDA effects) — giving a rapid, brief intoxication (euphoria, disinhibition, dizziness). They are cheap and legal, which is central to their use.

  • Who & why (social model): use is concentrated in young adolescents and deprived/marginalised groups precisely because inhalants are cheap, legal and easily available — a stark demonstration of the availability + social-disadvantage model.
  • Severe organic harm: solvents are directly neurotoxic (lasting brain, and also liver/kidney, damage) and can cause “sudden sniffing death” (fatal heart arrhythmia) even on a first use.
  • Psychological: self-medication of distress, peer influence, and easy escape in difficult circumstances.

Remember F18

“Cheap, legal, available → used by deprived youth; broad CNS depression; directly neurotoxic; risk of sudden death.”

F19 · Multiple drug use & other psychoactive substances — models

What it covers: use of several substances together or in a chaotic pattern, where no single drug dominates — the commonest real-world picture. The models above all apply, but with extra features:

  • Shared vulnerability: the polygenic “externalizing” genetics and impulsivity that raise risk for one substance raise it for many — explaining why polydrug use clusters.
  • Interacting pharmacology: combinations can be more dangerous (e.g. opioids + benzodiazepines + alcohol → multiplied breathing suppression and overdose).
  • Complex withdrawal & treatment: overlapping intoxication/withdrawal states are harder to read and manage.
  • Strong psychosocial loading: polydrug patterns are especially tied to severe disadvantage, trauma and dual diagnosis (co-existing mental illness).

Remember F19

“Shared genetic/impulsivity vulnerability → many drugs at once; dangerous interactions; heavy psychosocial & dual-diagnosis loading.”

Three unifying messages

  • 1. One highway, many on-ramps. Nearly all drugs converge on the dopamine reward pathway, but reach it by different pharmacology — and a few (hallucinogens) barely use it, reminding us these disorders are not identical.
  • 2. The cycle explains the trap. Koob & Volkow's binge → withdrawal → craving cycle, with tolerance and a “reward deficit”, explains why use shifts from pleasure to compulsion and why relapse is so common.
  • 3. Half genes, half world — and they interact. ~50% heritable vulnerability (mostly impulsivity, reward and metabolism) meets availability, stress, trauma and learning. Effective help therefore targets brain + mind + environment together.
