The F30–F39 Block — Overview

The ICD-10 block F30–F39 is titled "Mood [affective] disorders". These are disorders in which the fundamental disturbance is a change in affect (the outward, moment-to-moment expression of emotion) or mood (the sustained, prevailing emotional state that colours the whole of mental life) — usually to depression (with or without associated anxiety) or to elation. The mood change is normally accompanied by a change in the overall level of activity, and most of the other symptoms are secondary to, or easily understood in the context of, this change. Most of these disorders tend to be recurrent, and the onset of individual episodes is often related to stressful events or situations. ICD-10

In ICD-11, the corresponding grouping is "Mood disorders", divided into bipolar or related disorders (6A60–6A62) and depressive disorders (6A70–6A73). ICD-11 organises mood disorders around defined mood episodes (depressive, manic, mixed, hypomanic) which are the building blocks for the disorder diagnoses. ICD-11

The core clinical dichotomy of the whole block: is the patient's history unipolar (only depressive episodes ever) or bipolar (at least one manic, hypomanic, or mixed episode at some point)? A single lifetime manic/hypomanic episode moves the diagnosis from the depressive to the bipolar side, which changes both prognosis and treatment.

The Building Blocks — Mood Episodes

Before the disorders, learn the three episode types and the "somatic syndrome" — the disorders are then assembled from these.

Depressive episode — core and common symptoms ICD-10

Three typical (core) symptoms: (1) depressed mood; (2) loss of interest and enjoyment (anhedonia — inability to feel pleasure); (3) reduced energy leading to increased fatiguability and diminished activity.

Other common symptoms: (a) reduced concentration and attention; (b) reduced self-esteem and self-confidence; (c) ideas of guilt and unworthiness; (d) bleak and pessimistic views of the future; (e) ideas or acts of self-harm or suicide; (f) disturbed sleep; (g) diminished appetite.

The lowered mood varies little from day to day, is often unresponsive to circumstances, and may show characteristic diurnal variation (a regular daily pattern — classically worse in the morning).

Manic / hypomanic episode — core symptoms ICD-10

Mania: elevated, expansive, or irritable mood, out of keeping with circumstances, with increased energy and overactivity, pressure of speech, decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, distractibility, reckless behaviour, and loss of normal social inhibitions. In severe mania, psychotic symptoms (grandiose or persecutory delusions) may occur.

Hypomania (a lesser degree of mania — persistent mild elevation of mood, energy and activity, without psychotic features and without severe disruption of work or social life) is a milder form.

The "somatic syndrome" (ICD-10) — the biological/melancholic features ICD-10

A cluster used to specify depressive episodes as "with somatic syndrome": marked loss of interest/pleasure; lack of emotional reactivity to normally pleasurable surroundings; waking two or more hours early (early-morning wakening); depression worse in the morning; objective psychomotor retardation or agitation; marked loss of appetite; weight loss (5% or more in a month); and marked loss of libido. Four or more of these define the somatic syndrome. (These correspond broadly to what ICD-11 and clinicians call the "melancholic" pattern.)

Mnemonic for depressive symptoms — "SIG E CAPS + core mood"

Sleep disturbance · Interest lost (anhedonia) · Guilt/worthlessness · Energy reduced · Concentration reduced · Appetite/weight change · Psychomotor change · Suicidal ideas — on a background of depressed mood. For mania — "DIG FAST": Distractibility, Irresponsibility/disinhibition, Grandiosity, Flight of ideas, Activity/energy up, Sleep need reduced, Talkativeness (pressured speech).

Etiology of Mood Disorders (Comprehensive)

Mood disorders are best understood through a biopsychosocial model (the view that biological, psychological, and social factors interact to cause the disorder), often expressed as a diathesis–stress model (an inherited or acquired vulnerability, the "diathesis", is activated by life stress to produce the illness). The biological factors are covered first, then the psychological theories (which are especially important for depression and for psychological treatment). Kaplan & Sadock

A. Biological factors

FactorDetail
GeneticMood disorders run strongly in families. Heritability is higher for bipolar disorder (around 60–85%) than for unipolar depression (around 35–40%). First-degree relatives have markedly increased risk; twin studies show much higher concordance in identical than in non-identical twins. Inheritance is polygenic (many genes each of small effect).
Monoamine hypothesis (the classic biochemical theory of depression)Depression is associated with a functional deficiency of monoamine neurotransmitters — serotonin (5-HT), noradrenaline, and dopamine — at key brain synapses; mania is associated with a relative excess of catecholamine activity. This underpins the action of most antidepressants (which raise monoamine availability).
NeuroendocrineOveractivity of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis (the body's stress-hormone system) with raised cortisol and non-suppression on the dexamethasone suppression test; thyroid dysfunction can mimic or precipitate mood episodes.
Neuroplasticity / neurotrophicReduced brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (a protein that supports the growth and survival of neurons) and reduced hippocampal volume with chronic stress; antidepressants may act partly by restoring neuroplasticity.
Circadian & sleepDisrupted sleep architecture (reduced REM latency) and circadian rhythm disturbance; seasonal patterns in some depressions.

B. Psychological theories (highly relevant — especially for depression)

1. Beck's cognitive theory

Aaron Beck proposed that depression is maintained by systematic negative thinking. Its components: the cognitive triad (persistent negative views of the self, the world, and the future — "I am worthless, the world is harsh, the future is hopeless"); negative schemas (deep-seated, dysfunctional core beliefs, often laid down in childhood, that shape how information is interpreted); and cognitive distortions / errors (systematic biases in thinking, such as overgeneralisation, catastrophising, selective abstraction, and all-or-nothing thinking). This theory is the direct basis of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT).

2. Learned helplessness and the reformulated hopelessness theory

Learned helplessness (Seligman's theory that after repeated exposure to uncontrollable aversive events, a person learns that their actions have no effect and gives up trying) models depression as a state of perceived uncontrollability. The reformulated (attributional) model adds that vulnerability depends on the person's explanatory style (the habitual way of explaining bad events) — a depressive style attributes negative events to internal, stable, and global causes ("it's my fault, it always happens, it affects everything"). The later hopelessness theory (Abramson, Metalsky & Alloy) holds that the expectation that desired outcomes will not occur, and that nothing can change this, is a proximal cause of a "hopelessness depression".

3. Psychodynamic theories

Freud (in Mourning and Melancholia) and Karl Abraham viewed depression as arising from real or symbolic loss, with anger toward the lost object being turned inward against the self (anger that cannot be expressed toward the lost person is redirected onto oneself, producing self-reproach and guilt). Later analysts emphasised low self-esteem and the failure to live up to a harsh ideal (the gap between the actual and the idealised self).

4. Behavioural theory

Lewinsohn proposed that depression results from a loss of, or reduction in, positive reinforcement (a fall in the rewarding, pleasurable experiences a person receives from their environment). Reduced activity leads to fewer rewards, which lowers mood and activity further — a vicious cycle. This is the rationale for behavioural activation (a therapy that systematically increases engagement in rewarding activities).

5. Interpersonal theory

Depression is understood in the context of disturbed relationships and social roles — grief, interpersonal disputes, role transitions, and interpersonal deficits. This is the basis of interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT) (a structured therapy that treats depression by addressing current relationship problems).

6. Other relevant psychological concepts

  • Rumination (Nolen-Hoeksema: the tendency to dwell repetitively on distress and its causes, which prolongs and deepens depression).
  • Personality vulnerability: traits of high neuroticism, dependency, and self-criticism increase risk.
  • Bipolar-specific: the behavioural activation system (BAS) dysregulation model (the theory that an over-sensitive reward/goal-pursuit system drives manic responses to goal-attainment events).

C. Social factors

  • Life events: especially loss events (bereavement, separation, redundancy) commonly precede depression; goal-attainment and disruptive events can precede mania.
  • Brown and Harris's vulnerability model: in a classic study, vulnerability factors (loss of mother in childhood, lack of a confiding relationship, three or more children under 15 at home, unemployment) increased the risk that a provoking agent (a severe life event) would trigger depression.
  • Chronic difficulties, poverty, social isolation, childhood adversity and abuse, and lack of social support.

Mnemonic for psychological theories of depression — "Big CLouds Bring Icy Rain"

Beck's cognitive triad · CL = Cognitive/Learned helplessness (& hopelessness) · Behavioural (loss of reinforcement) · Interpersonal · R = psychodynamic (anger tuRned inward) & Rumination.

Epidemiology of Mood Disorders (Comprehensive)

Depressive disorders

MeasureDetail
Lifetime prevalenceMajor depression is common: lifetime prevalence is commonly cited around 10–20%, and it is a leading global cause of disability. Other evidence
Point prevalenceAround 4–7% at any given time in community samples.
Sex ratioDepression is about twice as common in women as in men (roughly 2:1), a finding consistent across most cultures. Kaplan & Sadock

CourseHighly recurrent: after one episode, the risk of a further episode is high and rises with each subsequent episode; a substantial minority follow a chronic course.

MeasureDetail

Suicide risk (whole block)

Mood disorders carry the highest suicide risk of the common psychiatric disorders; a large proportion of people who die by suicide had a mood disorder. Risk is elevated in severe depression, in the mixed and depressive phases of bipolar disorder, early in treatment, and where there is comorbid substance use or hopelessness. Other evidence

F30 · Manic Episode → ICD-11 (manic episode within Bipolar type I, 6A60)

ICD-10 F30 (.0 hypomania, .1 mania without psychotic symptoms, .2 mania with psychotic symptoms, .8/.9)
ICD-11 A manic episode; a single manic episode qualifies for Bipolar type I disorder (6A60)
Core Pathologically elevated or irritable mood + increased energy/activity
Key point A single manic episode is enough to diagnose bipolar disorder

1. Introduction

A manic episode is a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive, or irritable mood with a corresponding increase in energy and goal-directed activity. It ranges in severity from hypomania (a milder elevation without psychosis and without marked functional impairment) through mania without psychosis to mania with psychotic symptoms. ICD-10

2. Etiology

As per the shared etiology section — strongly genetic/biological (catecholamine excess; reward-system/BAS dysregulation), with episodes often triggered by goal-attainment events, sleep loss, or antidepressant treatment. Kaplan & Sadock

3. Epidemiology

See the bipolar epidemiology above — onset typically late teens to mid-20s; equal sex ratio; new-onset mania after 40 warrants exclusion of a secondary (organic/substance) cause.

4. Clinical features — identifying symptoms and presentations

Unique identifying features of mania

  • Decreased need for sleep (feeling rested after only a few hours — different from insomnia, where the person wants to sleep but cannot) — a highly characteristic early sign.
  • Pressure of speech (rapid, loud, hard-to-interrupt speech) and flight of ideas (thoughts and speech leaping rapidly from topic to topic on loose connections).
  • Grandiosity (inflated self-esteem — from over-confidence to grandiose delusions of special powers or identity).
  • Increased goal-directed activity, disinhibition, and reckless behaviour (over-spending, sexual indiscretion, risky driving, rash business ventures).
  • Distractibility; irritability when thwarted.
  • In severe mania: mood-congruent psychotic symptoms (grandiose or persecutory delusions), and, at the extreme, manic stupor.
  • Insight is characteristically lost, so the person rarely presents voluntarily.

Comorbidities and complications

  • Substance use disorders (very high comorbidity — especially alcohol and stimulants).
  • Anxiety disorders and ADHD (particularly in early-onset bipolar).
  • Consequences of disinhibition: financial ruin, legal problems, relationship breakdown, sexually transmitted infections.
  • Physical: exhaustion and dehydration in severe mania; metabolic and cardiovascular risk over the course of the illness.

5. Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10

F30.0 Hypomania: persistent mild elevation of mood, increased energy and activity, and usually marked feelings of well-being and physical/mental efficiency, for at least several days; without hallucinations or delusions and without severe disruption of work or social rejection.

F30.1 Mania without psychotic symptoms: mood elevated out of keeping with circumstances; increased energy, overactivity, pressure of speech, decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, and reckless behaviour; the episode should last at least one week and be severe enough to disrupt work and social activity.

F30.2 Mania with psychotic symptoms: as above, with delusions (usually grandiose) or hallucinations, or with such severe excitement/flight of ideas that the person is incomprehensible or inaccessible.

ICD-11 Manic episode

An extreme mood state lasting at least one week (unless shortened by treatment) characterised by euphoria, irritability, or expansiveness, and increased activity or a subjective experience of increased energy, accompanied by several of: rapid/pressured speech; flight of ideas; increased self-esteem/grandiosity; decreased need for sleep; distractibility; impulsive/reckless behaviour; and increased sexual drive or sociability. A single manic (or mixed) episode is sufficient to diagnose Bipolar type I disorder (6A60). Hypomanic episodes are less severe, last at least several days, and lack psychotic features and marked impairment.

Detailed subtypes of F30 with their diagnostic thresholds

  • F30.0 Hypomania: mild, sustained (at least several days) elevation of mood, energy, and activity, with increased sociability, talkativeness, over-familiarity, increased sexual energy, and reduced need for sleep — but not to the degree that it severely disrupts work/social functioning, and without hallucinations or delusions.
  • F30.1 Mania without psychotic symptoms: mood elevated/irritable and out of keeping with circumstances, lasting at least 1 week, with grandiosity, pressure of speech, flight of ideas, decreased sleep, distractibility, and reckless/disinhibited behaviour, severe enough to disrupt ordinary work and social activities — but no psychotic features.
  • F30.2 Mania with psychotic symptoms: as F30.1 plus delusions (usually grandiose; may be persecutory) or hallucinations, or flight of ideas/excitement so severe the person is incomprehensible. Specify mood-congruent or mood-incongruent psychotic symptoms.
  • F30.8 other manic episodes; F30.9 manic episode, unspecified.

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonic — "DIG FAST"

Distractibility · Irresponsibility / disinhibition · Grandiosity · Flight of ideas · Activity/energy increased · Sleep need decreased · Talkativeness (pressured speech). Mania ≥ 1 week; hypomania = milder, several days, no psychosis.

7. Treatment

  • Pharmacological (acute mania): mood stabilisers (lithium, valproate) and/or antipsychotics (e.g. olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, aripiprazole); benzodiazepines for sedation; stop any antidepressant. Lithium (a mood-stabilising drug requiring regular blood-level monitoring because of a narrow safety margin) also reduces suicide risk. ECT for severe/treatment-resistant mania. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Psychological: psychoeducation (recognising early warning signs, sleep and routine regulation), family involvement, and adherence support.

F31 · Bipolar Affective Disorder → ICD-11 6A60 / 6A61

ICD-10 F31 (.0–.9: coded by current episode type and severity)
ICD-11 6A60 Bipolar type I; 6A61 Bipolar type II
Core Repeated episodes of disturbed mood — at least one being manic/hypomanic/mixed
Key change ICD-11 formally distinguishes type I and type II

1. Introduction

A disorder of repeated (at least two) episodes in which mood and activity levels are significantly disturbed, with the disturbance being on some occasions an elevation (mania/hypomania) and on others a lowering (depression). Recovery between episodes is usually complete. ICD-10

2–3. Etiology and epidemiology

Among the most heritable psychiatric disorders (see above); onset typically late teens–20s; equal sex ratio for type I.

4. Clinical features, presentations, and comorbidities

Unique identifying features and the type I / type II distinction

  • Bipolar type I (6A60): at least one full manic (or mixed) episode (usually with depressive episodes too). One manic episode is sufficient.
  • Bipolar type II (6A61): at least one hypomanic and at least one depressive episode, but no full manic episode — often misdiagnosed as unipolar depression because patients present in the depressed phase and do not recognise hypomania as abnormal.
  • Rapid cycling (four or more mood episodes in a year) — carries a poorer prognosis.
  • Mixed episode/features — manic and depressive symptoms co-occurring (e.g. racing thoughts with despair) — a high-risk state for suicide.
  • Depressive episodes usually dominate the long-term course, especially in type II.

Comorbidities and complications

  • Substance use disorders and anxiety disorders (both very common).
  • ADHD and personality disorders.
  • Elevated cardiovascular and metabolic disease; high suicide risk (especially mixed/depressive phases).
  • Antidepressant monotherapy can precipitate a switch into mania or rapid cycling.

5. Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10

Requires at least two episodes in which mood and activity are significantly disturbed, at least one of which must be manic or hypomanic. The current episode is specified by the fourth character (F31.0 hypomanic; F31.1/.2 manic ± psychosis; F31.3–.5 depressive of varying severity ± psychosis; F31.6 mixed; F31.7 in remission).

Detailed ICD-10 subtypes of F31 (coded by the current episode)

The general requirement (two or more mood episodes, at least one manic/hypomanic) must be met; the fourth/fifth character then describes the current episode:

  • F31.0 — current episode hypomanic: present episode meets criteria for hypomania (F30.0), with a past manic/hypomanic or depressive episode.
  • F31.1 — current episode manic without psychotic symptoms: present episode meets F30.1.
  • F31.2 — current episode manic with psychotic symptoms: present episode meets F30.2 (delusions/hallucinations).
  • F31.3 — current episode mild or moderate depression (.30 without / .31 with somatic syndrome): present episode meets F32.0/F32.1.
  • F31.4 — current episode severe depression without psychotic symptoms: present episode meets F32.2.
  • F31.5 — current episode severe depression with psychotic symptoms: present episode meets F32.3.
  • F31.6 — current episode mixed: a mixture, or rapid alternation (within a few hours), of manic and depressive symptoms, present for at least 2 weeks.
  • F31.7 — currently in remission: past history of at least one manic/hypomanic and one other mood episode, but currently no significant mood disturbance.
  • F31.8 other (e.g. bipolar II, recurrent manic episodes); F31.9 unspecified. (ICD-11 formalises the type I / type II split: 6A60 requires a manic/mixed episode; 6A61 requires hypomania + depression with no full mania.)

ICD-11

6A60 Bipolar type I: at least one manic or mixed episode (a single such episode is sufficient). 6A61 Bipolar type II: at least one hypomanic episode and at least one depressive episode, with no history of a manic or mixed episode. The current episode and its severity are specified.

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

Type I = "one full mania is enough" (I = mania). Type II = "hypomania + depression, never full mania" (II = two milder poles). Rapid cycling = 4+ episodes in a year.

7. Treatment

  • Pharmacological: mood stabilisers (lithium — first-line for long-term prophylaxis and suicide-risk reduction; valproate, lamotrigine — especially for bipolar depression), and atypical antipsychotics. Manage bipolar depression carefully — antidepressants are used cautiously and usually with a mood stabiliser to avoid manic switch. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Psychological: psychoeducation, CBT, family-focused therapy, interpersonal and social rhythm therapy (a therapy that stabilises daily routines and sleep–wake cycles to prevent episodes), and relapse-prevention/early-warning-sign planning.

F32 · Depressive Episode → ICD-11 6A70 (Single Episode Depressive Disorder)

ICD-10 F32 (.0 mild, .1 moderate, .2 severe without psychosis, .3 severe with psychosis; .00/.01 etc. specify somatic syndrome)
ICD-11 6A70 Single episode depressive disorder (severity specified; with/without psychotic symptoms; with melancholia)
Core A single episode of clinically significant low mood and related symptoms
Duration At least 2 weeks

1. Introduction

A depressive episode is a period of at least two weeks of pervasive low mood, loss of interest/pleasure, and reduced energy, accompanied by cognitive and physical symptoms, causing distress and functional impairment. Severity (mild, moderate, severe) is judged from the number and intensity of symptoms and the degree of functional impairment. ICD-10

2–3. Etiology and epidemiology

See the comprehensive shared sections above (monoamine deficiency, HPA-axis overactivity, and the psychological theories — Beck's cognitive triad, learned helplessness/hopelessness, behavioural loss of reinforcement, interpersonal, and psychodynamic models; social loss events). Lifetime prevalence around 10–20%, about twice as common in women.

4. Clinical features, presentations, and comorbidities

Unique identifying features and presentations

  • Core triad: depressed mood + anhedonia + reduced energy, persistent for at least 2 weeks, varying little from day to day.
  • Melancholic / somatic features: early-morning wakening, diurnal variation (worse in the morning), marked psychomotor retardation (slowed movements, speech, and thinking) or agitation, loss of appetite, weight loss, loss of libido, and lack of emotional reactivity.
  • Psychotic depression: mood-congruent delusions (of guilt, poverty, nihilism — e.g. Cotard syndrome (the nihilistic delusion that one is dead or that one's body/organs are rotting or do not exist)) or hallucinations (derogatory voices).
  • Atypical depression: mood reactivity, over-eating, over-sleeping (hypersomnia), and rejection sensitivity.
  • Masked / somatic presentations: presenting with unexplained physical complaints (pain, fatigue) rather than reported sadness — common in some cultures and in the elderly.
  • "Depression without sadness" in the elderly: prominent apathy, cognitive complaints (pseudodementia — depression mimicking dementia), and somatic symptoms.

Comorbidities and complications

  • Anxiety disorders (the commonest comorbidity), substance use disorders, and personality disorders.
  • Chronic physical illness (diabetes, cardiovascular disease, chronic pain) — a bidirectional relationship that worsens both.
  • Suicide — the most serious complication; assess risk in every case.
  • Occupational and relationship impairment; in the perinatal period, postnatal depression with risks to mother–infant bonding.

5. Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10

The episode must last at least 2 weeks. Using the typical (core) symptoms (depressed mood, loss of interest, reduced energy) and the common symptoms (reduced concentration, low self-esteem, guilt, pessimism, self-harm ideas, disturbed sleep, reduced appetite): Mild (F32.0) = at least two core + at least two other symptoms; Moderate (F32.1) = at least two core + at least three (preferably four) others; Severe (F32.2/.3) = all three core + at least four others, some severe, with or without psychotic symptoms. The somatic syndrome is specified by a fifth character (four or more somatic features).

Detailed ICD-10 subtypes of the depressive episode (with symptom thresholds)

  • F32.0 — Mild depressive episode (.00 without / .01 with somatic syndrome): at least two of the three core symptoms + at least two of the other common symptoms; the person is distressed but usually able to continue most activities.
  • F32.1 — Moderate depressive episode (.10 without / .11 with somatic syndrome): at least two core + at least three (preferably four) other symptoms; considerable difficulty continuing ordinary activities.
  • F32.2 — Severe depressive episode without psychotic symptoms: all three core symptoms + at least four others, several of marked intensity; agitation or retardation prominent; substantial distress and functional loss; suicidal ideas/acts common.
  • F32.3 — Severe depressive episode with psychotic symptoms: as F32.2 plus delusions (guilt, poverty, nihilistic/Cotard, persecutory), hallucinations (derogatory/accusatory voices, smells of decay), or depressive stupor. Specify mood-congruent or mood-incongruent.
  • The somatic ("melancholic/biological") syndrome (fifth character): four or more of — marked anhedonia; lack of emotional reactivity; early-morning wakening (≥2 h early); diurnal variation (worse in the morning); marked psychomotor retardation/agitation; marked appetite loss; weight loss (≥5% in a month); marked loss of libido.
  • F32.8 other; F32.9 unspecified.

ICD-11 (6A70)

A single episode lasting at least 2 weeks during most of the day, nearly every day, with the presence of affective (depressed mood or reduced interest/pleasure), cognitive-behavioural (reduced concentration, worthlessness/guilt, hopelessness, suicidal ideation, indecisiveness), and neurovegetative (sleep disturbance, appetite/weight change, psychomotor change, reduced energy) symptoms. Severity (mild/moderate/severe), presence of psychotic symptoms, and a "with melancholia" specifier are recorded. No history of manic, hypomanic, or mixed episodes.

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonic — "SIG E CAPS" (plus core low mood)

Sleep · Interest (anhedonia) · Guilt · Energy · Concentration · Appetite/weight · Psychomotor change · Suicidality — over ≥2 weeks of low mood. Somatic/melancholic clue: early-morning wakening + worse in the morning.

7. Treatment

  • Pharmacological: antidepressants — SSRIs (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, e.g. fluoxetine, sertraline — usual first line), SNRIs, tricyclics, and others; combine with an antipsychotic for psychotic depression; ECT for severe, psychotic, or treatment-resistant depression or where a rapid response is needed (e.g. high suicide risk, refusal to eat/drink). Kaplan & Sadock
  • Psychological (directly derived from the theories above): CBT (Beck), behavioural activation (Lewinsohn), interpersonal psychotherapy, and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy for relapse prevention; problem-solving and supportive approaches for milder cases.

F33 · Recurrent Depressive Disorder → ICD-11 6A71

ICD-10 F33 (current episode specified by severity, ± somatic/psychotic features; F33.4 in remission)
ICD-11 6A71 Recurrent depressive disorder
Core Repeated depressive episodes with no history of mania/hypomania
Key point "Unipolar" recurrent depression — the absence of any manic/hypomanic episode is essential

1. Introduction

A disorder of repeated depressive episodes (as in F32) with no history of independent episodes of mood elevation (mania or hypomania). If a manic episode ever occurs, the diagnosis changes to bipolar disorder. ICD-10

2–3. Etiology and epidemiology

As for depressive episode; recurrence risk rises with each episode, and residual symptoms between episodes predict relapse. Onset often in the mid-30s to 40s for the recurrent pattern; strong female preponderance.

4. Clinical features and comorbidities

Identifying features

  • Two or more depressive episodes separated by several months of relative freedom from significant mood symptoms.
  • Individual episodes resemble F32; between episodes recovery is usually complete, but some have residual symptoms.
  • Never a manic or hypomanic episode (the single fact that separates this from bipolar disorder).
  • Seasonal pattern in some (recurrent winter depression).

Comorbidities and complications

  • Anxiety disorders, substance use, and chronic physical illness, as for single-episode depression.
  • Higher cumulative suicide risk and greater chronicity with more episodes.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Repeated episodes of depression (each meeting the criteria for a depressive episode, F32), with no history of independent episodes of elevated mood and increased energy (mania). Episodes usually last 3–12 months, separated by usually symptom-free periods of a few months.

Detailed ICD-10 subtypes of F33 (coded by the current episode)

  • F33.0 — current episode mild (.00 without / .01 with somatic syndrome): present episode meets F32.0.
  • F33.1 — current episode moderate (.10 without / .11 with somatic syndrome): present episode meets F32.1.
  • F33.2 — current episode severe without psychotic symptoms: present episode meets F32.2.
  • F33.3 — current episode severe with psychotic symptoms: present episode meets F32.3.
  • F33.4 — currently in remission: criteria for recurrent depressive disorder have been met in the past but the current state is free of significant depressive symptoms.
  • F33.8 other; F33.9 unspecified. (A single lifetime manic/hypomanic episode would reclassify the disorder as bipolar, F31.)

ICD-11 (6A71)

A history of at least two depressive episodes separated by at least several months without significant mood disturbance, in the absence of any history of manic, hypomanic, or mixed episodes. Current episode severity and features are specified.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic

"Depression again and again, but the mood never swings up." One lifetime manic/hypomanic episode → reclassify as bipolar.

Treatment: acute treatment as for a depressive episode (antidepressants + psychotherapy); continuation and maintenance antidepressant treatment is emphasised to prevent relapse, especially after multiple episodes; mindfulness-based cognitive therapy and CBT reduce relapse. Kaplan & Sadock

F34 · Persistent Mood (Affective) Disorders → ICD-11 6A62 / 6A72

ICD-10 F34 (.0 cyclothymia, .1 dysthymia, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6A62 Cyclothymic disorder; 6A72 Dysthymic disorder
Core Persistent, fluctuating, but usually sub-threshold mood disturbance lasting years
Key point Chronic and low-grade — rarely severe enough to be called an "episode"

1. Introduction

Persistent and usually fluctuating disorders of mood in which individual episodes are rarely, if ever, severe enough to be described as hypomanic or even mild depressive episodes. Because they last for years at a time (sometimes for the greater part of adult life), they cause considerable distress and disability. ICD-10

2–3. Etiology and epidemiology

Genetically related to the mood disorders (cyclothymia to bipolar disorder; dysthymia to depressive disorder); temperament and early adversity contribute. Often begins insidiously in adolescence or early adult life and runs a chronic course. Kaplan & Sadock

4. The two disorders — identifying features and comorbidities

Cyclothymia (F34.0 / 6A62)

  • Persistent instability of mood over at least two years, with numerous periods of mild depression and mild elation (hypomania-like), none severe or long enough to meet the criteria for bipolar disorder or recurrent depressive disorder.
  • Considered a mild, chronic form on the bipolar spectrum; a proportion later develop bipolar disorder.

Dysthymia (F34.1 / 6A72)

  • A chronic depression of mood lasting at least two years that does not currently fulfil the criteria for recurrent depressive disorder in terms of severity or duration of individual episodes ("depression that never quite lifts").
  • Sufferers often have periods of days or weeks when they feel well, but most of the time (often for months) they are tired and depressed.
  • Double depression (a full major depressive episode superimposed on pre-existing dysthymia) is a common and important presentation.

Comorbidities

  • Dysthymia: frequent comorbid major depression (double depression), anxiety disorders, and personality disorders.
  • Cyclothymia: substance use and later progression to bipolar disorder.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Cyclothymia: persistent mood instability with numerous periods of mild depression and mild elation, none sufficiently severe or prolonged to meet criteria for bipolar affective disorder or recurrent depressive disorder, present for at least 2 years. Dysthymia: chronic depression of mood lasting at least several years that does not meet the criteria (in severity or episode duration) for mild, moderate, or severe recurrent depressive disorder.

Detailed ICD-10 subtypes of F34 (persistent mood disorders) with criteria

  • F34.0 — Cyclothymia: a persistent instability of mood over at least 2 years, involving numerous periods of mild depression and mild elation (hypomania-like), none of which is sufficiently severe or long to meet the criteria for bipolar affective disorder (F31) or recurrent depressive disorder (F33). Onset usually early adult life; considered part of the bipolar spectrum.
  • F34.1 — Dysthymia: a chronic depression of mood lasting at least 2 years (several years), present for most of the time, in which individual episodes are not severe or long enough to meet the criteria for even a mild recurrent depressive episode. There may be brief periods of feeling well. Superimposition of a full depressive episode produces "double depression".
  • F34.8 other persistent mood disorders; F34.9 unspecified.

ICD-11

6A62 Cyclothymic disorder: persistent (≥2 years) mood instability involving numerous hypomanic and depressive periods that are sub-threshold for a manic, mixed, or depressive episode. 6A72 Dysthymic disorder: persistent depressed mood lasting ≥2 years, for most of the day, more days than not, without a period of full remission lasting longer than a couple of months, and not meeting the requirements for a depressive episode during the first two years.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonics

Dysthymia = "chronic low-grade depression ≥ 2 years" (the depressive spectrum). Cyclothymia = "chronic mood swings, both mild, ≥ 2 years" (the bipolar spectrum). Watch for double depression in dysthymia.

Treatment: dysthymia — antidepressants (SSRIs) plus psychotherapy (CBT, IPT); cyclothymia — mood stabilisers and psychoeducation, avoiding antidepressant-induced switching; both benefit from long-term support and monitoring. Kaplan & Sadock

ICD-11 Other specified bipolar/depressive disorder
Type Residual "other specified" categories

Used for mood disorders that do not fit the specific categories above. ICD-10

Detailed ICD-10 subtypes of F38 with criteria

  • F38.00 — Mixed affective episode: a single mood episode lasting at least 2 weeks characterised by either a mixture of, or a rapid alternation (usually within a few hours) between, hypomanic/manic and depressive symptoms.
  • F38.10 — Recurrent brief depressive disorder: recurrent brief depressive episodes that have occurred about once a month over the past year, each lasting less than 2 weeks (typically 2–3 days) but meeting the symptom criteria for a depressive episode, and not occurring only in relation to the menstrual cycle.
  • F38.8 — Other specified mood disorders: a residual category for mood disorders not classifiable elsewhere.

Two golden rules for the block

Rule 1 — always ask about past highs: screen every depressed patient for any past manic/hypomanic episode — a single one makes the diagnosis bipolar and changes treatment (mood stabiliser, cautious antidepressant use).

Rule 2 — assess suicide risk in every mood disorder; risk is highest in severe/psychotic depression and in mixed and depressive bipolar states.
