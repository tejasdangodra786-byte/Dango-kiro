### AREA I: FAMILY (Total: 2/6 — Mild Disturbance)

#### 1. Attitude toward Mother (Items 14, 29, 44, 59) — Rating: 1 (Mildly Disturbed)

The subject demonstrates an overall positive but mildly ambivalent attitude toward his mother. While affection and regard for the maternal figure are present, there are indications of mild emotional conflict — possibly related to guilt over having caused her distress through his substance use history, or to a sense that the mother-son bond carries unresolved tension. The ambivalence does not reach a level requiring therapeutic intervention specifically for this relationship, but it colours his broader relational world. This is consistent with the pattern observed in the TAT protocol (Card 4) where maternal devotion was acknowledged alongside guilt for burdening the self-sacrificing mother.

The mild disturbance suggests the subject recognizes his mother's importance and investment in him but experiences internal discomfort — likely guilt-driven — when reflecting on this relationship. There is no evidence of overt hostility, rejection, or deep-seated resentment toward the maternal figure.

#### 2. Attitude toward Father (Items 1, 16, 31, 46) — Rating: 1 (Mildly Disturbed)

The subject's attitude toward his father reveals mild emotional conflict. There are indications of a complex father-son dynamic characterized by elements of admiration and dependency alongside felt distance, unmet wishes, or mild resentment. The father appears to be experienced as a significant and powerful figure — one whose approval is deeply desired but perhaps inconsistently felt.

This finding is congruent with the TAT protocol (Card 3, Card 11) where the father was supremely idealized as a life-saving figure ("once I drank poison and he saved me"), yet the relationship also carried an undercurrent of guilt and inadequacy ("I fell behind"). The mild disturbance score on the SSCT corroborates that while the paternal relationship is fundamentally intact and highly cathected, it is not entirely free of conflict — the subject likely experiences himself as having "failed" this idealized father, generating a tension between gratitude/admiration and shame/inadequacy.

#### 3. Attitude toward Family Unit (Items 12, 27, 42, 57) — Rating: 0 (No Significant Disturbance)

The subject perceives his family unit positively and without significant conflict. He appears to view his family as cohesive, supportive, and a source of security. This is consistent with the idealised family-as-refuge theme pervading the TAT protocol (Card 2's idyllic village, Card 6's dream world, Card 11's all-good family enclave). The family is experienced as the "safe harbour" — the good object that provides structure and meaning.

This absence of disturbance at the family-unit level, combined with mild disturbance in the individual parental relationships, suggests that the conflict is personalised rather than systemic — he struggles with specific guilt/inadequacy vis-à-vis each parent individually rather than perceiving the family structure itself as dysfunctional.

### AREA III: INTERPERSONAL RELATIONSHIPS (Total: 3/8 — Moderate, with Severe Focal Disturbance)

#### 6. Attitude toward Friends (Items 8, 23, 38, 53) — Rating: 1 (Mildly Disturbed)

The subject demonstrates mild conflict in his attitude toward friends and acquaintances. While he values friendship and shows capacity for peer connection, there are indications of wariness, selective distrust, or dissatisfaction in peer relationships. This may reflect:
- Past experiences where peer associations contributed to substance initiation or relapse ("bad company" identified in TAT Card 5 and 11)
- A degree of social insecurity or uncertainty about where he stands with others
- Ambivalence between wanting closeness and recognizing that certain friendships have been harmful

The mild disturbance is clinically meaningful in the context of substance use disorder, where peer relationships are frequently the vector for relapse. The subject appears to be developing a discriminating stance toward friendships — distinguishing between genuinely supportive connections and those associated with his substance-use history.

#### 7. Attitude toward Superiors (Items 6, 21, 36, 51) — Rating: 2 (SEVERELY DISTURBED)

**This is one of two areas of maximal disturbance in the profile and constitutes a primary clinical finding.**

The subject demonstrates severe conflict in his relationship with authority figures — supervisors, teachers, bosses, and those in positions of power over him. This disturbance suggests deeply held resentment, rebelliousness, fear, or experienced oppression in hierarchical relationships. The authority figure is likely perceived as controlling, unreasonable, threatening, or worthy of defiance.

This severe authority conflict is of high clinical significance for several reasons:

1. **Pattern with Paternal Relationship:** The mild disturbance toward the father (score 1) combined with severe disturbance toward superiors (score 2) constitutes what Sacks & Levy describe as an "authority conflict pattern." The father — while idealized — represents a specific, beloved authority; but *generalized* authority (teachers, bosses, institutional figures) is experienced with intense negativity. This suggests that the father's idealization may be a defended, partial exception to an otherwise hostile stance toward power and control.

2. **Relevance to Substance Use:** Authority conflict is among the most frequently observed interpersonal patterns in substance-dependent populations. Rebelliousness toward external control, resentment of institutional demands, and difficulty accepting direction are well-established correlates of addictive behavior. The patient's substance use may have served, in part, as an expression of autonomy/defiance against perceived external control.

3. **Treatment Implications:** This disturbance carries direct implications for the therapeutic alliance and institutional compliance. The patient is currently in an inpatient de-addiction facility — an inherently hierarchical, rule-bound setting. The severe authority disturbance predicts potential friction with treatment staff, difficulty accepting program rules, and vulnerability to premature treatment termination if he perceives the therapeutic structure as oppressive.

4. **Developmental Context:** The TAT's recurrent pattern of superego inconsistency (strict internal standards but lax impulse control at the moment of action) may be rooted in this authority ambivalence — he has internalized strict expectations but simultaneously rebels against external enforcement of those expectations.

#### 8. Attitude toward People Supervised (Items 4, 19, 34, 49) — Rating: 0 (No Significant Disturbance)

#### 9. Attitude toward Colleagues (Items 13, 28, 43, 58) — Rating: 0 (No Significant Disturbance)

The absence of disturbance here, contrasted with the severe disturbance toward superiors, further sharpens the specificity of his conflict: it is *vertical power dynamics* (being subordinate), not *horizontal relationships* (being among equals), that activate his disturbance.

### AREA IV: SELF-CONCEPT (Total: 6/12 — Moderate Disturbance)

#### 10. Fears (Items 7, 22, 37, 52) — Rating: 2 (SEVERELY DISTURBED)

**This is the second area of maximal disturbance and a primary clinical finding.**

The subject demonstrates severe disturbance in the domain of fears. His fears are likely pervasive, significantly impactful on his daily functioning, and possibly debilitating. The severity of this rating indicates that his fears are not merely acknowledged anxieties but are experienced as forces that *control* his behavior, limit his functioning, and cause substantial internal distress.

The nature of these fears, in the context of the broader clinical picture, likely includes:
- **Fear of relapse** — returning to substance use and the destructive cycle
- **Fear of loss** — particularly of the idealized father ("when the father is gone, a person is alone" — TAT Card 11) and family support
- **Fear of failure** — in recovery, in life, in meeting parental expectations
- **Fear of the future** — uncertainty about post-treatment life and capacity to maintain sobriety
- **Existential fears** — related to the prior suicide attempt and confrontation with mortality

The severe fear disturbance, coexisting with the pervasive guilt (mild) and damaged self-concept (mild disturbance in abilities), suggests an anxious-depressive undercurrent beneath the surface presentation. The fears likely represent a condensation of past traumatic experiences (overdose, poison episode, loss of relationships) and anticipatory anxiety about the vulnerability of his current recovery.

This finding corroborates the Rorschach protocol's elevated MOR content (damage-oriented cognitive set) and the TAT's recurrent themes of helplessness and abandonment (Cards 8, 11). Across all three projective instruments, a picture emerges of an individual who carries substantial internal dread beneath whatever surface presentation he maintains.

#### 11. Guilt Feelings (Items 15, 30, 45, 60) — Rating: 1 (Mildly Disturbed)

The subject demonstrates mild but clinically meaningful guilt. His guilt is present and emotionally active — he is aware of past transgressions and experiences remorse — but it does not reach a debilitating or paralyzing intensity. The content of his guilt likely centers on:

This mild guilt finding is particularly informative when read alongside the TAT protocol, where guilt-and-reparation was identified as the *signature dynamic* of the entire record. The SSCT's mild (rather than severe) rating suggests that while guilt is a constant companion, it has been *partially processed* — possibly through the very act of entering treatment, which serves as a reparative gesture ("I am here for my parents' sake"). The subject demonstrates mild disturbance in his perception of his own capabilities. This finding aligns with:

The mild rather than severe rating suggests that self-efficacy is damaged but not destroyed. The capacity for effort and mastery demonstrated in TAT Card 1 (the violin/veena) — where the boy works hard and eventually succeeds — indicates a reparable self-concept in this domain. With sustained sobriety and accumulating evidence of competence, this area has potential for improvement.

#### 14. Attitude toward Past (Items 9, 24, 39, 54) — Rating: 0 (No Significant Disturbance)

The subject demonstrates no significant disturbance in his attitude toward his past. This is a clinically interesting finding given the documented history of substance dependence, a suicide attempt, and relational difficulties. The absence of disturbance suggests one of several possibilities:

1. **Acceptance and integration:** He has achieved a degree of acceptance regarding past events — neither fixated on trauma nor defensively avoiding memories. The past is acknowledged without being a source of active torment.

2. **Forward-oriented stance:** His psychological energy is directed toward the present and future rather than ruminating on past events. This may represent a healthy recovery orientation.

3. **Mild denial/avoidance:** Less favorably, the zero score could reflect avoidance of painful past material — a glossing-over rather than genuine integration. However, given the TAT's open acknowledgment of past failures (Card 11's autobiography), frank avoidance seems less likely.

The most parsimonious interpretation, given the broader clinical picture, is that the subject has *largely come to terms* with his past without idealizing or being haunted by it — a positive indicator for treatment engagement.

#### 15. Attitude toward Future (Items 5, 20, 35, 50) — Rating: 1 (Mildly Disturbed)

The subject demonstrates mild conflict regarding the future. While not overtly hopeless, he experiences the future with some degree of uncertainty, ambivalence, or anxiety. The future is neither wholly dark nor confidently bright — it exists as a space of possibility tinged with doubt.

This finding is consistent with:

- The TAT's "hopeful/reparative but not certain" endings

The mild (rather than severe) disturbance is actually a positive prognostic indicator — it suggests that hopelessness has not settled in, and that the future retains motivational pull, even if accompanied by anxiety. Complete absence of future-orientation disturbance would, paradoxically, be more concerning (suggesting denial of realistic challenges ahead).

## Integrated Formulation

### Profile Pattern Analysis

The SSCT profile reveals a **specific and clinically coherent pattern** of emotional disturbance organized around two axes:

**Axis 1 — Authority and Control (External World):**
The most severe interpersonal disturbance lies in the relationship with authority figures (Score 2), surrounded by mild conflict with both parents (Score 1 each) and mild wariness toward friends (Score 1), while lateral (colleagues) and downward (subordinates) relationships are undisturbed. This configuration describes a person whose interpersonal conflicts are specifically activated by *hierarchical subordination* — being under someone else's power or direction. The family unit as an abstraction is idealized and conflict-free, but the specific individuals who hold authority (parents individually, superiors generally) generate tension.

**Axis 2 — Internal Fear and Self-Doubt (Inner World):**
The most severe self-concept disturbance lies in the domain of fears (Score 2), accompanied by mild disturbances in guilt, goals, abilities, and future orientation (all Score 1), while the past is accepted (Score 0). This configuration describes a person whose internal world is organized around *pervasive fearfulness* — a chronic anticipatory dread that colours his goal-striving, self-appraisal, and future-orientation without being specifically localized in any single content. The fears are the *core* of the self-concept disturbance; the surrounding mild disturbances in goals, abilities, and future are best understood as *consequences* of this fearfulness rather than independent conflicts.

### Cross-Domain Integration

When both axes are read together, the profile describes a young man who:

1. **Experiences external authority as threatening** — not merely disappointing or distant, but evoking intense conflict, likely a mixture of resentment, fear, and felt oppression
2. **Carries pervasive internal fears** that constrain his functioning and confidence
3. **Harbors guilt** that motivates reparative efforts but does not paralyze
4. **Maintains an idealized family attachment** that serves as his primary source of meaning and motivation
5. **Holds his sexuality and gender attitudes relatively intact** — these are not conflict zones
6. **Relates adequately to equals** but struggles with hierarchical power dynamics
7. **Views the future with cautious, anxious hope** rather than despair or denial

### Dynamic Understanding

The subject's personality organization, as revealed across the projective battery, can be summarized as follows:

He is a young man whose central conflict revolves around **autonomy vs. submission**. He deeply needs the love and approval of authority figures (parents, and by extension, the parental surrogates of institutions), yet simultaneously experiences intense resentment and oppositional feeling toward being controlled or directed by them. This conflict has historically been "resolved" through **substance use** — which simultaneously expresses rebellion (defying parental/societal expectations), self-destruction (punishing the self that cannot meet standards), and escape (flight from the unbearable tension between compliance and autonomy).

His fears — severe and pervasive — represent the accumulated emotional residue of this unresolved conflict: fear of what happens if he submits (loss of self), fear of what happens if he rebels (loss of love), fear of relapse, fear of the future without substances, fear of the world without the father's protection. The fears are not irrational phobias but *existential terrors* rooted in genuine developmental and clinical history.

His guilt, while mild, serves a crucial *adaptive* function: it connects him to his family, motivates treatment engagement, and provides the emotional fuel for his reparative efforts. Without guilt, the authority-rebellion axis might dominate unchecked; the guilt acts as a bridge back to the family attachment system.

## Recommendations

Based on the SSCT findings, integrated with the broader assessment battery:

1. **Address authority dynamics directly in therapy.** The severe disturbance toward superiors is likely to manifest in the therapeutic relationship and institutional compliance. The treatment team should anticipate periodic resistance, defiance, or alliance ruptures, and should utilize these as *in-vivo* material for therapeutic work rather than as behavioral management problems alone. A collaborative, autonomy-supporting therapeutic stance (rather than a directive one) is indicated.

2. **Targeted fear-reduction and anxiety management.** The severe fear disturbance warrants specific intervention — whether through cognitive-behavioral techniques addressing catastrophic cognitions, exposure-based approaches to feared situations, or mindfulness-based anxiety regulation. The content of fears should be explored in individual therapy to determine which are realistic and which are distorted.

3. **Strengthen ego resources for autonomy.** The mild goal and ability disturbances suggest that treatment should include structured activities that build self-efficacy and demonstrate competence — vocational planning, skill-building, graded task assignment — so that autonomy is achieved through *mastery* rather than through rebellion or substance use.

4. **Utilize the guilt-reparation dynamic therapeutically.** The mild guilt is a treatment asset. Motivational interviewing techniques that highlight the discrepancy between current behavior and valued relationships (particularly parental) can leverage this guilt in the service of sustained recovery motivation.

5. **Gradually shift motivational base.** Goals remain somewhat externally anchored (parent-directed). Over the course of treatment, help the subject develop intrinsically motivated goals that reflect his own values and aspirations, reducing dependence on parental approval as the sole engine of recovery.

6. **Monitor for authority-related treatment disruption.** Given the severe disturbance score, establish early in treatment an explicit collaborative frame that acknowledges the patient's autonomy within the treatment structure. Periodic check-ins about how he experiences the treatment environment are warranted.

7. **Corroborate with clinical interview.** The present findings should be integrated with detailed history, mental-status examination, and other assessment data before finalizing any diagnostic or treatment formulation.

## Conclusion

The Sacks Sentence Completion Test reveals a profile of **moderate overall emotional disturbance (Total Score: 11/30)** with **two focal areas of severe conflict: attitude toward authority/superiors and fears**. The disturbance pattern is organized around a core tension between the need for attachment/approval and the resentment of external control, expressed through pervasive fearfulness and moderated by active but manageable guilt. The sexual domain and family-unit perception serve as areas of relative strength, while the self-concept carries mild but widespread vulnerability across goals, abilities, guilt, and future orientation.

The findings are clinically coherent, convergent with TAT and Rorschach data, and directly relevant to treatment planning within the inpatient de-addiction context. The subject possesses genuine assets — intact sexuality/gender attitudes, positive family perception, manageable guilt, acceptance of the past, and cautious hope for the future — alongside significant vulnerabilities in authority relationships and fear management that will require focused therapeutic attention.

## Interpretive Cautions

- The SSCT is a **semi-structured projective test** and is not a standardized instrument with formal normative data. Interpretation relies on clinical judgment within the framework described by Sacks & Levy (1950).
- Ratings (0-2) are assigned to **attitude areas** (groups of 4 items), not to individual items.
- Findings represent **hypotheses about underlying attitudes and conflicts** that must be validated against clinical history, behavioral observation, and corroborating assessment data.
- The report assumes the scoring provided is accurate and based on careful reading of all four items within each attitude area.
- Cultural context has been considered; items are interpreted within the Indian socio-cultural framework relevant to the subject.
- The SSCT should never be used as the **sole basis for diagnosis**; it provides complementary data within a comprehensive assessment battery.
