Handout · opening section

Therapeutic formulation — definition, goals & benefits

Definition A process for developing a hypothesis about — and a plan to address — the causes, precipitants and maintaining influences of a person's psychological, interpersonal and behavioural problems, understood in the context of that individual's culture and environment.

Three ideas are doing the work in that definition. First, formulation is a hypothesis — provisional, revisable, and testable, not a fixed verdict. Second, it is explicitly causal and functional: it asks what set the problem up (causes), what set it off (precipitants) and what keeps it going (maintaining influences). Third, it is contextual — the same behaviour means different things in different cultural and environmental settings, so culture and environment are part of the formulation, not an afterthought.

What a formulation includes

The four domains the handout says a formulation must take in
DomainWhat to look atWhy it matters clinically
The individual's behaviourWhether it is under- or over-expressed, normative or non-normative, and adaptive or maladaptiveStops you pathologising behaviour that is normal for the person's context, and pinpoints which behaviours are genuine treatment targets
Interaction with othersThe beliefs the person holds about other people's intentions and wishes, and how they respond to those expectationsInterpersonal expectations drive relationship patterns that often maintain the problem (e.g., expecting rejection → withdrawing → being seen as cold)
The individual's environmentCultural influences, social roles and conflictsRole strain and cultural rules shape both the distress and what counts as an acceptable solution
The physical environment's influence on functioningSafety of the neighbourhood, socioeconomic factors, education and work opportunitiesMaterial and structural realities constrain coping and recovery; a formulation that ignores them will misread "lack of motivation"

Goals of formulating

  • Guides treatment & keeps the therapist on track from one session to the next — it lets you monitor progress and recognise when a change of direction is needed.
  • Increases treatment efficiency — the therapist can build a time-effective, evidence-based plan that runs coherently from the beginning to the end of treatment.
  • Tailors treatment to the client's specific circumstances — the particular problems, the range of diagnoses being addressed, and the context of treatment.
  • Enhances therapist empathy — understanding why the person does what they do makes their behaviour intelligible and the therapist more compassionate.

General model

Cognitive Behavioural Case Formulation

A · Introduction

Origin: the generic cognitive-behavioural formulation (Aaron T. Beck's cognitive model, 1960s–70s; developed by Judith Beck and others). Addresses: a broad range of emotional and behavioural problems. Core assumption: it is not events themselves but the meaning a person attaches to them that drives emotion, body and behaviour — and those meanings grow from early experience, are stored as core beliefs/schemas, and are expressed as day-to-day rules. Distinctive feature vs disorder-specific models: it is a developmental (longitudinal) map of the whole person, and it explicitly includes compensatory strategies — the lifestyle-level manoeuvres people build to cope with a painful core belief.

B · Diagram

The generic CBT case formulation (longitudinal)

Fig. Generic CBT formulation (after Beck; J. Beck). Development runs top-down; the emotion–behaviour–somatic triangle feeds back to maintain the negative automatic thoughts.

Plain text: Early experience (dashed = hypothesised link) shapes schemas / core beliefs (unconditional, e.g. "I am unlovable") → these generate assumptions / rules (conditional "if…then…"). A current trigger/situation activates the assumptions and produces negative automatic thoughts, which drive the triangle of emotions ⇄ behaviours ⇄ somatic reactions (green = each influences the others). Compensatory strategies branch off the assumptions — lifestyle rules built to avoid the pain of the core belief — and themselves shape which triggering situations arise. The dashed gold loop shows the triangle feeding back up to sustain the negative thoughts.

C · Every component explained

(Early) experience
What it means: formative life events — parenting, attachment, losses, trauma, bullying, illness, cultural messages. Simple: the soil the beliefs grow in. Clinical significance: explains why this person is vulnerable to these beliefs. Assess: attachment quality, adversity, the messages received about self/others/world. How to assess: developmental history; "What was growing up like? What did you learn about yourself?" What to write: "Early loss and inconsistent care plausibly contributed to a belief of being unsafe." Clinical example (fictional): repeated criticism in childhood → belief "I'm not good enough." Common mistakes: treating history as proof of a belief — it informs, it does not prove; always corroborate with current evidence.
Schemas / core beliefs (unconditional)
What: absolute, global beliefs about self, others and the world ("I am worthless"; "others are dangerous"). Simple: the deepest "bottom-line" truths the person holds. Significance: they bias how everything is interpreted. Assess: downward-arrow technique ("if that were true, what would it mean about you?"); recurring themes across situations. What to write: "Core belief: 'I am unlovable.'" Example (fictional): a depressed client whose bottom line is "I'm a failure." Mistakes: labelling a situational thought as a core belief — core beliefs are trait-like and global.
Assumptions / expectations / rules / intermediate beliefs (conditional)
What: the "if…then…", "should/must", "unless" rules that link the core belief to daily life. Simple: the coping rules a person adopts so the painful core belief stays hidden. Significance: more accessible than core beliefs and usually the first therapy target. Assess: "What rules do you live by? What has to happen for you to feel OK?" What to write: "Assumption: 'If I'm not perfect, I'll be rejected.'" Example (fictional): "If I always say yes, people won't leave me." Mistakes: missing the conditional structure — these are rules, not facts.
Compensatory strategies
What: broad, often lifelong behavioural strategies the person uses to compensate for the core belief and keep the assumptions "working" (e.g., perfectionism, over-achievement, people-pleasing, avoidance of closeness, controlling others). Simple: the life-pattern built around the rule. Significance: they are what others see as "personality" and they shape which situations the person enters — so they influence what triggers appear. Assess: "How do you make sure your fear doesn't come true? What lengths do you go to?" What to write: "Compensates with relentless over-work to prevent the feared exposure as 'inadequate'." Mistakes: mistaking a compensatory strategy for a strength only — it is adaptive short-term but maintains the belief long-term.
Current trigger / situation
What: the specific present event (internal or external) that activates the dormant beliefs. Simple: the switch that turns the system on now. Significance: tells you where and when the problem fires, and it is partly produced by the person's compensatory strategies. Assess: situational analysis of a recent concrete episode. How: "Describe the last time it happened — where, who, what exactly?" What to write: "Trigger: an ambiguous email from the manager." Mistakes: accepting a vague trigger ("stress") instead of a concrete situation.
Negative automatic thoughts (NATs)
What: rapid, involuntary appraisals (words or images) in the situation. Simple: the instant running commentary. Significance: the proximal driver of the emotion–body–behaviour triangle; the main cognitive target. Assess: "What went through your mind right then? Any image?" What to write: "NAT: 'They think I'm incompetent.'" Mistakes: recording the emotion instead of the thought that produced it.
Emotions · Behaviours · Somatic reactions (the maintaining triangle)
What: the three reciprocally-linked outputs — felt affect, actions (including avoidance/safety behaviours), and bodily arousal. Simple: how the thought lands in feeling, body and action, each feeding the others. Significance: this triangle is where the problem is maintained in the present and where change is observable; behaviour (e.g., avoidance) feeds back to keep the NATs alive. Assess: name and rate emotions (0–100); map body sensations; behavioural analysis/diaries. What to write: "Anxiety 80/100; palpitations; leaves the meeting early → relief → belief 'I can't cope' confirmed." Mistakes: listing the three without drawing the feedback loop; ignoring covert behaviours (mental rituals, reassurance-seeking).

Dialectical Behavioural Formulation — Biosocial Theory

A · Introduction

Origin: Marsha Linehan's biosocial theory, the foundation of Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT); the transactional diagram is adapted from Fruzzetti, Shenk & Hoffman (2005). Addresses: pervasive emotion dysregulation (classically in borderline presentations and self-harm). Core assumption: emotion dysregulation arises from an ongoing transaction between a biologically based emotional vulnerability and a persistently invalidating environment — neither alone is sufficient; they shape each other over time. Distinctive feature: it is explicitly transactional (two-way, developing over time), not a one-way "cause → effect" model.

B · Diagram

DBT biosocial transactional cycle

Fig. Biosocial transaction (after Linehan; adapted from Fruzzetti, Shenk & Hoffman, 2005).

Plain text: a pervasive history of invalidating responses feeds into the person's emotion vulnerability; that vulnerability leads to heightened emotional arousal → inaccurate expression of emotion (maladaptive behaviour) → which draws fresh invalidating responses from the environment. Those responses loop back (gold, maintaining) to intensify emotional vulnerability/arousal, and also accumulate into the pervasive history — a self-reinforcing two-way transaction.

C · Every component explained

Emotion vulnerability (biological side)
What: a temperamental, biologically-based disposition marked by high emotional sensitivity (low threshold to react), high reactivity (intense responses) and slow return to baseline. Simple: emotions come faster, hit harder and last longer. Significance: the constitutional half of the transaction — it is not a fault, it is a vulnerability. Assess: "Do you feel things more intensely than others? How long does it take to calm down?" temperament/early-reactivity history. What to write: "High constitutional emotional sensitivity and slow recovery to baseline." Mistakes: framing it as the whole cause (ignoring the environment) or as mere "drama."
Pervasive history of invalidating responses (social side)
What: a developmental environment that repeatedly dismisses, punishes or trivialises the person's inner experience ("you're overreacting", "there's nothing to cry about"), or oscillates between ignoring and over-reacting. Simple: being told, over and over, that what you feel is wrong or doesn't count. Significance: the environmental half; it teaches the person to distrust and inaccurately label their emotions. Assess: "When you were upset as a child, how did people respond?" What to write: "Chronic invalidation taught the client that emotions are unacceptable and must be suppressed or escalated to be heard." Mistakes: equating invalidation only with overt abuse — well-meaning but mismatched ("poorness of fit") caregiving also invalidates.
Heightened emotional arousal
What: the acute surge of intense emotion produced when vulnerability meets a trigger. Significance: at high arousal, skills go "offline" and behaviour becomes mood-dependent. Assess: SUDS ratings; identify arousal triggers. What to write: "Rapidly escalating arousal that overwhelms coping."
Inaccurate expression (maladaptive behavioural responses)
What: because emotions were never accurately labelled/validated, the person expresses distress in extreme or indirect ways (self-harm, rage, shutting down, escalation). Simple: the "volume" is wrong because the emotional "vocabulary" was never learned. Significance: these behaviours often work short-term (relief, or finally getting a response) which reinforces them. Assess: behavioural chain analysis. What to write: "Self-harm functions to down-regulate unbearable arousal and to communicate distress." Mistakes: reading the behaviour as manipulation rather than as an emotion-regulation attempt.
Invalidating responses (environmental reaction)
What: the environment's reaction to the maladaptive expression — often more invalidation (criticism, withdrawal, punishment), which confirms the person's belief that their emotions are bad. Significance: closes the loop and renews the history of invalidation. Assess: "How do people around you react when you're at your worst?" What to write: "Others respond with criticism/withdrawal, renewing invalidation and raising vulnerability."

D · Every arrow explained

ArrowTypeWhy it exists / how to test it
Pervasive invalidation → emotion vulnerabilitydevelopmentalRepeated invalidation amplifies and entrenches biologically-based vulnerability; test via developmental history.
Vulnerability → heightened arousalcausalLow threshold + high reactivity mean triggers produce large arousal spikes.
Arousal → inaccurate expressioncausalAt high arousal, skills fail and mood-dependent maladaptive behaviour takes over.
Expression → invalidating responsescausalExtreme expression tends to elicit invalidation/withdrawal from others.
Invalidating responses → vulnerability (loop)maintainingFresh invalidation reconfirms "my emotions are wrong", raising future vulnerability — the self-perpetuating transaction. Test: validating responses reduce escalation.

E · Clinical application

Model → treatment (DBT)Because the problem is a transaction, DBT works on both sides: it teaches emotion-regulation and distress-tolerance skills (reducing vulnerability and giving accurate expression), uses validation in therapy to break the invalidation loop, and uses chain analysis to find where to intervene in the cycle, held within a dialectic of acceptance and change.

Diathesis–Stress Model

A · Introduction

Origin: a classic vulnerability–stress framework used across psychopathology. Addresses: why the same stressor produces disorder in one person and not another. Core assumption: a disorder emerges when stress acts on a pre-existing diathesis (vulnerability) — a person with high vulnerability needs only mild stress to cross the threshold into disorder, while a person with low vulnerability can withstand severe stress. The environment modulates both. It unfolds over time.

B · Diagram

Diathesis × Stress, modulated by environment, over time

Fig. Diathesis–stress model — vulnerability, stress and environment interact bidirectionally across time.

Plain text: Stress (disease, trauma, noxious input) acts upon a diathesis (genes, anatomy, neurophysiology, learning history). The environment (support, resources, healthcare) buffers or worsens the interaction. The double-headed grey arrows show all three influence one another, and the whole system plays out along a time axis. Disorder appears when the combined load crosses the person's threshold.

C · Components

Diathesis (vulnerability)
What: the predisposing, relatively stable vulnerability — genetic loading, anatomical/neurophysiological factors, and prior learning history. Simple: how "primed" the person is before any stress arrives. Significance: sets the threshold; high diathesis = disorder from small stress. Assess: family history, temperament, medical/developmental history, prior episodes. What to write: "Strong family history and early adverse learning constitute a high diathesis."
Stress
What: the precipitating load — illness, trauma, or aversive input. Simple: the pressure applied now. Significance: the trigger that interacts with vulnerability. Assess: recent life events, losses, physical illness. What to write: "Onset followed a major stressor (bereavement) acting on this vulnerability."
Environment
What: the surrounding resources — interpersonal support, economic resources, healthcare access. Simple: the cushion (or lack of one) around the person. Significance: raises or lowers the effective threshold; good support can absorb stress that would otherwise precipitate disorder. Assess: social network, finances, access to care. What to write: "Limited support and poor healthcare access reduce the buffer, increasing risk."

D · Arrows

ArrowTypeWhy / how tested
Diathesis ⇄ Stressreciprocal/interactiveVulnerability magnifies the impact of stress; chronic stress can also deepen vulnerability (e.g., sensitisation). Test: same stressor, different outcomes by vulnerability level.
Environment ⇄ Stress & DiathesisreciprocalSupport/resources buffer the stress–vulnerability interaction; adversity adds to it. Test: outcome improves when support/resources increase.
Model → treatmentYou cannot change the diathesis much, so treatment lowers stress (problem-solving, pacing) and strengthens the environment (support, resources, access to care) to keep total load below threshold — while psychological work reduces the functional impact of the vulnerability.

Biopsychosocial Model (Engel)

A · Introduction

Origin: George Engel (1977). Addresses: any presentation — it is a general framework insisting that illness is produced by interacting biological, psychological and social factors, not by biology alone. Core assumption: no single level is sufficient; the clinician must formulate across all three and can intervene at any level. The handout illustrates the model with an evolutionary biopsychosocial account of shame (after Gilbert, 2002), showing how innate social motives, cultural context and personal experiences of shaming combine to produce internalised shame, external shame or humiliation.

B · Diagram (domains)

The three interacting domains (Engel, 1977)

Fig. Engel's biopsychosocial model — three overlapping domains; the problem lives in their interaction.

Plain text: biological, psychological and social factors overlap; the central overlap is the whole person. Treatment can enter through any domain (medication, therapy, social intervention).

B · Diagram (illustrative shame model, Gilbert 2002)

An evolutionary & biopsychosocial model of shame (after Gilbert, 2002)

Fig. Biopsychosocial/evolutionary model of shame (after Gilbert, 2002) — an illustration of how the BPS levels combine.

Plain text: innate social motives (biological/psychological) lead into social-cultural contexts and personal experiences of shaming (social). These branch into three outcomes — internalised shame, external shame and humiliation (which are reciprocally linked) — and produce reflected stigma / community rejection, which loops back to intensify external shame and humiliation. It demonstrates biological, psychological and social levels operating together.

C · Components (domains)

Biological
What: genes, physical illness, neurochemistry, substances, sleep, medication effects. Assess: family history, physical health, substance use, sleep. What to write: "Biological: family history of depression; poorly controlled diabetes."
Psychological
What: beliefs, schemas, coping style, emotion regulation, personality, attachment style. Assess: beliefs about self/others, coping, attachment. What to write: "Psychological: insecure attachment; avoids expressing negative emotion; unassertive."
Social
What: relationships, family, work, culture, finances, stigma, community. Assess: relationships, roles, support, cultural context. What to write: "Social: marital tension and withdrawal from friends; some supportive colleagues."

D · Arrows

RelationshipTypeWhy
Bio ⇄ Psycho ⇄ SocialreciprocalEach level influences the others (e.g., illness → low mood → withdrawal → worse illness). No level is primary.
Shaming experiences → shame outcomescausalCultural rules + personal shaming generate internalised/external shame or humiliation.
Reflected stigma → shame (loop)maintainingCommunity rejection feeds back to sustain external shame and humiliation.
Model → treatmentBecause causation is multilevel, intervention can be multilevel — medication/physical-health care (bio), cognitive and compassion-focused work on internal shame (psycho), and work on relationships, roles and stigma (social). Compassion-focused therapy grew directly out of Gilbert's shame model.

Anxiety Disorders — general schema model

A · Introduction

Origin: Adrian Wells' general schema model of the anxiety disorders (after Wells, 1997). Addresses: anxiety disorders as a family. Core assumption: a learning experience lays down danger schemas; a critical incident later activates them, producing negative automatic thoughts, anxiety symptoms, behavioural responses and cognitive biases that feed back to keep the schema active. It is the shared template that the panic, social-phobia and GAD models specialise.

B · Diagram

General schema model of anxiety (after Wells, 1997)

Fig. General schema model of anxiety disorders (after Wells, 1997).

Plain text: a learning experience forms danger schemas; later a critical incident activates the schema, producing negative automatic thoughts → anxiety symptoms → behavioural responses → cognitive biases. The dashed gold feedback arrows show symptoms, behaviours and biases looping back to reinforce the negative thoughts and keep the schema active — the maintenance engine.

C · Components

Danger schemas
What: stored beliefs that certain situations/sensations signal threat. Simple: a "the world/my body is dangerous" template learned earlier. Assess: "What have you learned to see as dangerous? When did that start?" What to write: "Danger schema: 'physical symptoms mean something is seriously wrong.'" Mistakes: treating the anxiety as irrational rather than schema-driven.
Schema activation & negative automatic thoughts
What: the critical incident switches the dormant schema on, generating threat-themed NATs. Assess: "What was happening when it flared? What went through your mind?" What to write: "On [trigger], schema activated → NAT 'I'm in danger.'"
Anxiety symptoms, behavioural responses & cognitive biases (maintaining)
What: physiological arousal; avoidance/safety behaviours; and attentional/interpretive biases (e.g., scanning for threat, interpreting ambiguity as dangerous). Significance: all three feed back to confirm the danger schema. Assess: symptom map; "What do you do to feel safe? What do you pay attention to?" What to write: "Avoidance and threat-scanning prevent disconfirmation, maintaining the schema."

D · Arrows

ArrowTypeWhy / test
Learning → danger schemadevelopmentalAversive learning encodes threat beliefs; test via history.
Critical incident → activation → NATscausalAn event matching the schema switches it on. Onset tracks the incident.
Symptoms/behaviour/biases → NATsmaintainingEach confirms "danger", so the schema stays active. Test: dropping avoidance and refocusing attention reduces anxiety.
Model → treatmentTarget each maintaining element: behavioural experiments and exposure (behaviour), attention training and reducing threat-scanning (biases), and restructuring the danger appraisals (NATs/schema).

Panic Disorder

A · Introduction

Origin: cognitive model of panic (Clark, 1986; as presented by Wells, 1997). Addresses: panic attacks. Core assumption: panic results from the catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily or cognitive sensations (e.g., palpitations → "heart attack"), creating a rapid vicious cycle. Avoidance and safety behaviours — including selective attention to the body — maintain it.

B · Diagram

Cognitive model of panic (after Clark, 1986 / Wells, 1997)

Fig. Cognitive model of panic (after Clark, 1986; Wells, 1997).

Plain text: an internal/external trigger → perceived threat → anxiety, which drives physical/cognitive symptoms; those symptoms are catastrophically misinterpreted, which raises anxiety further — a tight vicious circle. Avoidance & safety behaviours (incl. selective attention to the body) feed back into both the misinterpretation and the symptoms, maintaining the disorder.

C · Components

Catastrophic misinterpretation (the pivot)
What: reading benign sensations (palpitations, dizziness, derealisation) as signs of imminent catastrophe (heart attack, collapse, madness, losing control). Significance: the engine of panic and the key target. Assess: "When your heart raced, what did you think it meant? What did you fear would happen?" What to write: "Misinterprets palpitations as 'I'm about to die'." Mistakes: reassuring about health repeatedly (acts as a safety behaviour) instead of testing the misinterpretation.
Physical / cognitive symptoms
What: the arousal sensations and frightening cognitions (unreality, racing thoughts). Significance: they are re-interpreted as further "proof" of threat. Assess: symptom inventory during an attack. What to write: "Palpitations, breathlessness, dizziness interpreted as danger."
Avoidance & safety behaviours (incl. selective attention)
What: sitting down, carrying water/medication, gripping objects, avoiding exertion/places, and scanning the body for symptoms. Significance: they prevent disconfirmation ("I only survived because I sat down") and selective attention amplifies normal sensations. maintaining Assess: "What do you do to stop the worst happening? What do you avoid? Do you monitor your body?" What to write: "Safety behaviours and body-scanning maintain the catastrophic belief."

D · Arrows

ArrowTypeWhy / test
Symptoms → misinterpretation → anxiety → symptomscausal vicious cycleCatastrophic meaning raises arousal, which intensifies sensations — a positive-feedback spiral. Test: inducing sensations (interoceptive exposure) without catastrophe disconfirms it.
Safety behaviours → misinterpretation/symptomsmaintainingThey block disconfirmation and amplify sensations. Test: dropping them reduces panic.
Model → treatmentCognitive therapy of panic: restructure the misinterpretation, use interoceptive exposure (deliberately bring on sensations) and behavioural experiments, and drop safety behaviours and body-scanning. This cognitive therapy is NICE-recommended as first-line.

Social Phobia

A · Introduction

Origin: cognitive model of social phobia (Clark & Wells, 1995; as presented by Wells, 1997). Addresses: social anxiety disorder. Core assumption: on entering a feared social situation the person's assumptions are activated, they perceive social danger, and they turn attention inward to process the self as a social object — judging themselves by a distorted internal image rather than by real feedback. Safety behaviours and somatic/cognitive symptoms maintain the fear.

B · Diagram

Cognitive model of social phobia (after Clark & Wells, 1995)

Fig. Cognitive model of social phobia (after Clark & Wells, 1995).

Plain text: a social situation activates assumptions → perceived social danger (NATs) → the person turns attention inward to processing the self as a social object (a distorted internal image), which is reciprocally linked to safety behaviours and somatic & cognitive symptoms. Those two loop back to the social situation itself (the person re-enters future situations more primed), preventing disconfirmation.

C · Components

Processing of self as a social object
What: attention shifts from the task/others onto an observer-perspective image of how bad one appears (blushing, shaking, "looking stupid"). Simple: you watch and judge yourself from the outside, using a distorted picture. Significance: the person judges themselves by this internal image, so positive external feedback never corrects it. Assess: "In that moment, were you more aware of the conversation or of yourself? Did you have an image of how you looked?" What to write: "Self-focused attention on a distorted 'red, foolish' self-image." Mistakes: missing that the self-image is distorted and taken as fact.
Safety behaviours
What: rehearsing sentences, avoiding eye contact, gripping a cup, saying little, over-preparing. Significance: they prevent disconfirmation and can themselves create the feared impression (e.g., appearing aloof). maintaining Assess: "What do you do to get through social situations?"
Somatic & cognitive symptoms + anticipatory/post-event processing
What: blushing, sweating, trembling, mind going blank; plus dread beforehand and a biased negative "post-mortem" afterwards. Significance: these fuel the perceived danger and lock in negative self-appraisal regardless of the actual outcome. Assess: "Do you dread it in advance? Do you replay it afterwards?"

D · Arrows

ArrowTypeWhy / test

Generalized Anxiety Disorder — metacognitive model

A · Introduction

Origin: Adrian Wells' metacognitive model of GAD. Addresses: chronic, uncontrollable worry. Core assumption: the problem is not the worry topics but beliefs about worry itself. A trigger activates positive meta-beliefs ("worry helps me cope/prepare"), prompting Type 1 worry (about external things). Then negative meta-beliefs ("my worry is uncontrollable and dangerous") switch on Type 2 worry (worry about worry = meta-worry), which drives counter-productive behaviour, thought-control attempts and emotion that keep the cycle going.

Obsessive–Compulsive Disorder — two cognitive models

The handout presents OCD through two complementary cognitive accounts: Salkovskis' model (built around inflated responsibility and the misinterpretation of intrusions) and Rachman's model (built around catastrophic misinterpretation, responsibility and thought–action fusion). Both agree the core problem is the appraisal of normal intrusive thoughts, not the intrusions themselves.

Salkovskis' Model

A · Introduction

Origin: Paul Salkovskis (cognitive model of OCD, 1985, 1999). Core assumption: intrusive thoughts, images, urges and doubts are universal and normal; OCD develops when early experiences and critical incidents activate assumptions/general beliefs (e.g., "not preventing disaster is as bad as making it happen"; "better safe than sorry") that lead the person to misinterpret the significance of their intrusions as implying personal responsibility for harm. Neutralising, attention/reasoning biases, counter-productive safety strategies and mood changes all feed back to keep the intrusions salient.

B · Diagram

Cognitive model of OCD (after Salkovskis)

Fig. Cognitive model of OCD (after Salkovskis).

Plain text: early experiences create vulnerability and a critical incident activates OCD-relevant assumptions/general beliefs → these shape how normal intrusive thoughts/images/urges/doubts are handled. The intrusions drive neutralizing actions and attention & reasoning biases, both feeding the central misinterpretation of the significance of intrusions (responsibility for harm), which in turn produces counter-productive safety strategies and mood changes. The dashed gold loops show all of these feeding back to make the intrusions more frequent and salient.

C · Components

Intrusive thoughts, images, urges, doubts
What: unwanted, ego-dystonic mental events (harm, contamination, taboo themes, doubts). Simple: the "mental hiccups" nearly everyone has. Significance: the content is not the problem — appraisal is. Assess: "What thoughts pop in uninvited? How do you react to them?" What to write: "Recurrent intrusive doubt about having left the gas on." Mistakes: treating the content as dangerous/diagnostic.
Assumptions / general beliefs
What: beliefs like "not preventing harm = causing it", "better safe than sorry", over-importance of thoughts. Significance: they set up the responsibility appraisal. Assess: "If you could prevent something bad and didn't, how responsible would you feel?" What to write: "Holds an inflated-responsibility assumption."
Misinterpretation of significance — responsibility for actions
What: appraising the intrusion as meaning "I am pivotally responsible for preventing harm." Significance: the core cognitive target; generates distress and the urge to neutralise. Assess: "What does it mean about you that you had that thought?" What to write: "Appraises intrusions via inflated responsibility." Mistakes: targeting the intrusion rather than the appraisal.
Neutralizing actions, safety strategies, biases & mood (maintaining)
What: rituals, reassurance, mental argument; thought suppression, "impossible criteria" for certainty, avoidance; threat-scanning; and distress/anxiety/depression. Significance: all give short-term relief but prevent disconfirmation and increase the frequency/salience of intrusions. maintaining Assess: "What do you do to feel safe or sure? Any mental rituals or reassurance-seeking?"

D · Arrows

ArrowTypeWhy / test
Critical incident → assumptionscausalAn event activates latent responsibility beliefs.
Assumptions → misinterpretation of intrusionscausalThe beliefs frame normal intrusions as significant/dangerous.
Neutralising/safety/biases/mood → intrusionsmaintainingEach sustains the appraisal and increases intrusions. Test: ERP + dropping reassurance reduces symptoms.
Model → treatment (ERP + cognitive work)If neutralising/avoidance maintain OCD by giving relief that blocks disconfirmation, then Exposure and Response Prevention — confronting the trigger while not neutralising — lets the person learn that anxiety subsides on its own and the feared catastrophe does not occur, directly disconfirming the responsibility appraisal. Cognitive work targets the inflated-responsibility belief itself.

Rachman's Model of OCD

A · Introduction

Origin: Stanley Rachman (cognitive theory of obsessions, 1997–98). Core assumption: obsessions arise from the catastrophic misinterpretation of the significance of intrusive thoughts as signalling threat — amplified by inflated responsibility and thought–action fusion. The misinterpretation increases attention/focus, internal anxiety sensations and the perceived range/seriousness of triggers, producing obsessions, more distress and neutralisation.

B · Diagram

Fig. Cognitive model of obsessions (after Rachman).

Plain text: a trigger stimulus → unwanted intrusive thoughts → catastrophic misinterpretation of their significance (THREAT), amplified on each side by responsibility and thought–action fusion. Thought–Action Fusion (TAF)
What: the belief that thinking something is morally equivalent to doing it (moral TAF) or that thinking it makes it more likely to happen (likelihood TAF). Simple: "having the thought is as bad as — or will cause — the act." Significance: a central amplifier that turns a fleeting intrusion into a moral/real threat. Assess: "If you think it, does that make you a bad person, or make it more likely to happen?" What to write: "Marked moral and likelihood TAF." Mistakes: providing reassurance that the thought won't come true (a neutralising move) instead of testing the belief.
Increased focus/attention, internal anxiety, widened range of triggers
What: the misinterpretation makes the person hyper-attentive to the thought, more physically anxious, and more likely to treat new, loosely-related stimuli as triggers. Significance: explains why OCD spreads over time. Assess: "Has the number of things that set it off grown?"
Obsessions → distress → neutralization (maintaining)
What: the escalating intrusions become full obsessions, distress rises, and neutralisation follows. Significance: neutralisation relieves distress briefly but reinforces the whole threat appraisal. maintaining

Depression — three complementary models

The handout presents depression through three lenses: Beck's cognitive theory (intra-psychic), Coyne's interpersonal model (the relational cycle), and a biopsychosocial formulation (integrating all levels in a specific case). Together they show the same condition from cognitive, interpersonal and integrative angles.

Beck's Cognitive Theory of Depression

A · Introduction

Origin: Aaron T. Beck (1967, 1979). Core assumption: early experience forms basic beliefs / core schemata and corresponding (often latent) assumptions and behavioural strategies; a critical incident activates these, producing negative automatic thoughts about the self, world and future (the negative cognitive triad) and the full depressive syndrome.

B · Diagram

Beck's cognitive model of depression

Fig. Beck's cognitive model of depression.

Plain text: early experiences form basic beliefs/core schemata → assumptions, rules and behavioural strategies (which can be latent, i.e. dormant until triggered) → typical negative automatic thoughts about self, world and future (the triad). A critical incident activates the latent chain, producing depressive symptoms across behavioural, affective, physiological, cognitive and motivational domains. Symptoms loop back to reinforce the negative thoughts.

C · Components

Negative cognitive triad (self / world / future)
What: globally negative views of the self ("I'm worthless"), the world/experience ("everything is pointless") and the future ("it will never improve"). Significance: the signature of depressive cognition; future-hopelessness is a key suicide-risk marker. Assess: thought records sampling all three domains; "How do you see yourself / your life / your future?" What to write: "Triad: 'I'm a failure / life is empty / nothing will change.'" Mistakes: treating hopelessness as fixed fact rather than a testable cognition.
Latent beliefs/assumptions & the critical incident
What: dormant schemata and rules that a matching life event switches on (e.g., loss activating "I'm only worthwhile if loved"). Significance: explains why depression often follows specific losses/failures. Assess: "What was happening when this started? What did the event mean to you?" What to write: "Redundancy activated a latent 'worth = achievement' assumption."
Depressive symptoms & the behavioural loop
What: the five-domain syndrome (behavioural withdrawal, low affect, physiological changes, negative thinking, loss of motivation). Significance: withdrawal reduces rewarding experiences, which confirms the triad — a maintaining loop. Assess: activity diary; symptom review. What to write: "Inactivity reduces reward and confirms 'nothing is worthwhile.'"

Critical incident → activation → NATs → symptomscausalA matching event switches on latent beliefs. Onset tracks the incident.
Symptoms → NATs (loop)maintainingWithdrawal/inactivity confirms the triad. Test: behavioural activation lifts mood and belief.
Model → treatmentBehavioural activation to break the withdrawal loop, cognitive restructuring of the triad and distortions, and (for recurrent depression) schema-level and relapse-prevention work.

Coyne's Interpersonal Model of Depression

A · Introduction

Origin: James Coyne (interpersonal theory of depression). Core assumption: depression is maintained in relationships. Depressive symptoms initially draw genuine support, but persistent reassurance-seeking becomes aversive to others, who grow ambivalent and give mixed or false reassurance; the depressed person detects this, feels rejected, and reassurance-seeks more — a self-defeating interpersonal spiral that generates real rejection and sustains the depression.

B · Diagram

Coyne's interpersonal model of depression

Fig. Coyne's interpersonal model of depression.

Plain text: a stressful event (esp. relationship loss) → depressive symptoms → others give genuine support (which reinforces the symptoms) → but ambiguity creeps in → the person continues symptoms seeking reassurance → this becomes aversive → others feel hostility/resentment. On the right, this generates perceived or actual rejection (the person interprets responses as rejection; others give excuses/false reassurance), which loops back to deepen the depression.

C · Key components

Excessive reassurance-seeking
What: repeatedly seeking reassurance that one is cared for/not a burden. Significance: the engine of the interpersonal cycle — it provides brief relief but erodes relationships over time. Assess: "Do you often check whether people still care? How do they respond over time?" What to write: "Reassurance-seeking initially elicits support but becomes aversive, generating rejection."
Aversiveness & others' ambivalence
What: others become ambivalent — offering support out of duty while feeling resentment, giving mixed/false reassurance. Significance: the depressed person detects the incongruity and feels rejected, confirming their negative self-view. Assess: collateral where possible; "Do people seem to pull back?"

Psychosis — clinical CBT model

A · Introduction

Origin: the clinical cognitive model used in CBT for psychosis (CBTp). Addresses: positive psychotic symptoms (hallucinations, delusions) and their consequences. Core assumption: internal (biological, psychological) and external (environmental) factors act through a mediating pathway of arousal-regulation and information-processing dysfunction to produce psychotic experiences; it is the appraisal of those experiences — not their mere presence — that determines distress, and short- and long-term consequences feed back to maintain the problem.

B · Diagram

A clinical model for CBT of psychosis

Fig. A clinical model used in CBT for psychosis.

Plain text: internal factors (biological; psychological, e.g. cognitive deficits/maladaptive attitudes) and external factors (environmental stressors; increased exposure to stressors) act through a mediating pathway (arousal & information-processing dysfunction) to produce psychotic experiences / positive symptoms. These undergo primary & secondary appraisal, generating short-term consequences (emotional, behavioural, cognitive, coping) and then long-term consequences (withdrawal, low reinforcement, disability, stigma). C · Components

Appraisal of the psychotic experience
What: the personal meaning given to a voice or unusual experience (e.g., "the voice is an all-powerful persecutor I must obey"). Significance: appraisal — not the experience itself — determines distress and disability; it is the main CBTp target. Assess: "What do you make of the voice? How powerful is it? Mediating pathway (arousal & information-processing)
What: heightened arousal and biased information processing (e.g., jumping to conclusions) that make anomalous experiences more likely and more threatening. Assess: stress/arousal triggers; reasoning style. What to write: "High arousal and a jump-to-conclusions bias mediate symptom onset."
Short- and long-term consequences (maintaining)
What: emotional distress, safety behaviours/avoidance, and over time withdrawal, lost opportunities, disability and stigma. Significance: these feed back (increased arousal, bias-confirming behaviour) to maintain the experiences. maintaining

A · Introduction

Origin: a cognitive-behavioural maintenance model of persistent physical symptoms without sufficient organic explanation (functional/somatic symptoms). Core assumption: real body experiences (from physical and psychological sources) are attended to and interpreted as illness/abnormal; the behavioural, emotional, social and cognitive responses to that interpretation, plus physiological changes, feed back to amplify the body experiences — a self-maintaining cycle, independent of whether a disease is present.

Personality Disorder — three models

The handout presents personality disorder (focusing on borderline presentations) through three complementary formulations: Linehan's biosocial developmental model (how it develops), the schema-therapy account (the maladaptive schemas and their domains), and the cognitive formulation (core/conditional schemas and over-/under-developed strategies).

Biosocial Developmental Model (Linehan)

A · Introduction

Origin: Marsha Linehan's biosocial developmental theory of borderline personality disorder. Core assumption: borderline difficulties develop from the ongoing transaction between an inherited emotional vulnerability (biological) and a high-risk, invalidating environment (social). Over time this produces pervasive emotion dysregulation; repeated maladaptive behaviours that regulate or avoid emotion become reinforcing and crystallise into the borderline pattern.

B · Diagram

Biosocial developmental model (after Linehan)

Fig. Biosocial developmental model of borderline personality disorder (after Linehan).

Plain text: biological vulnerabilities + child temperament and a high-risk invalidating environment enter a two-way transaction; inherited emotion-control deficits meet environmental reinforcement of emotional lability → increased risk for psychopathology → heightened emotion dysregulation → characteristic reactions to emotional situations → repetitive maladaptive behaviours that regulate/avoid emotion, become reinforcing, and repeat over time to crystallise into a borderline personality disorder pattern.

C · Components

Emotional vulnerability (biological) × invalidating environment (social)
What: the biological side = high sensitivity, high reactivity, slow return to baseline; the social side = an environment that dismisses/punishes/trivialises emotional experience. Significance: neither alone produces the disorder — it is their transaction over development. Assess: temperament history; "How were your emotions responded to growing up?" What to write: "Constitutional emotional vulnerability transacting with chronic invalidation."
Pervasive emotion dysregulation
What: inability to modulate intense, rapidly shifting emotions. Significance: the central mechanism underlying the behaviours. Assess: emotion-intensity and recovery patterns; chain analysis of episodes.
Repetitive maladaptive (emotion-regulating) behaviours
What: self-harm, impulsivity, dissociation, relationship turmoil — behaviours that regulate or avoid unbearable emotion. Significance: they "work" short-term, are reinforced, and become entrenched traits. maintaining What to write: "Self-harm functions to down-regulate arousal and is negatively reinforced."

Case 2 · Panic disorder (Clark/Wells)

Background: "A", 29. Presenting: sudden palpitations/breathlessness with "I'm having a heart attack"; avoids exertion; checks pulse, carries water.
Mapping: trigger → perceived threat → anxiety → symptoms → catastrophic misinterpretation → more anxiety (vicious circle); safety behaviours + body-scanning maintain it.
Formulation: "A misinterprets benign arousal sensations as cardiac catastrophe, driving a self-amplifying panic cycle; safety behaviours and selective attention to the body prevent disconfirmation."
Treatment: restructure the misinterpretation, interoceptive exposure, drop safety behaviours. Limitation: confirm cardiac work-up is clear; assess agoraphobic avoidance.

Case 3 · Depression via Beck + Coyne

Background: "R", 42, after redundancy, in a strained marriage. Presenting: low mood, anhedonia, withdrawal, reassurance-seeking.
Mapping (Beck): redundancy activated a latent "worth = achievement" assumption → negative triad (worthless self / pointless world / hopeless future) → withdrawal → fewer rewards → triad confirmed. Mapping (Coyne): reassurance-seeking initially drew support but became aversive, generating marital rejection that deepened the depression.
Formulation: "R's depression is maintained both intra-psychically (negative triad + inactivity loop) and interpersonally (reassurance-seeking → aversiveness → rejection)."
Treatment: behavioural activation + cognitive restructuring (Beck) and couple/interpersonal work reducing reassurance-seeking (Coyne). Limitation: rule out bipolarity and physical causes; monitor hopelessness/risk.

Case 4 · Borderline presentation via Linehan biosocial + schema

Background: "M", 23, high emotional sensitivity; invalidating, abusive childhood. Presenting: rapid mood shifts, self-harm, turbulent relationships, fear of abandonment.
Mapping (Linehan): constitutional emotional vulnerability × chronic invalidation → pervasive emotion dysregulation → self-harm functions to regulate unbearable arousal and is negatively reinforced. Mapping (schema): Disconnection & Rejection domain active — Mistrust/Abuse, Abandonment, Defectiveness/Shame.
Formulation: "M's difficulties reflect a biosocial transaction producing emotion dysregulation, overlaid with early-maladaptive schemas of abandonment, mistrust and shame that are reactivated in close relationships."
Treatment: DBT skills (emotion regulation, distress tolerance) + validation; schema-focused work on abandonment/defectiveness. Limitation: assess risk and trauma; sequence trauma work when stabilised.
