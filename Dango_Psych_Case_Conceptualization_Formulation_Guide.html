How to read this guide

A case formulation is a shared, testable explanation of why this person has these difficulties now, built to guide treatment. This guide explains the formulation frameworks and models used in the source case material and expands them with additional evidence-based models, each cited to its original author.

The arrow key used in every diagram solid orange → causal / leads to dashed gold ⟳ maintaining loop green ⇄ reciprocal blue ⇢ hypothesised / developmental
Every diagram is a high-definition flowchart with clean labelled nodes, and is followed by a plain-text description so the model is understandable even without the picture.
Sourcing of diagrams & honesty The case-formulation source document contains the 5 Ps, a narrative diagnostic formulation and the OCD/ERP rationale as text — it holds no ready-made CBT model diagrams. Published textbook figures of these models are copyrighted and cannot be embedded in an offline file. The diagrams here are therefore accurate, high-definition redrawings of the established published models, rendered as inline vector graphics so they display identically offline. All clinical cases are fictional educational illustrations; models are attributed to their original authors with verified years.

Source model

The diagnostic formulation (as used in the source case material)

A · Introduction

The diagnostic formulation is the integrative paragraph that closes a psychodiagnostic assessment. It is the model most directly present in the source documents: each case in the source draws together socio-demographic data, the history of present illness, mental status examination (MSE) findings, test results and the 5 Ps into a single coherent explanation that justifies a provisional diagnosis and a treatment rationale. It is the bridge between raw assessment data and a defensible clinical conclusion.

B · Diagram — how a diagnostic formulation is assembled

Building a diagnostic formulation

Fig. The diagnostic formulation integrates four data streams into one synthesis, which then yields both a provisional diagnosis and a treatment rationale.

Plain text: four inputs — socio-demographic + history, MSE findings, test results, and the 5 Ps — all feed (orange causal arrows) into an integrated formulation. That synthesis then produces two outputs: a provisional diagnosis (with the points in its favour) and a treatment rationale (with short- and long-term goals).

C · The parts of a diagnostic-formulation paragraph

Opening summary (who + what)
What: one or two sentences giving identity, demographics and the core presenting picture. Write (fictional): "The index patient, a 24-year-old unmarried woman of lower socioeconomic status, presented with repetitive washing, checking and intrusive distressing sexual images of ~4 years' duration, with insidious onset and a continuous course."
Onset, course & progression
What: when it began, how it has evolved (insidious/acute; continuous/episodic; static/worsening/improving). Significance: onset pattern narrows the differential (e.g., insidious + continuous favours a chronic disorder over an acute reaction).
Key MSE findings woven in
What: the positive signs that support the diagnosis — e.g., circumstantiality in stream of thought, obsessions/compulsions in thought possession, dysphoric affect, grade-3 insight, death wishes. Significance: anchors the formulation in observed data, not just report.
The 5 Ps stated explicitly
What: predisposing (e.g., early maternal loss, lack of parental support), precipitating (e.g., a specific trauma), perpetuating (e.g., family conflict, poor treatment compliance), protective (e.g., motivation, family support). Significance: this is the explanatory spine linking vulnerability → onset → maintenance.
Provisional diagnosis + points in favour
What: the ICD/DSM label plus the specific criteria met (duration/distress threshold; thoughts recognised as one's own and resisted; ego-dystonic/unpleasant content; which component predominates). Mistake: stating a label without listing the evidence that satisfies each criterion.
Treatment rationale + goals
What: why the chosen therapy follows from the formulation (e.g., ERP targets the ritual→relief→reinforcement cycle), with short-term goals (psychoeducation, hierarchy, response prevention) and long-term goals (durable symptom reduction, restored functioning, relapse prevention). Significance: this is where formulation becomes a plan.

D · The core relationship

Data → synthesis → conclusionThe defining logic is that the diagnosis and treatment plan are outputs of the integrated formulation, not inputs. You do not start with "OCD, therefore ERP"; you start with the data, synthesise why the picture holds together, and let the diagnosis and treatment fall out of that synthesis. This is the causal direction shown in the diagram.

General framework

The 5 Ps framework

A · Introduction

The 5 Ps organise everything you gather into five questions along a timeline. It is a scaffold, not a theory — it tells you which factors to collect, while a CBT/disorder model explains how they interact. It is the explanatory spine of the source diagnostic formulations.

B · Diagram

The 5 Ps across the lifespan

Fig. Predisposing → precipitating → perpetuating factors unfold along the timeline; protective factors buffer; together they explain the present problem.

Plain text: predisposing factors create vulnerability over the lifespan; a precipitating event triggers onset; perpetuating factors keep the problem going; protective factors buffer it (green = helpful/reciprocal); together these explain the presenting problem (blue = where it all converges).

C · Each P — meaning, what to assess, how to ask

PPlain meaningWhat to assessSample question
Presenting problemCurrent difficulties as the patient experiences themSymptoms, onset, course, severity, functional impact"What brought you here? When did it start and how has it changed?"
PredisposingBackground making the person vulnerableGenetics/family history, temperament, early adversity/loss, attachment, medical"What was your childhood like? Any losses, trauma or family history?"
PrecipitatingThe trigger around onsetRecent stressors, life events, losses, substance changes"What was happening just before this began?"
PerpetuatingWhat keeps it going now (treatment targets)Safety behaviours, avoidance, beliefs, reinforcement, relationships, poor compliance"What do you do to cope? What happens afterwards?"
ProtectiveStrengths and resources aiding recoverySupport, insight, motivation, skills, prior coping, values"What's helped before? Who supports you?"
Distinguishing overlapping PsClassify by timing & function. A broken relationship is precipitating if it triggered onset but perpetuating if ongoing conflict sustains the problem. Childhood abuse is usually predisposing but becomes precipitating if a recent reminder triggered this episode. Ask: did it cause the start, or does it maintain it now?
What to write"[Problem] is understood on a background of [predisposing], triggered by [precipitating], and maintained by [perpetuating]; recovery is supported by [protective]."

Interviewing & clinical-writing: from history to formulation

A formulation is built, not guessed. These ten steps move from raw history to a defensible, collaborative explanation that drives treatment.

1 · Collect presenting complaints & history
Gather the problem in the patient's words plus onset, course, frequency, duration, severity and functional impact. Ask: "Tell me what's troubling you. Walk me through a recent bad day." Write a problem list before theorising.
2 · Identify developmental & contextual factors
Early experiences, attachment, losses, trauma, family/medical history, culture. These seed core beliefs and vulnerability (predisposing).
3 · Identify triggers & maintaining processes
Find activating situations and the cycle: thought → feeling → behaviour → consequence. Safety behaviours and avoidance usually live here. Ask: "What sets it off? What do you do then? What happens next?"
4 · Assess strengths & protective factors
Supports, skills, motivation, insight, values, what has helped before — these become treatment assets.
5 · Develop tentative explanatory hypotheses
Choose a model that fits the data. State it as a hypothesis ("It may be that…"). Avoid premature certainty.
6 · Link hypotheses to assessment findings
Anchor every claim to evidence: history, MSE, questionnaire scores, observations. If you can't cite data for a link, flag it as hypothesised.
7 · Check alternatives & contradictory evidence
Actively seek disconfirmation — different disorder? organic cause? normal reaction? A good formulation survives this or is revised.
8 · Discuss the formulation collaboratively
Share it in plain language, often as a simple diagram. Ask: "Does this fit? What would you change?" Agreement builds the alliance.
9 · Revise as new information emerges
Formulation is a living hypothesis; update it as therapy reveals new triggers, beliefs or maintaining factors.
10 · Connect formulation to goals & interventions
Each maintaining factor becomes a target; each target suggests a technique (safety behaviours → behavioural experiments; avoidance → graded exposure).
Good vs poor formulation statements Poor: "Patient has OCD and needs ERP." (a diagnosis + a treatment, no explanation)
Good: "Intrusive images are appraised as meaning she is dangerous; washing reduces anxiety short-term but prevents her learning the thoughts are harmless, maintaining the cycle — so ERP targets this mechanism." (explains the mechanism and justifies the treatment)

Generic CBT formulation

The CBT model (Beck) — the full component map

A · Introduction

Author: Aaron T. Beck (cognitive model, 1960s–70s); developed into longitudinal (developmental) and cross-sectional (maintenance) formulations by Judith Beck and Jacqueline Persons. Addresses: a wide range of emotional disorders. Core assumption: it is not events themselves but the meaning a person gives them (their cognitions) that drives emotion and behaviour; meanings are shaped by early experience and organised into beliefs and schemas. Useful when you need a transdiagnostic developmental map; differs from disorder-specific models, which zoom in on a single maintaining cycle.

B · Diagram — longitudinal CBT formulation

Longitudinal CBT formulation (developmental chain + maintenance triangle)

Fig. Early experiences shape beliefs → rules → (on a trigger) automatic thoughts, which drive the emotion–physiology–behaviour maintenance triangle; behaviour feeds back to sustain the thoughts.

Plain text (top→bottom): orange = causal. Early experiences shape core beliefs/schemas (this first link is hypothesised, not proven), which generate assumptions/rules. A critical incident that breaks a rule activates the beliefs, producing automatic thoughts/images. These drive the maintenance triangle (emotion ⇄ physiology ⇄ behaviour). Dashed gold = maintaining loops: avoidance/safety behaviours feed back and intensify thoughts and feelings.

C · Every component explained (in depth)

Early experiences & developmental history

What it means: formative life events — parenting, attachment, losses, trauma, bullying, illness. Plain: the soil the beliefs grow from. Clinical significance: explains why this person is vulnerable to these beliefs. What to assess: attachment quality, adversity, the messages received about self/others/world. How: developmental history; "What were you told about yourself as a child? What stands out about growing up?" What to write: "Early maternal loss and later abuse plausibly contributed to beliefs of being unsafe and unworthy." Example (fictional): childhood abuse → belief "I am dirty/bad." Common mistakes: treating history as destiny — it informs but does not prove a belief; always corroborate with current evidence.

Core beliefs

What: absolute, global beliefs about self, others and world ("I am unlovable", "others are dangerous"). Plain: the deepest "bottom-line" statements. Significance: they bias interpretation of everything. Assess: downward-arrow ("if that were true, what would it mean about you?"). How: watch themes across situations; belief questionnaires. Write: "Core belief: 'I am worthless.'" Example: depression — "I'm a failure." Mistake: labelling a situational thought as a core belief — core beliefs are global and trait-like.

Schemas

What: broad cognitive–affective structures (knowledge + emotion + memory) that organise beliefs and guide processing. Plain: mental templates that filter experience. Significance: drive self-perpetuating patterns. Assess: recurring interpersonal/emotional patterns; schema questionnaires. Write: "Mistrust/abuse schema active in close relationships." Example: "defectiveness/shame" schema → hypersensitivity to criticism. Mistake: conflating schema (structure) with a single belief (content). Schema therapy (Young, 1990) is a later adaptation of Beck's model.

Intermediate beliefs: assumptions, rules, conditional beliefs

What: the "if…then…", "should/must" and "unless" rules linking core beliefs to daily life. Plain: coping strategies for a painful core belief. Significance: more accessible than core beliefs and often the first therapy target. Assess: "What rules do you live by? What has to happen for you to feel OK?" Write: "Assumption: 'If I'm not perfect, I'll be rejected.'" Example: social anxiety — "If I blush, people will think I'm weird." Mistake: missing the conditional structure — these are rules, not facts.

Triggers / activating situations

What: the specific internal or external events that activate the beliefs. Plain: the "switch". Significance: pinpoints where and when the problem fires — guides exposure design. Assess: situational analysis of recent episodes. How: "Describe the last time — where, who, what exactly?" Write: "Trigger: touching a door handle." Mistake: accepting a vague trigger ("stress") instead of a concrete situation.

Automatic thoughts, images & interpretations

What: rapid, involuntary appraisals (words or images) in a situation. Plain: the running commentary in your head. Significance: the proximal driver of emotion — the key CBT target. Assess: "What went through your mind right then? Any picture in your mind?" Write: "AT: 'I'm going to collapse.'" Mistake: recording the emotion ("I felt scared") instead of the thought that produced it.

Emotions & physiological responses

What: the felt affect (fear, shame, sadness) and bodily arousal (racing heart, nausea, tension). Plain: how body and mood react to the thought. Significance: physical sensations are often re-interpreted as further evidence, fuelling the loop (esp. panic). Assess: name emotions and rate 0–100; map body sensations. Write: "Anxiety 85/100; palpitations, breathlessness." Mistake: blurring emotion and thought — keep them separate.

Behavioural responses

What: what the person does (checking, washing, escaping, arguing, withdrawing). Plain: the action the feeling pushes for. Significance: where maintaining cycles usually operate and where change is observable. Assess: behavioural analysis; diaries. Write: "Washes hands for 10 min." Mistake: ignoring covert behaviours (mental rituals, reassurance-seeking).

Avoidance & safety behaviours

What: avoidance = staying away from feared situations; safety behaviours = actions done within a situation to prevent a feared catastrophe. Plain: escape hatches and "insurance". Significance: the engine of maintenance — short-term relief but they prevent disconfirmation, so fear never updates. Assess: "What do you do to stop the worst? What do you avoid?" Write: "Grips trolley to avoid 'collapsing'." Example: social anxiety — rehearsing sentences, avoiding eye contact. Mistake: missing subtle safety behaviours, which quietly sabotage exposure.

Coping strategies

What: broader efforts to manage distress (adaptive or maladaptive). Significance: distinguishes helpful coping (strengthen) from counter-productive coping (target). Assess: "What do you do to get through? Does it help long-term?" Write: "Uses alcohol to manage anxiety — short-term relief, long-term worsening." Mistake: assuming all coping is good.

Maintaining factors & feedback loops

What: the self-reinforcing cycles keeping the problem alive now. Significance: primary treatment targets (modifiable in the present). Assess: trace consequence → back to trigger. Write: "Washing reduces anxiety → negatively reinforces washing → strengthens the urge." Mistake: listing maintaining factors without showing the loop.

Short-term relief vs long-term consequences

What: the trade-off explaining why unhelpful behaviours persist — they work short-term but worsen the problem long-term. Significance: the core rationale you share to justify dropping safety behaviours. Write: "Avoidance brings immediate relief but maintains the belief the situation is dangerous."

Protective factors, strengths & resources

What: internal/external assets (insight, motivation, support, skills, values). Significance: predict prognosis; built into treatment. Assess: "What are you good at? Who helps? What's worked before?" Write: "Protective: strong therapeutic motivation; supportive father." Mistake: omitting strengths → a demoralising deficit-only formulation.

E · Clinical application

Fill the chain downward, then draw the maintaining loops. Translate each box into a statement and each loop into a treatment target. Different CBT approaches use different formulations — do not force every component into every case; a simple maintenance cycle suits a focal problem, while a longitudinal map suits complex or recurrent presentations.

Generic CBT formulation

The maintenance (vicious) cycle — "hot cross bun"

The cross-sectional "hot cross bun" (Greenberger & Padesky, 1995) is the simplest, most widely used formulation: in any situation, thoughts, emotions, physical sensations and behaviour interact and feed back on one another. It is the engine inside every disorder-specific model below.

The hot cross bun maintenance cycle

Fig. All four corners interact reciprocally; breaking any one corner weakens the whole cycle.

Plain text: a thought triggers emotion and physical arousal, which drive a behaviour, which feeds back to confirm the thought. The green reciprocal arrows mean every corner influences every other. Disorder-specific models simply specify what fills each corner.

Fictional mini-example — panicHeart races on stairs → Thought: "I'm having a heart attack" → Physical: palpitations intensify → Emotion: terror → Behaviour: stops, grips rail → the relief "confirms" that stopping prevented disaster → next time the belief is stronger.

Generic CBT formulation

The Five Areas model (Williams)

A · Introduction

Author: Chris Williams (Five Areas assessment model, widely used in UK primary-care and self-help CBT). Key idea: it extends the hot cross bun by adding a crucial fifth area — life situation / relationships / practical problems — recognising that external circumstances interact with the four internal areas. It is deliberately accessible, making it ideal for sharing a formulation with patients.

B · Diagram

The Five Areas

Fig. Five interacting areas — the fifth (life situation) distinguishes this from the four-part hot cross bun.

Plain text: the person's life situation feeds into thoughts, which interact reciprocally (green arrows) with emotions, physical symptoms and behaviour; behaviour loops back to affect the life situation. Any area can be an entry point for change.

Why it mattersAdding "life situation" prevents an overly intra-psychic formulation and reminds the clinician that practical and relationship changes (not just thought-challenging) can break the cycle — useful in low-intensity CBT and guided self-help.

Disorder-specific

OCD — Salkovskis & Rachman, and the ERP rationale

A · Introduction

Authors: Paul Salkovskis (cognitive-behavioural model, 1985, 1989, 1999) and Stanley Rachman (1997, 1998). Addresses: obsessive–compulsive disorder. Key idea: intrusive thoughts are normal and near-universal; OCD develops not from the intrusions themselves but from the catastrophic appraisal of them — especially inflated responsibility (Salkovskis: "if I might prevent harm, I'm to blame if I don't act") and thought–action fusion (Rachman: thinking something is morally equivalent to doing it, or makes it more likely). Wells & Matthews (1994) later added a metacognitive account (beliefs about thoughts, e.g. "I must control every thought"). Differs from earlier purely behavioural accounts by inserting the appraisal step between intrusion and distress.

B · Diagram — the OCD maintenance cycle

The cognitive-behavioural cycle of OCD

Fig. A normal intrusion is catastrophically appraised → anxiety → ritual → relief; relief negatively reinforces the ritual and blocks disconfirmation, so the cycle strengthens.

Plain text: a normal intrusive thought is appraised as meaning "I'm responsible for harm" or "thinking it makes it real" → anxiety rises → the person performs a compulsion / avoidance / reassurance / mental ritual → relief. The dashed gold loop shows relief negatively reinforces the ritual and prevents disconfirmation, so appraisals and intrusions strengthen over time.

C · Components (in depth)

Intrusive thought / image
What: an unwanted, ego-dystonic thought, urge or image (harm, contamination, taboo sexual/religious content). Plain: a "mental hiccup" that most people experience. Significance: the content is not the problem — studies show ~90% of people have such intrusions. Assess: "What thoughts pop in uninvited? How do you react to them?" Write: "Recurrent intrusive image of harming a relative, experienced as repugnant." Mistake: treating the content as dangerous or diagnostic.
Catastrophic appraisal — inflated responsibility & thought–action fusion
What: the meaning given to the intrusion. Inflated responsibility = feeling pivotally responsible for preventing harm. Thought–action fusion = moral TAF ("thinking it is as bad as doing it") and likelihood TAF ("thinking it makes it more likely"). Significance: the appraisal — not the intrusion — generates distress and drives rituals; the main cognitive target. Assess: "What does it mean about you that you had that thought? What might happen if you ignore it?" Write: "Appraises intrusions via inflated responsibility and moral TAF." Mistake: targeting the intrusion rather than the appraisal.
Anxiety / distress
What: the emotional surge driven by the appraisal. Assess: SUDS (0–100). Write: "Peak distress 90/100 on contamination exposure." Significance: the discomfort the ritual is designed to reduce.
Compulsion · neutralising · avoidance · reassurance · mental rituals
What: overt (washing, checking) and covert (mental reviewing, counting, praying, reassurance-seeking) acts to reduce distress or "prevent" harm. Significance: the maintaining behaviour and the ERP target. Assess: "What do you do to feel safe/sure? How long? Any mental rituals?" Write: "Washes 10 min; mentally reviews conversations; seeks reassurance from father." Mistake: missing covert rituals and reassurance-seeking — a common ERP failure point.
Temporary relief (negative reinforcement)
What: the short-lived drop in anxiety after ritualising. Significance: relief rewards the ritual (negative reinforcement) and blocks learning that anxiety would fall on its own and the catastrophe would not occur. Write: "Relief after washing negatively reinforces the compulsion, maintaining the cycle."

D · Arrows

ArrowTypeWhy / how tested
Intrusion → appraisalcausalThe intrusion is interpreted through responsibility/TAF beliefs. Test: non-OCD controls have the same intrusions without distress — supporting the model.
Appraisal → anxietycausalCatastrophic meaning generates distress; reducing the appraisal reduces anxiety.
Anxiety → compulsioncausal / motivationalThe urge to neutralise is driven by distress.
Relief → appraisal (loop)maintainingRelief negatively reinforces ritualising and prevents disconfirmation. Test: ERP breaks the loop → symptoms fall.

E · From formulation to ERP

Why ERP follows from the formulationIf rituals/avoidance maintain OCD by producing relief that prevents disconfirmation, the logical treatment is to confront the trigger (Exposure) while not performing the ritual (Response/Ritual Prevention). The patient then learns experientially that (a) anxiety rises, peaks and falls on its own (habituation / inhibitory learning) and (b) the feared catastrophe does not occur — directly disconfirming the appraisal. Treatment follows from this mechanism, not from the diagnostic label alone.
Sample ERP-building questions
  • "If you didn't wash/check, what's the worst you fear would happen?"
  • "On 0–100, how distressing is touching this / resisting that?"
  • "What's the smallest step that still raises some anxiety?"
Common mistakesChallenging the content of intrusions (reassurance in disguise); allowing covert rituals during exposure; building a hierarchy without eliciting the feared consequence; using relaxation during exposure as a safety behaviour (keep it a between-session skill).

Disorder-specific

Panic — Clark's cognitive model (1986)

A · Introduction

Author: David M. Clark (1986, 1988). Addresses: panic disorder. Key idea: panic attacks result from the catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily sensations (palpitations → "heart attack"; dizziness → "I'm going mad"). Differs from general anxiety models by specifying an internal (interoceptive) vicious cycle driven by misinterpretation rather than external threat. NICE-recommended as first-line.

B · Diagram — the panic vicious cycle

Clark's cognitive model of panic

Fig. A rapid positive-feedback loop: sensations are catastrophically misinterpreted, raising threat and arousal, which intensifies the sensations.

Plain text (clockwise): trigger → perceived threat → apprehension → bodily sensations → catastrophic misinterpretation → which feeds back to raise perceived threat again, escalating into a panic attack. All causal.

C · Key components & D · arrows

Body sensations → catastrophic misinterpretation (the pivot)
What: normal arousal sensations (palpitations, breathlessness, dizziness) read as signs of imminent catastrophe (heart attack, suffocation, madness, loss of control). Significance: the pivot of the model and the main cognitive target. Assess: "When your heart raced, what did you think it meant? What did you fear would happen?" Write: "Misinterprets palpitations as 'I'm about to die'." causal — remove the misinterpretation and the loop cannot escalate.
Safety behaviours & avoidance (maintaining)
What: sitting down, carrying water/medication, gripping objects, avoiding exertion/places. Significance: they prevent disconfirmation ("I only survived because I sat down"). maintaining Treatment link: drop safety behaviours + interoceptive exposure (deliberately induce sensations) to learn they are harmless.

E · Application

Model → treatmentCognitive therapy targets the misinterpretation (behavioural experiments; interoceptive exposure — e.g., over-breathing or spinning to show sensations are benign) and removes safety behaviours.

Disorder-specific

Social anxiety — Clark & Wells model (1995)

A · Introduction

Authors: David M. Clark & Adrian Wells (1995). Addresses: social anxiety disorder (social phobia). Key idea: in feared social situations the person shifts attention inward onto a distorted negative self-image ("processing the self as a social object"), deploys safety behaviours, and engages in anticipatory and post-event processing — all of which prevent disconfirmation of feared beliefs. NICE-recommended as first-line.

B · Diagram

Clark & Wells cognitive model of social anxiety

Fig. Attention turns inward to a distorted self-image; safety behaviours and anticipatory/post-event processing prevent disconfirming feedback.

Plain text: a social situation activates assumptions → perceived danger → the person turns attention inward (distorted self-image) and deploys safety behaviours. Reciprocal loops (anticipatory worry before; rumination after) feed the assumptions, so real social evidence never corrects the fear.

C · Key components

Self-focused attention & distorted self-image
What: attention shifts from the task/others onto monitoring one's own anxiety and an "observer-perspective" image of how bad one looks. Significance: the person judges themselves by this internal image, not external reality, so social feedback can't correct it. Assess: "In that moment, were you more aware of the conversation or of yourself? Any image of how you appeared?" Treatment link: attention training + video feedback to correct the self-image.
Safety behaviours
What: rehearsing sentences, gripping a glass, avoiding eye contact, speaking little. Significance: prevent the feared catastrophe in the mind, so disconfirmation never happens; some even cause the feared outcome (appearing aloof). maintaining
Anticipatory & post-event processing
What: dread/rehearsal before; biased negative review ("rumination") after. Significance: both lock in negative self-appraisal regardless of what actually happened. maintaining

E · Application

Model → treatmentReverse each maintaining process: shift attention outward, drop safety behaviours, use video/behavioural experiments to update the distorted self-image, cut anticipatory/post-event processing.

Disorder-specific

GAD — intolerance of uncertainty & metacognition

A · Introduction

Two complementary, evidence-based accounts of generalised anxiety disorder (chronic worry):

  • Dugas, Ladouceur & colleagues (1998) — the intolerance-of-uncertainty (IU) model: people with GAD find uncertainty intensely aversive and worry to "prepare for" or eliminate it, aided by positive beliefs about worry, negative problem orientation and cognitive avoidance.
  • Wells (1995) metacognitive model — distinguishes Type 1 worry (about external events) from Type 2 worry ("worry about worry", e.g. "my worrying is uncontrollable and harmful"). Negative metacognitive beliefs maintain GAD.

B · Diagram (metacognitive)

Wells' metacognitive model of GAD

Fig. Type 1 worry triggers negative metabeliefs (Type 2 worry), driving counter-productive coping that paradoxically increases worry.

Plain text: a "what if" trigger starts Type 1 worry; this activates negative metacognitive beliefs ("I can't control my worry; it will harm me") = Type 2 worry → counter-productive coping (suppression, avoidance, reassurance) that feeds back and increases worry.

Model → treatmentIU model → uncertainty-focused CBT (worry exposure, problem-solving training, experiments tolerating uncertainty). Metacognitive therapy → modify metacognitive beliefs and use detached mindfulness, rather than challenging each worry's content.

Disorder-specific

Depression — Beck's cognitive triad (+ behavioural loop)

A · Introduction

Author: Aaron T. Beck (1967, 1979). Key idea: depression is maintained by the negative cognitive triad — persistently negative views of the self, the world/experience and the future — sustained by negative schemas and systematic cognitive distortions. A complementary behavioural account (Lewinsohn; Martell's behavioural activation) emphasises loss of response-contingent positive reinforcement.

B · Diagram

Beck's cognitive triad & the withdrawal loop

Fig. The triad drives low mood and withdrawal; reduced activity means fewer rewards, which loops back to "confirm" the triad.

Plain text: the negative triad (self/world/future) drives low mood → withdrawal and inactivity → fewer rewarding experiences → feeds back to confirm the triad. This loop is why behavioural activation and cognitive restructuring are the twin targets.

C · Components

Negative cognitive triad
Assess: thought records sampling views of self, experience and future. Write: "Triad: 'I'm a failure / life is empty / it won't get better.'" Mistake: treating hopelessness as fixed fact rather than a testable cognition. (Hopelessness is a key suicide-risk marker — formulate risk explicitly.)
Cognitive distortions
All-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralisation, mental filter, disqualifying the positive, catastrophising, personalisation — they manufacture triad-consistent "evidence". Treatment link: identify and test with Socratic questioning and behavioural experiments.
Withdrawal / inactivity loop
maintaining Reduced activity → fewer pleasure/mastery experiences → more negative cognition. Treatment link: behavioural activation directly breaks this loop and often precedes cognitive work.
Model → treatmentBehavioural activation (scheduling activity/mastery) + cognitive restructuring of the triad and distortions; for recurrent depression, schema-level work and relapse prevention (incl. mindfulness-based cognitive therapy).

Disorder-specific

PTSD — Ehlers & Clark cognitive model (2000)

A · Introduction

Authors: Anke Ehlers & David M. Clark (2000). Key idea: PTSD persists when the person processes the trauma in a way that produces a sense of serious current threat, arising from (1) negative appraisals of the trauma and its aftermath and (2) a poorly elaborated, disjointed trauma memory that is strongly cue-driven. Maintained by maladaptive coping (avoidance, rumination, safety behaviours). NICE first-line (with EMDR as an alternative).

B · Diagram

Ehlers & Clark cognitive model of PTSD

Fig. A disjointed trauma memory + negative appraisals create a sense of current threat; coping strategies loop back to block recovery.

Plain text: a disjointed trauma memory and negative appraisals both generate a sense of serious current threat → re-experiencing, hyperarousal and maladaptive coping. Maintaining loops: avoidance/rumination/safety behaviours prevent memory elaboration and appraisal change, so the threat sense persists.

C · Key components

Nature of the trauma memory
What: poorly contextualised in time/place, fragmented, easily triggered by sensory cues → intrusions feel like "happening now" (lack of time-coding). Treatment link: imaginal reliving / updating to elaborate and contextualise the memory.
Negative appraisals
What: of the event ("it was my fault"), of initial symptoms ("I'm going mad"), of others' responses. Treatment link: cognitive restructuring of appraisals.
Maladaptive coping (maintaining)
maintaining Thought suppression, avoidance, rumination, safety behaviours — block natural recovery and memory updating. Treatment link: drop these; stimulus discrimination ("then vs now").
Model → treatmentTrauma-focused CBT: reliving/updating the memory, restructuring appraisals, discriminating trauma cues from the safe present, dropping avoidance/rumination.

Disorder-specific

Health anxiety — Warwick & Salkovskis (1990)

A · Introduction

Authors: Hilary Warwick & Paul Salkovskis (1990). Key idea: health anxiety is driven by the catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily signs and medical information as evidence of serious illness, maintained by checking, reassurance-seeking and avoidance — structurally a panic-type model over a longer time-course.

B · Diagram

The health-anxiety maintenance cycle

Fig. A bodily sign is misinterpreted as serious illness; checking/reassurance/avoidance give brief relief but increase body-focus and sensitise to the next sign.

Plain text: a bodily sign is misinterpreted as serious illness → anxiety rises → the person checks, seeks reassurance and avoids, increasing body-focus with only brief relief → sensitised to the next sign. All causal, forming a self-perpetuating loop.

C · Components

Reassurance-seeking (maintaining)
maintaining Reassurance works like a compulsion: relief is brief and teaches the person they need reassurance, so doubt returns stronger. Treatment link: reduce reassurance-seeking + behavioural experiments (treat like ERP).
Body checking & increased interoceptive focus
Repeated checking (palpating, measuring) creates real sensations (soreness, redness) that "confirm" illness. Treatment link: drop checking; test the effect of attention on sensations.
Model → treatmentCBT reattributes sensations to benign causes, cuts checking/reassurance/avoidance, uses behavioural experiments — not repeated medical investigation, which feeds the cycle.

Disorder-specific

Substance use — Marlatt & Gordon relapse-prevention model (1985)

A · Introduction

Authors: G. Alan Marlatt & Judith Gordon (1985); the cognitive model of substance use (Beck et al., 1993) is complementary. Key idea: relapse is a process: a high-risk situation + poor coping → lowered self-efficacy and positive outcome expectancies → lapse → the abstinence violation effect (guilt + "I've blown it" thinking) → full relapse.

B · Diagram

Marlatt & Gordon relapse-prevention model

Fig. A high-risk situation forks: effective coping maintains abstinence; poor coping + positive expectancies lead to a lapse, which the abstinence violation effect converts into full relapse.

Plain text: a high-risk situation branches. With effective coping, self-efficacy rises and abstinence is maintained. With poor coping + positive expectancies, a lapse occurs; the abstinence violation effect (guilt, all-or-nothing collapse) turns a single lapse into full relapse. All causal/branching.

Model → treatmentRelapse prevention: identify high-risk situations, build coping skills, challenge outcome expectancies, reframe a lapse as a learning event (defuse the AVE), and use cue-exposure / urge-surfing for craving.

Disorder-specific · added model

Psychosis — cognitive models (Garety et al. 2001; Morrison 2001)

A · Introduction

Authors: Philippa Garety, Elizabeth Kuipers, David Fowler, Daniel Freeman & Paul Bebbington (2001; Kuipers et al. 2006); Anthony Morrison (2001). Addresses: positive symptoms of psychosis (delusions, hallucinations). Key idea: psychotic experiences lie on a continuum with normal experience; distress and disability arise from the appraisal of anomalous experiences (Morrison: the misinterpretation of intrusions as external/threatening), shaped by reasoning biases (jumping to conclusions), emotional processes, and negative schemas about self and others. CBT for psychosis (CBTp) targets appraisals and responses, not the mere presence of symptoms.

B · Diagram

Cognitive model of positive symptoms of psychosis

Fig. Anomalous experiences (on a continuum with normal experience) become distressing through appraisal, shaped by reasoning biases; safety behaviours maintain the belief.

Plain text: vulnerability + life events and an anomalous experience lead to an appraisal (is it external? personal? threatening?); reasoning biases such as jumping to conclusions (dashed blue = contributing) feed the appraisal. A threatening appraisal produces distress and the delusion/hallucination, prompting safety behaviours/avoidance that loop back to prevent disconfirmation.

Model → treatmentCBTp (NICE-recommended alongside medication): normalise experiences, gently test the appraisal/belief with guided discovery and behavioural experiments, reduce safety behaviours and distress — the aim is reduced distress and disability, not necessarily elimination of the experience.
CautionCBT for psychosis is an adjunct to, not a replacement for, pharmacological and multidisciplinary care; formulation must integrate biological and social factors.

Transdiagnostic · added model

Barlow's triple-vulnerability model of emotional disorders

A · Introduction

Author: David H. Barlow (2000, 2002). Addresses: the shared aetiology of anxiety and mood ("emotional") disorders — a transdiagnostic model that complements the disorder-specific ones above and underpins the Unified Protocol. Key idea: three vulnerabilities combine to produce an emotional disorder; no single one is sufficient.

B · Diagram

The three vulnerabilities

Fig. A general biological + general psychological vulnerability set the stage; a specific psychological vulnerability (learning) channels it into a particular disorder.

Plain text: all three causally converge on an emotional disorder. (1) General biological vulnerability (heritable temperament/neuroticism) + (2) general psychological vulnerability (a sense of low control over threats and emotions) create broad proneness; (3) a specific psychological vulnerability (e.g., early learning that focuses anxiety on bodily sensations, social evaluation or intrusive thoughts) determines which disorder develops — e.g., thought–action fusion channels it toward OCD, interoceptive focus toward panic.

Why it's useful in formulationIt explains high comorbidity (shared general vulnerabilities) and justifies transdiagnostic treatment (the Unified Protocol) that targets neuroticism and emotion regulation across disorders, rather than one protocol per diagnosis.

Worked examples

Seven worked fictional cases

All cases are invented for teaching only — not real patients or research data.

Case 1 · OCD (Salkovskis/Rachman + ERP) — closest to the source case

Background: "Sapna", 24, unmarried; early maternal loss and later sexual assault in adolescence.
Presenting: repetitive washing/bathing/cleaning, checking (gas, belongings), intrusive distressing sexual images of the people who assaulted her, avoidance of eye contact and of leaving home; low mood, reduced sleep/libido; passive death wishes. Y-BOCS = 27 (severe).
Model & why: cognitive-behavioural OCD model — intrusions appraised via inflated responsibility / thought–action fusion ("having this image means I'm bad/dangerous"), neutralised by washing/checking/avoidance/reassurance.
Formulation: "Intrusive images and contamination thoughts are appraised catastrophically, producing intense anxiety. Washing, checking, avoidance and reassurance reduce anxiety short-term but negatively reinforce the rituals and prevent her learning the thoughts are harmless, maintaining OCD. Predisposed by early loss and lack of support; precipitated by the assault; perpetuated by rituals, family conflict and poor compliance. Protective: strong motivation, supportive father."
Every component/arrow: intrusion → appraisal (causal) → anxiety (causal) → ritual (causal) → relief → back to appraisal (maintaining loop).
Treatment: ERP targeting the maintaining rituals (graded exposure + ritual prevention, including mental rituals and reassurance), psychoeducation (thought ≠ action ≠ identity), relaxation as a between-session skill, relapse prevention. Risk: monitor death wishes/hopelessness each session.
Alternatives / missing info / limits: screen for co-occurring PTSD given the assault history — if trauma re-experiencing dominates, trauma-focused work may be needed; distinguish ego-dystonic obsessions from trauma intrusions.

Case 2 · Depression (Beck's model)

Background: "Ravi", 42, accountant, recent redundancy. Presenting: 3 months of low mood, anhedonia, insomnia, hopelessness, withdrawal, passive "better off not here" thoughts. Assessment: PHQ-9 = 19; near-total inactivity; thought record "I'm useless; no one will hire me; it'll never improve."
Model & why: Beck — clear negative triad + withdrawal loop. Formulation: "Depression organised around a negative triad (worthless self, hopeless future, empty world) activated by redundancy against a backdrop of self-worth contingent on work. Low mood → withdrawal → fewer rewards → confirms the triad. Hopelessness elevates suicide risk."
Treatment: behavioural activation first, then cognitive restructuring; explicit risk monitoring. Alternatives/limits: rule out bipolar (past hypomania?), thyroid, adjustment disorder; collateral and risk detail needed.

Case 3 · Panic disorder (Clark 1986)

Background: "Aisha", 29, teacher. Presenting: sudden attacks of palpitations, breathlessness, dizziness with "I'm having a heart attack"; now avoids the gym and travelling alone. Assessment: normal cardiac work-up; attacks peak ~10 min; safety behaviours = carries water, sits down, checks pulse.
Formulation: "Aisha misinterprets benign arousal (palpitations) as a heart attack; perceived threat heightens arousal, intensifying sensations in a rapid vicious cycle. Safety behaviours and avoidance prevent her learning the sensations are harmless, maintaining the disorder."
Treatment: restructure the misinterpretation; interoceptive exposure; drop safety behaviours. Alternatives/limits: exclude cardiac/thyroid pathology and caffeine; assess agoraphobic avoidance.

Case 4 · Social anxiety (Clark & Wells 1995)

Background: "Tom", 21, student. Presenting: intense fear of seminars/conversations; fears blushing/being judged "boring"; avoids speaking, rehearses sentences, leaves early; ruminates for hours afterward; reports an "observer image" of himself looking red and foolish.
Formulation: "Assumptions ('if I seem anxious I'll be rejected') create perceived danger; Tom turns attention onto a distorted self-image and uses safety behaviours, which prevent disconfirmation, while anticipatory worry and post-event rumination lock in negative self-appraisal regardless of actual outcome."
Treatment: attention training, video feedback to update the self-image, behavioural experiments dropping safety behaviours, reducing pre/post processing. Alternatives/limits: differentiate avoidant personality pattern and autism-spectrum social difficulty; assess depression.

Case 5 · PTSD (Ehlers & Clark 2000)

Background: "Meera", 34, 6 months after a road-traffic collision. Presenting: intrusive sensory flashbacks, nightmares, hypervigilance; avoids driving and the crash road; "I've changed forever". Assessment: fragmented, cue-driven memory with "then vs now" confusion; rumination about "why me".
Formulation: "A poorly elaborated trauma memory plus negative appraisals ('the world is dangerous', 'I'm permanently damaged') generate a sense of current threat → re-experiencing and hyperarousal. Avoidance and rumination block memory elaboration and appraisal change, maintaining PTSD."
Treatment: trauma-focused CBT — reliving/updating the memory, restructuring appraisals, stimulus discrimination, dropping avoidance/rumination. Alternatives/limits: screen for depression, complicated grief, head injury; check timing (acute stress vs PTSD).

Case 6 · Complex presentation via 5 Ps + biopsychosocial

Background: "Daniel", 48, chronic low mood + harmful alcohol use + marital breakdown — a 5 Ps/biopsychosocial map first.
5 Ps: Presenting — depression, daily drinking, conflict. Predisposing — family history of depression/alcohol (bio), childhood emotional neglect → "I'm unlovable" (psycho). Precipitating — wife's departure. Perpetuating — alcohol numbs distress short-term but worsens mood/sleep and conflict (loop); withdrawal; "there's no point". Protective — employed, good relationship with daughter, help-seeking.
Biopsychosocial: Bio — genetic loading, alcohol effects on sleep/mood; Psycho — unlovability belief, avoidant coping; Social — marital loss, isolation.
Formulation: "Depression and alcohol use are mutually maintaining: low mood prompts drinking for relief, which deepens depression and fuels conflict, confirming his belief he is unlovable; vulnerability was activated by marital breakdown."
Treatment: integrated — alcohol (relapse-prevention model) + mood (behavioural activation + cognitive work) together; rebuild support. Alternatives/limits: assess dependence severity and whether mood is substance-induced; sequence accordingly; risk assessment.

Case 7 · Distressing voices (cognitive model of psychosis)

Background: "Imran", 26, hears a critical male voice daily for 18 months, alongside stable antipsychotic treatment. Presenting: appraises the voice as an all-powerful persecutor he must obey; isolates himself and performs rituals to "keep it happy"; low mood.
Model & why: Garety/Morrison cognitive model — the appraisal of the anomalous experience (not its mere presence) drives distress; safety behaviours maintain the belief in the voice's power.
Formulation: "Against a background of adversity and high arousal, Imran experiences an anomalous perception (a voice) which he appraises as an omnipotent persecutor. This appraisal — supported by a jumping-to-conclusions bias and beliefs about his own powerlessness — produces distress and compliance. Isolation and appeasement rituals (safety behaviours) prevent him discovering the voice has no real power, maintaining the belief."
Treatment: CBTp as an adjunct to medication — normalise voice-hearing, gently test beliefs about the voice's power/identity via guided discovery and behavioural experiments, reduce safety behaviours, treat low mood; aim at reduced distress and disability. Alternatives/limits: integrate biological/social factors; ensure multidisciplinary care; assess command-hallucination risk explicitly.

Describing boxes, not arrowsLists factors without explaining interaction — no mechanism, no treatment logicExplain every link: causal, reciprocal, maintaining or hypothesised
