What this guide covers & how to use it

This guide explains how clinicians build a case formulation — a shared, testable explanation of why a person has the difficulties they have, right now, that guides treatment. It is built around the formulation material in the source repository (a worked OCD case using the 5 Ps, diagnostic formulation and an ERP treatment plan) and expanded with the established, evidence-based models a clinician is expected to know, each cited to its original author.

How to read the diagrams Every diagram uses a consistent arrow key so you can read the theory, not just admire the picture: solid orange → causal / leads to dashed gold ⟳ maintaining loop green ⇄ reciprocal blue ⇢ hypothesised
Each diagram is followed by a plain-text description so the model is understandable even if the SVG does not render.

Scope & honesty All clinical cases in this guide are fictional educational illustrations — not research data. Models are attributed to their original authors with verified years; later adaptations are labelled as such. Established theories are explained in original wording (no verbatim textbook reproduction).

Foundations

Key terminology — what overlaps, what differs

These words are often used loosely. They coexist in real clinical work but are not interchangeable.

Core terms compared
TermWhat it answersKey feature
Psychiatric diagnosisWhat disorder label fits?Categorises a cluster of symptoms against ICD-11/DSM-5 criteria. Descriptive, not explanatory.
Case formulationWhy this person, this problem, now?An individualised, testable hypothesis linking predisposing, precipitating and maintaining factors to the presentation.
Case conceptualizationWhy + the fuller cognitive-behavioural mapOften used interchangeably with formulation; in CBT it tends to denote the broader developmental map (beliefs, schemas) within which the problem sits.
Therapeutic (shared) formulationHow do we understand this together?The version co-created and discussed with the patient to guide therapy and build the alliance.
Treatment planningWhat will we do about it?Turns the formulation's maintaining factors into goals, targets and specific interventions.
Risk formulationWhat harm, to whom, when, and under what conditions?A specific formulation of risk (nature, severity, imminence, triggers, protective factors) that feeds a risk-management plan.
Worked example — how they coexist (fictional) A 24-year-old woman presents with repetitive handwashing and intrusive distressing sexual images.
  • Diagnosis: Obsessive–compulsive disorder (mixed obsessional thoughts & acts).
  • Formulation: intrusive images are appraised as meaning "I am bad/dangerous" (inflated responsibility); washing and avoidance reduce anxiety short-term but prevent disconfirmation, maintaining the disorder — predisposed by early loss and adversity, precipitated by a specific stressor, perpetuated by rituals and low mood.
  • Treatment plan: ERP targeting the maintaining rituals; psychoeducation; relapse prevention.
  • Risk formulation: passive death wishes present; risk rises with symptom severity and hopelessness; protective factors = family support, help-seeking — monitored each session.
The same patient has one diagnosis, one formulation, one treatment plan and one risk formulation — each doing a different job.

General frameworks: the 5 Ps & the biopsychosocial model

The 5 Ps framework

The 5 Ps organise everything you gather into five questions. It is a scaffold, not a theory — it tells you which factors to collect, while a CBT/disorder model explains how they interact.

Plain text: Predisposing factors create vulnerability over the lifespan → a precipitating event triggers onset → perpetuating factors keep the problem going → protective factors buffer it → together these explain the present presenting problem.

The 5 Ps — meaning, what to gather, how to gather it
PMeaning (plain)What to assessHow to ask (sample)
Presenting problemThe current difficulties as the patient experiences themSymptoms, onset, course, severity, impact on function"What brought you here? When did it start and how has it changed?"
PredisposingBackground factors that made the person vulnerableGenetics/family history, temperament, early adversity/loss, attachment, medical"What was your childhood like? Any losses, trauma, or family history of similar problems?"
PrecipitatingThe trigger(s) around onsetRecent stressors, life events, losses, substance changes"What was happening in your life just before this began?"
PerpetuatingWhat keeps it going now (the treatment targets)Safety behaviours, avoidance, beliefs, reinforcement, relationships, poor compliance"What do you do to cope when it's bad? What happens afterwards?"
ProtectiveStrengths and resources that help recoverySupport, insight, motivation, skills, previous coping, values"What's helped before? Who supports you? What keeps you going?"
Distinguishing overlapping Ps (common confusion) The same factor can look like two Ps — classify it by timing and function. A broken relationship is precipitating if it triggered onset, but perpetuating if ongoing conflict keeps the problem alive. Childhood abuse is usually predisposing (distal vulnerability) but becomes precipitating if a recent reminder triggered this episode. Ask: did it cause the start, or does it maintain it now?

What to write (template) "Mr X's [presenting problem] is best understood as arising on a background of [predisposing], triggered by [precipitating], and maintained by [perpetuating]. Recovery is supported by [protective]."

The biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977)

Where the 5 Ps organise by time, the biopsychosocial model organises by domain — reminding us that no single lens is sufficient. The two are complementary: you can place every "P" into a bio/psycho/social cell (a 3×5 grid).

Plain text: Biological (genes, illness, substances, sleep), Psychological (beliefs, coping, emotion regulation, personality), and Social (relationships, work, culture, finances) factors overlap and interact to produce and maintain the problem. Interventions can target any domain.

Interviewing & clinical-writing: from history to formulation

A formulation is built, not guessed. These ten steps move from raw history to a defensible, collaborative explanation that drives treatment.

1 · Collect presenting complaints & history
Gather the problem in the patient's words, plus onset, course, frequency, duration, severity and functional impact (work, relationships, self-care). Ask: "Tell me what's been troubling you. When did it start? Walk me through a recent bad day." Write: a crisp problem list before theorising.
2 · Identify developmental & contextual factors (predisposing)
Early experiences, attachment, losses, trauma, family/medical history, culture. Ask: "What was growing up like? Were there events that shaped how you see yourself or others?" These seed core beliefs and vulnerability.
3 · Identify triggers & maintaining processes
Find the activating situations and the cycle: thought → feeling → behaviour → consequence. Ask: "What sets it off? What do you do then? What happens next — short term and long term?" Safety behaviours and avoidance usually live here.
4 · Assess strengths & protective factors
Supports, skills, motivation, insight, values, what has helped before. Ask: "What's kept you going? What's worked even a little?" Strengths become treatment assets, not an afterthought.
5 · Develop tentative explanatory hypotheses
Choose a model that fits the data (generic CBT or a disorder-specific model). State it as a hypothesis: "It may be that…". Avoid premature certainty.
6 · Link hypotheses to assessment findings
Anchor every claim to evidence: history, MSE, questionnaire scores, behavioural observations. If you can't point to data for a link, flag it as hypothesised.
7 · Check alternative explanations & contradictory evidence
Actively seek disconfirmation. Could this be a different disorder? Organic cause? Normal reaction? What doesn't fit the model? A good formulation survives this test or is revised.
8 · Discuss the formulation collaboratively
Share it with the patient in plain language, often as a simple diagram. Ask: "Does this fit your experience? What would you change?" Agreement builds the alliance and treatment rationale.
9 · Revise as new information emerges
Formulation is a living hypothesis. Update it when therapy reveals new triggers, beliefs or maintaining factors.
10 · Connect formulation to goals & interventions
Each maintaining factor becomes a target; each target suggests a technique (e.g., safety behaviours → behavioural experiments; avoidance → graded exposure). This is where formulation earns its keep.
Good vs poor formulation statements Poor: "Patient has OCD and needs ERP." (a diagnosis + a treatment, with no explanation)
Good: "Intrusive images are appraised as meaning she is dangerous (inflated responsibility); washing and avoidance reduce anxiety short-term but prevent her from learning the thoughts are harmless, maintaining the cycle — so ERP targets this maintaining mechanism." (explains the mechanism and justifies the treatment)

Generic CBT formulation

The CBT model (Beck) — the full component map

A · Introduction

Author: Aaron T. Beck (cognitive model, 1960s–70s), developed by Judith Beck, J. Persons and others into longitudinal (developmental) and cross-sectional (maintenance) formulations. Addresses: a very wide range of emotional disorders. Core assumption: it is not events themselves but the meaning a person gives them (their cognitions) that drives emotion and behaviour; these cognitions are shaped by early experience and organised into beliefs/schemas. Useful when you want a transdiagnostic developmental map; differs from disorder-specific models, which zoom in on one maintaining cycle rather than the whole life history.

B · The diagram

Plain text (top to bottom): Solid arrows = causal/developmental. Early experiences shape core beliefs/schemas, which generate assumptions/rules ("if…then…"). A critical incident that breaks a rule activates the beliefs, producing automatic thoughts/images. These drive the maintenance triangle of emotion ⇄ physiology ⇄ behaviour. Dashed gold loops = maintaining feedback: behaviour (avoidance/safety behaviours) feeds back to intensify thoughts and feelings, keeping the problem going.

C · Every component explained

Early experiences & developmental history

What it means: formative life events — parenting, attachment, losses, trauma, bullying, illness. Plain: the soil the beliefs grow from. Clinical significance: explains why this person is vulnerable to these beliefs. Assess: attachment quality, adversity, messages received about self/others/world. How: developmental history; "What were you told about yourself as a child?" Write: "Early loss of her mother and later abuse plausibly contributed to beliefs of being unsafe and unworthy." Example: childhood abuse → belief "I am dirty/bad." Common mistake: treating history as destiny — it informs but does not prove a belief; always link to current evidence.

Core beliefs

What: absolute, global beliefs about self, others and the world ("I am unlovable", "others are dangerous"). Plain: the deepest "bottom-line" statements. Significance: they bias interpretation of everything. Assess: use the downward-arrow technique ("if that were true, what would it mean about you?"). How: watch for themes across situations; belief questionnaires. Write: "Core belief: 'I am worthless.'" Example: depressed patient: "I'm a failure." Mistake: labelling a situational thought as a core belief — core beliefs are global and trait-like.

Schemas

What: broad cognitive–affective structures (knowledge + emotion + memory) that organise core beliefs and guide processing (Young's schema model extends this). Plain: mental templates that filter experience. Significance: drive self-perpetuating patterns. Assess: recurring interpersonal/emotional patterns; schema questionnaires. Write: "Mistrust/abuse schema active in close relationships." Example: "defectiveness/shame" schema → hypersensitivity to criticism. Mistake: conflating schema (structure) with a single belief (content). (Schema therapy is a later adaptation of Beck's model — Young, 1990.)

Intermediate beliefs: assumptions, rules, conditional beliefs

What: the "if…then…", "should/must", and "unless" rules linking core beliefs to daily life. Plain: the strategies people adopt to cope with a painful core belief. Significance: they are more accessible than core beliefs and often the first therapy target. Assess: "What rules do you live by? What has to happen for you to feel OK?" Write: "Assumption: 'If I'm not perfect, I'll be rejected.'" Example: social anxiety: "If I blush, people will think I'm weird." Mistake: missing the conditional structure — these are "rules for living", not facts.

Triggers / activating situations

What: the specific internal or external events that activate the beliefs and set off the cycle. Plain: the "switch". Significance: pinpoints where and when the problem fires — guides exposure design. Assess: situational analysis of recent episodes. How: "Describe the last time it happened — where, who, what exactly?" Write: "Trigger: touching a door handle." Example: panic triggered by a racing heart after coffee. Mistake: accepting a vague trigger ("stress") instead of a concrete situation.

Automatic thoughts, images & interpretations

What: rapid, involuntary appraisals (words or images) that pop up in a situation. Plain: the running commentary in your head. Significance: the proximal driver of emotion — the key CBT target. Assess: "What went through your mind right then? Any picture in your mind?" Write: "AT: 'I'm going to collapse.'" Example: OCD image of harming someone. Mistake: recording the emotion ("I felt scared") instead of the thought that produced it.

Emotions & physiological responses

What: the felt affect (fear, shame, sadness) and bodily arousal (racing heart, nausea, tension). Plain: how the body and mood react to the thought. Significance: physical sensations are often re-interpreted as further evidence (fuelling the loop, especially in panic). Assess: name emotions and rate intensity (0–100); map body sensations. Write: "Emotion: anxiety 85/100; palpitations, breathlessness." Mistake: blurring emotion and thought — keep them in separate columns.

Behavioural responses

What: what the person does in response (checking, washing, escaping, arguing, withdrawing). Plain: the action the feeling pushes for. Significance: behaviour is where maintaining cycles usually operate and where change is observable. Assess: behavioural analysis; diaries. Write: "Behaviour: washes hands for 10 min." Mistake: ignoring covert behaviours (mental rituals, reassurance-seeking).

Avoidance & safety behaviours

What: avoidance = staying away from feared situations; safety behaviours = actions done within a situation to prevent a feared catastrophe. Plain: escape hatches and "insurance" behaviours. Significance: the engine of maintenance — they give short-term relief but prevent disconfirmation of the feared belief, so the fear never updates. Assess: "What do you do to stop the worst from happening? What do you avoid?" Write: "Safety behaviour: grips trolley to avoid 'collapsing'." Example: social anxiety — rehearsing sentences, avoiding eye contact. Mistake: missing subtle safety behaviours, which quietly sabotage exposure.

Coping strategies

What: broader efforts to manage distress (adaptive or maladaptive). Significance: distinguishes helpful coping (to strengthen) from counter-productive coping (to target). Assess: "What do you do to get through? Does it help long-term?" Write: "Uses alcohol to manage anxiety — short-term relief, long-term worsening." Mistake: assuming all coping is good.

Maintaining factors & feedback loops

What: the self-reinforcing cycles that keep the problem alive now. Plain: the loops. Significance: these are the primary treatment targets because they are modifiable in the present. Assess: trace consequence → back to trigger. Write: "Washing reduces anxiety → negatively reinforces washing → strengthens the urge." Mistake: listing maintaining factors without showing the loop.

Short-term relief vs long-term consequences

What: the trade-off that explains why unhelpful behaviours persist — they work short-term (relief) but worsen the problem long-term. Significance: the core rationale you share with the patient to justify dropping safety behaviours. Write: "Avoidance brings immediate relief but maintains the belief that the situation is dangerous."

Protective factors, strengths & resources

What: internal and external assets (insight, motivation, support, skills, values). Significance: predict prognosis and are actively built into treatment. Assess: "What are you good at? Who helps? What's worked before?" Write: "Protective: strong therapeutic motivation; supportive father." Mistake: omitting strengths, producing a deficit-only, demoralising formulation.

Relationships in the CBT model
ArrowTypeWhy it exists / how to test it

Core belief → assumptionscausalAssumptions are coping rules devised to live with a painful core belief. Test with downward-arrow.

E · Clinical application

Move from history → formulation by filling the chain downward, then drawing the maintaining loops. Translate each box into a statement and each loop into a treatment target (see templates and worked cases). Different CBT approaches use different formulations — do not force every component into every case; a simple maintenance ("hot cross bun") cycle is often enough for a focal problem, while a longitudinal map suits complex or recurrent presentations.

Generic CBT formulation

The maintenance (vicious) cycle — "hot cross bun"

The cross-sectional "hot cross bun" (Greenberger & Padesky, 1995) is the simplest, most widely used formulation: in any situation, thoughts, emotions, physical sensations and behaviour interact and feed back on one another. It is the engine inside every disorder-specific model below.

Plain text: all four corners are linked by reciprocal green arrows — a thought triggers emotion and physical arousal, which drive a behaviour, which feeds back to confirm the thought. Break any corner (e.g., change the behaviour) and the whole cycle weakens. Disorder-specific models simply specify what fills each corner for that problem.

Fictional mini-example — panic Situation: heart races on stairs → Thought: "I'm having a heart attack" → Physical: palpitations intensify → Emotion: terror → Behaviour: stops, grips rail, calls for help → the relief "confirms" that stopping prevented disaster → next time the belief is stronger.

Disorder-specific

OCD — Salkovskis & Rachman, and the ERP rationale

A · Introduction

Authors: Paul Salkovskis (cognitive-behavioural model, 1985, 1989, 1999) and Stanley Rachman (1997, 1998). Addresses: obsessive–compulsive disorder. Key idea: intrusive thoughts are normal and near-universal; OCD develops not from the intrusions themselves but from the catastrophic appraisal of them — especially inflated responsibility (Salkovskis) and thought–action fusion (Rachman: the belief that thinking something is morally equivalent to doing it, or makes it more likely). Differs from earlier purely behavioural accounts by adding the appraisal step; Wells & Matthews (1994) later added a metacognitive account.

B · Diagram — the OCD maintenance cycle

Plain text: a normal intrusive thought is appraised as meaning "I'm responsible for harm" or "thinking it makes it real/likely" → anxiety rises → the person performs a compulsion / avoidance / reassurance to neutralise it → relief follows. The dashed maintaining loop shows relief negatively reinforces the ritual and prevents disconfirmation (the person never learns the feared catastrophe wouldn't have happened), so the appraisal and intrusions strengthen.

C · Components

Intrusive thought / image
What: an unwanted, ego-dystonic thought, urge or image (harm, contamination, taboo sexual/religious content). Plain: a "mental hiccup" everyone gets. Significance: the content is not the problem — most people have these. Assess: "What thoughts pop in uninvited? How do you react to them?" Write: "Recurrent intrusive image of harming a relative." Mistake: treating the thought's content as dangerous/diagnostic.
Appraisal — inflated responsibility & thought–action fusion
What: the catastrophic meaning given to the intrusion. Inflated responsibility = "if I might prevent harm, I'm to blame if I don't" (Salkovskis). Thought–action fusion = "thinking it is as bad as doing it / makes it likely" (Rachman). Significance: the appraisal — not the intrusion — generates distress and drives rituals; it is the main cognitive target. Assess: "What does it mean about you that you had that thought? What might happen if you ignore it?" Write: "Appraises intrusions via inflated responsibility and moral TAF." Mistake: targeting the intrusion rather than the appraisal.
Anxiety / distress
What: the emotional surge driven by the appraisal. Significance: the discomfort the ritual is designed to reduce. Assess: SUDS (0–100) ratings. Write: "Peak distress 90/100 on contamination exposure."
Compulsion / neutralising / avoidance / reassurance
What: overt (washing, checking) or covert (mental reviewing, counting, praying, reassurance-seeking) acts to reduce distress or "prevent" harm. Significance: the maintaining behaviour — and the ERP target. Assess: "What do you do to feel safe or sure? How long? Any mental rituals?" Write: "Washes 10 min; mentally reviews conversations; seeks reassurance from father." Mistake: missing covert/mental rituals and reassurance-seeking (common ERP failure point).
Temporary relief (negative reinforcement)
What: the short-lived drop in anxiety after ritualising. Significance: relief rewards the ritual (negative reinforcement) and blocks learning that anxiety would fall on its own and the catastrophe would not occur. Write: "Relief after washing negatively reinforces the compulsion, maintaining the cycle."

D · Arrows

ArrowTypeWhy / how tested
Intrusion → appraisalcausalThe intrusion is interpreted through pre-existing responsibility/TAF beliefs. Test: do non-OCD controls have the same intrusions without distress? (Yes — supporting the model.)
Appraisal → anxietycausalCatastrophic meaning generates distress. Test: reducing the appraisal reduces anxiety.
Anxiety → compulsioncausal/motivationalThe urge to neutralise is driven by distress.
Compulsion → relief → (back to appraisal)maintainingRelief negatively reinforces ritualising and prevents disconfirmation, so appraisals persist. Test: ERP breaks the loop → symptoms fall.

E · From formulation to ERP

Why ERP follows from the formulation If rituals/avoidance maintain OCD by producing relief that prevents disconfirmation, then the logical treatment is to confront the trigger (Exposure) while not performing the ritual (Response/Ritual Prevention). The patient then learns experientially that (a) anxiety rises, peaks and falls on its own (habituation / inhibitory learning) and (b) the feared catastrophe does not occur — directly disconfirming the appraisal. Treatment does not follow from the diagnosis alone; it follows from this mechanism.

Sample ERP-building questions
  • "If you didn't wash/check, what's the worst you fear would happen?"
  • "On 0–100, how distressing is touching this / resisting that?"
  • "What's the smallest step we could start with that still raises some anxiety?"
Common mistakes Challenging the content of intrusions (reassurance in disguise); allowing covert rituals during exposure; building a hierarchy without eliciting the feared consequence; using relaxation during exposure as a safety behaviour (it should be a general coping skill used outside exposures).

Panic — Clark's cognitive model (1986)

A · Introduction

Author: David M. Clark (1986, 1988). Addresses: panic disorder. Key idea: panic attacks result from the catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily sensations (e.g., reading palpitations as "a heart attack", dizziness as "I'm going mad"). Differs from general anxiety models by specifying an internal (interoceptive) vicious cycle driven by misinterpretation, not external threat.

B · Diagram — the panic vicious cycle

Plain text (clockwise loop): a trigger → perceived threat → apprehension → bodily sensations (arousal) → catastrophic misinterpretation of those sensations → which increases perceived threat again → escalating spiral = a panic attack. All causal, forming a rapid positive-feedback loop.

C · Components & D · Arrows

Body sensations → catastrophic misinterpretation (the key link)
What: normal arousal sensations (palpitations, breathlessness, dizziness) interpreted as signs of imminent catastrophe (heart attack, suffocation, madness, losing control). Significance: this misinterpretation is the pivot of the model and the main cognitive target. Assess: "When your heart raced, what did you think it meant? What did you fear would happen?" Write: "Misinterprets palpitations as 'I'm about to die'." Arrow type: causal — remove the misinterpretation and the loop cannot escalate.
Safety behaviours & avoidance (maintaining)
What: sitting down, carrying water/medication, gripping objects, avoiding exertion/places. Significance: they prevent disconfirmation ("I only survived because I sat down"), maintaining the belief. maintaining Assess: "What do you do to stop the worst happening?" Treatment link: drop safety behaviours + interoceptive exposure (deliberately induce sensations) to learn they are harmless.

E · Application

Model → treatmentCognitive therapy targets the misinterpretation (behavioural experiments, interoceptive exposure — e.g., hyperventilating or spinning to show sensations are benign) and removes safety behaviours. NICE recommends this cognitive therapy as first-line for panic disorder.

Disorder-specific

Social anxiety — Clark & Wells model (1995)

A · Introduction

Authors: David M. Clark & Adrian Wells (1995). Addresses: social anxiety disorder (social phobia). Key idea: in feared social situations the person shifts attention inward onto a distorted negative self-image ("processing the self as a social object"), uses safety behaviours, and engages in anticipatory and post-event processing — all of which prevent disconfirmation of feared beliefs.

B · Diagram

Plain text: a social situation activates assumptions → perceived social danger → the person turns attention inward, constructing a distorted negative self-image, and deploys safety behaviours. Reciprocal/maintaining loops (anticipatory worry before, post-event "rumination" after) feed the assumptions, so the person never gathers disconfirming evidence from the real social world.

C · Key components

Self-focused attention & distorted self-image
What: attention shifts from the task/others onto monitoring one's own anxiety and an imagined "observer-perspective" image of how bad one looks. Significance: the person judges themselves by this distorted internal image rather than external reality, so social feedback cannot correct it. Assess: "In that moment, what were you most aware of — the conversation or yourself? Did you have an image of how you appeared?" Treatment link: attention-training + video feedback to correct the distorted self-image.
Safety behaviours
What: rehearsing sentences, gripping a glass, avoiding eye contact, speaking little. Significance: prevent feared catastrophe in the person's mind, so disconfirmation never happens; some even cause the feared outcome (e.g., appearing aloof). maintaining
Anticipatory & post-event processing
What: dread and rehearsal before; biased negative review ("rumination") after. Significance: both lock in negative self-appraisal independent of what actually happened. maintaining/reciprocal

E · Application

Model → treatmentCognitive therapy for social anxiety reverses each maintaining process: shift attention outward, drop safety behaviours, use video/behavioural experiments to update the distorted self-image, and cut anticipatory/post-event processing. NICE-recommended as first-line.

Disorder-specific

Depression — Beck's cognitive model

A · Introduction

Author: Aaron T. Beck (1967, 1979). Key idea: depression is maintained by the negative cognitive triad — persistently negative views of the self ("I'm worthless"), the world/experience ("everything is pointless"), and the future ("it will never improve") — sustained by negative schemas and systematic cognitive distortions (e.g., all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralisation).

B · Diagram — the cognitive triad + behavioural loop

Plain text: the negative triad (self/world/future) drives low mood, which drives withdrawal and inactivity; reduced activity means fewer rewarding experiences, which feeds back to "confirm" the triad ("nothing's worth it") — a self-maintaining loop. This loop is why behavioural activation and cognitive restructuring are the twin treatment targets.

C · Components (brief)

Negative cognitive triad
Assess: thought records sampling views of self, experience, future. Write: "Triad: 'I'm a failure / life is empty / it won't get better.'" Mistake: treating hopelessness as fixed fact rather than a testable cognition (note: hopelessness is a key suicide-risk marker — formulate risk explicitly).
Cognitive distortions (information-processing biases)
All-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralisation, mental filter, disqualifying the positive, catastrophising, personalisation. Significance: they manufacture triad-consistent "evidence". Treatment link: identify and test with Socratic questioning and behavioural experiments.
Withdrawal / inactivity loop
maintaining Reduced activity → fewer pleasure/mastery experiences → more negative cognition. Treatment link: behavioural activation directly breaks this loop and often precedes cognitive work.
Model → treatmentBehavioural activation (scheduling activity/mastery) + cognitive restructuring of the triad and distortions; for recurrent depression, schema-level and relapse-prevention work (incl. MBCT).

Disorder-specific

PTSD — Ehlers & Clark cognitive model (2000)

A · Introduction

Authors: Anke Ehlers & David M. Clark (2000). Key idea: PTSD persists when the person processes the trauma in a way that creates a sense of serious current threat. This arises from two linked problems: (1) negative appraisals of the trauma and its aftermath ("the world is utterly dangerous", "I've permanently changed"), and (2) a poorly elaborated, disjointed trauma memory that is strongly cue-driven, producing re-experiencing. Maintained by maladaptive coping (avoidance, rumination, safety behaviours).

B · Diagram

Plain text: a disjointed trauma memory and negative appraisals both generate a sense of serious current threat → producing re-experiencing, hyperarousal and maladaptive coping (avoidance, rumination, safety behaviours). Maintaining loops: coping strategies prevent memory elaboration and appraisal change, so the threat sense persists.

C · Key components (brief)

Nature of the trauma memory
What: poorly contextualised in time/place, fragmented, easily triggered by sensory cues → intrusions feel like "happening now" (lack of time-coding). Treatment link: imaginal reliving / updating to elaborate and contextualise the memory.
Negative appraisals
What: of the event ("it was my fault"), of initial symptoms ("I'm going mad"), and of others' responses. Treatment link: cognitive restructuring of appraisals.
Maladaptive coping (maintaining)
maintaining Thought suppression, avoidance, rumination, safety behaviours — all block natural recovery and memory updating. Treatment link: drop these; reclaim life (stimulus discrimination "then vs now").
Model → treatmentTrauma-focused CBT: reliving/updating the memory, restructuring appraisals, discriminating trauma cues from safe present ("then vs now"), and dropping avoidance/rumination. NICE first-line (with EMDR as an alternative).

Disorder-specific

Health anxiety — Warwick & Salkovskis (1990)

A · Introduction

Authors: Hilary Warwick & Paul Salkovskis (1990). Key idea: health anxiety (hypochondriasis) is driven by the catastrophic misinterpretation of benign bodily signs and medical information as evidence of serious illness, maintained by checking, reassurance-seeking and avoidance — structurally a "panic model for illness fears over a longer time-course".

B · Diagram

Plain text: a bodily sign is misinterpreted as serious illness → anxiety rises → the person checks the body, seeks reassurance (Dr Google, doctors) and avoids, which increases body-focus and provides only brief relief → sensitising them to the next sign. All causal, forming a self-perpetuating loop.

C · Components (brief)

Reassurance-seeking (maintaining)
maintaining Why it maintains: reassurance works like a compulsion — relief is brief and teaches the person they need reassurance, so doubt returns stronger. Treatment link: reduce reassurance-seeking + behavioural experiments; treat like ERP.
Body checking & increased interoceptive focus
Repeated checking (palpating, measuring) creates real sensations (soreness, redness) that "confirm" illness. Treatment link: drop checking; test the effect of attention on sensations.
Model → treatmentCBT reattributes sensations to benign causes, cuts checking/reassurance/avoidance, and uses behavioural experiments — not repeated medical investigation, which feeds the cycle.

Disorder-specific

GAD — intolerance of uncertainty & metacognition

A · Introduction

Two complementary, evidence-based accounts of generalised anxiety disorder (chronic worry):

  • Dugas, Ladouceur & colleagues (1998) — the intolerance-of-uncertainty model: people with GAD find uncertainty intensely aversive and worry as an attempt to "prepare for" or eliminate it, aided by positive beliefs about worry, negative problem orientation and cognitive avoidance.
  • Wells (1995) metacognitive model — distinguishes Type 1 worry (about external events) from Type 2 worry ("worry about worry" — e.g., "my worrying is uncontrollable and harmful"). Negative metacognitive beliefs maintain GAD.

B · Diagram (metacognitive)

Plain text: a "what if" trigger starts Type 1 worry; this activates negative metacognitive beliefs ("I can't control my worry; it will harm me") = Type 2 worry, which drives counter-productive coping (thought suppression, avoidance, reassurance) that paradoxically increases worry — a self-maintaining loop.

Model → treatmentIU model → uncertainty-focused CBT (worry exposure, problem-solving training, behavioural experiments tolerating uncertainty). Metacognitive therapy → modify metacognitive beliefs and detached mindfulness rather than challenging each worry's content.

Disorder-specific

Substance use — Marlatt & Gordon relapse-prevention model (1985)

A · Introduction

Authors: G. Alan Marlatt & Judith Gordon (1985); cognitive-behavioural model of substance use (Beck et al., 1993) is complementary. Key idea: relapse is understood as a process: a high-risk situation + poor coping → lowered self-efficacy and positive outcome expectancies about the substance → lapse → the abstinence violation effect (guilt + "I've blown it" thinking) → full relapse. Craving is cue-triggered and maintained by negative/positive reinforcement.

B · Diagram

Plain text: a high-risk situation forks two ways. With effective coping, self-efficacy rises and abstinence is maintained. With poor coping + positive expectancies, a lapse occurs; the abstinence violation effect (guilt, "all-or-nothing" collapse) then converts a single lapse into full relapse. All causal/branching.

Model → treatmentRelapse prevention: identify high-risk situations, build coping skills, challenge outcome expectancies, reframe a lapse as a learning event (not catastrophe) to defuse the AVE, and use cue-exposure/urge-surfing for craving.

Worked examples

Six worked fictional cases

All cases are invented for teaching only and are not real patients or research data.

Case 1 · Depression (Beck's model)

Background: "Ravi", 42, accountant, recent redundancy.
Presenting: 3 months of low mood, anhedonia, insomnia, hopelessness, social withdrawal, passive thoughts "better off not here".
Assessment findings: PHQ-9 = 19 (moderately severe); activity diary shows near-total inactivity; thought record: "I'm useless; no one will hire me; it'll never improve."
Model & why: Beck's cognitive model — clear negative triad + withdrawal loop.
Formulation (written): "Ravi's depression is organised around a negative cognitive triad (worthless self, hopeless future, empty world) activated by redundancy against a backdrop of high self-worth contingent on work. Low mood drives withdrawal and inactivity, reducing rewarding experiences, which confirms the triad — a self-maintaining loop. Hopelessness elevates suicide risk."
Components/arrows: redundancy (precipitant) → activates belief "I'm only worth my job"; triad → low mood → inactivity (maintaining loop) → fewer rewards → stronger triad.
Treatment implications: behavioural activation first (break inactivity loop), then cognitive restructuring of the triad; explicit risk monitoring.
Alternatives / missing info / limits: rule out bipolar (past hypomania?), medical causes (thyroid), adjustment disorder; need collateral and risk detail.

Case 2 · Panic disorder (Clark 1986)

Background: "Aisha", 29, teacher.
Presenting: recurrent sudden attacks of palpitations, breathlessness, dizziness with thoughts "I'm having a heart attack"; now avoids the gym and travelling alone.
Assessment: normal cardiac work-up; attacks peak in ~10 min; safety behaviours = carries water, sits down, checks pulse.
Model & why: Clark's panic model — catastrophic misinterpretation of benign arousal.
Formulation: "Aisha misinterprets normal arousal sensations (palpitations) as signs of a heart attack; this perceived threat heightens apprehension and arousal, intensifying sensations in a rapid vicious cycle = a panic attack. Safety behaviours (sitting, pulse-checking) and avoidance prevent her from learning the sensations are harmless, maintaining the disorder."
Arrows: sensation → catastrophic misinterpretation → ↑ threat → ↑ arousal → ↑ sensation (causal loop); safety behaviours = maintaining.
Treatment: cognitive restructuring of the misinterpretation; interoceptive exposure (induce sensations) and dropping safety behaviours.
Alternatives/limits: exclude ongoing cardiac/thyroid pathology and substance (caffeine) effects; check for agoraphobic avoidance severity.

Case 3 · OCD (Salkovskis/Rachman + ERP) — based on the source case

Background: "Sapna", 24, unmarried, early maternal loss and later sexual assault in adolescence.
Presenting: repetitive handwashing/bathing/cleaning, checking (gas, belongings), intrusive distressing sexual images of those who assaulted her, avoidance of eye contact and of leaving home; low mood, reduced sleep/libido; passive death wishes. Y-BOCS = 27 (severe).
Model & why: cognitive-behavioural OCD model — intrusions appraised via inflated responsibility/thought–action fusion ("having this image means I'm bad/dangerous"), neutralised by washing/checking/avoidance/reassurance.
Formulation: "Intrusive sexual images and contamination thoughts are appraised catastrophically (inflated responsibility, thought–action fusion), producing intense anxiety. Washing, checking, avoidance and reassurance reduce anxiety short-term but negatively reinforce the rituals and prevent her from learning the thoughts are harmless, maintaining OCD. Predisposed by early loss and lack of support; precipitated by the assault; perpetuated by rituals, family conflict and poor treatment compliance. Protective: strong motivation, supportive father."
Treatment: ERP targeting the maintaining rituals (graded exposure + ritual/response prevention, including mental rituals and reassurance), psychoeducation (thought ≠ action ≠ identity), relaxation as a between-session skill (not during exposure), relapse prevention. Risk: monitor death wishes/hopelessness each session.
Alternatives/limits: screen for co-occurring PTSD (given assault history) — if trauma intrusions dominate, trauma-focused work may be needed; distinguish ego-dystonic obsessions from trauma re-experiencing.

Case 4 · Social anxiety (Clark & Wells 1995)

Background: "Tom", 21, student.
Presenting: intense fear of seminars and conversations; fears blushing/being judged "boring"; avoids speaking, rehearses sentences, leaves early.
Assessment: SPIN elevated; reports an "observer image" of himself looking red and foolish; ruminates for hours afterward.
Model & why: Clark & Wells — self-focused attention + distorted self-image + safety behaviours + anticipatory/post-event processing.
Formulation: "In social situations Tom's assumptions ('if I seem anxious, I'll be rejected') create perceived danger; he shifts attention onto a distorted negative self-image and uses safety behaviours (rehearsing, minimal eye contact). These prevent disconfirmation, while anticipatory worry and post-event rumination lock in negative self-appraisal regardless of actual outcome."
Treatment: attention training (shift focus outward), video feedback to correct the self-image, behavioural experiments dropping safety behaviours, reducing anticipatory/post-event processing.
Alternatives/limits: differentiate from avoidant personality pattern and from autism-spectrum social difficulty; assess depression.

Case 5 · PTSD (Ehlers & Clark 2000)

Background: "Meera", 34, after a road-traffic collision 6 months ago.
Presenting: intrusive sensory flashbacks (screech of brakes), nightmares, hypervigilance, avoids driving and the crash road, appraises herself as "permanently damaged".
Assessment: fragmented, disjointed trauma memory with strong sensory cues; "then vs now" confusion during intrusions; rumination about "why me".
Model & why: Ehlers & Clark — disjointed memory + negative appraisals → sense of serious current threat.
Formulation: "A poorly elaborated, cue-driven trauma memory plus negative appraisals ('the world is dangerous', 'I've changed forever') generate a sense of current threat, producing re-experiencing and hyperarousal. Avoidance of driving and rumination block memory elaboration and appraisal change, maintaining PTSD."
Treatment: trauma-focused CBT — imaginal reliving/updating the memory, restructuring appraisals, stimulus discrimination (then vs now), dropping avoidance/rumination.
Alternatives/limits: screen for depression, complicated grief, head injury; check timing (acute stress vs PTSD).

Case 6 · Complex presentation via 5 Ps + biopsychosocial

Background: "Daniel", 48, chronic low mood + harmful alcohol use + marital breakdown — too complex for one disorder model, so a 5 Ps/biopsychosocial map is used first.
5 Ps: Presenting — depression, daily drinking, conflict. Predisposing — family history of depression and alcohol use (bio), childhood emotional neglect → belief "I'm unlovable" (psycho). Precipitating — wife's departure. Perpetuating — alcohol numbs distress short-term but worsens mood/sleep and conflict (loop); social withdrawal; belief "there's no point". Protective — employed, good relationship with daughter, now help-seeking.
Biopsychosocial: Bio — genetic loading, alcohol effects on sleep/mood; Psycho — unlovability belief, avoidant coping; Social — marital loss, isolation.
Formulation: "Daniel's depression and alcohol use are mutually maintaining: low mood prompts drinking for relief, which deepens depression and fuels conflict, confirming his belief that he is unlovable. Vulnerability (genetic + early neglect) was activated by marital breakdown."
Treatment: integrated — address alcohol (relapse-prevention model) and mood (behavioural activation + cognitive work) together; rebuild social support; leverage the daughter relationship.
Alternatives/limits: assess severity/dependence of alcohol use and whether mood is substance-induced; sequence or integrate treatment accordingly; risk assessment.

Reusable formulation-writing templates
