1. Definitions of Psychotherapy

Meaning and origin of the word

  • Etymology (Kleinke, 1994): "psychotherapy" literally means caring for another person's soul or being, from the Greek:
    • psyche — "soul" or "being";
    • therapeutikos — "caring for another".
  • Umbrella term: it covers an enormous range of interventions, modalities and integrative strategies used to improve quality of life and health.
  • Historical root — the "talking cure": Anna O., an early psychoanalytic patient of Breuer, called her treatment the "talking cure"; an elegant if vague description — talking, expressing and sharing one's pain can itself be healing. It is a foundational frame, though too loose for contemporary researchers.

Some shorter "favourite" definitions (secondary notes; imprecise but illustrative)

  • Korchin (1976): "a conversation with a therapeutic purpose."
  • Schofield (1964): "the purchase of friendship."
  • Bruch (1981): a situation in which two people interact and try to understand one another, with the specific goal of accomplishing something beneficial for the complaining person.
  • J. Watkins (1983): "when one person with an emotional disorder gets help from another person who has less of an emotional disorder."

Classic definitions

  • Wolberg (1954): a form of treatment for problems of an emotional nature, in which a trained person deliberately establishes a professional relationship with the patient in order to:
    • remove, modify or retard existing symptoms;
    • mediate (change) disturbed patterns of behaviour;
    • promote positive personality growth and development.
  • Jerome Frank (1961, Persuasion and Healing): a planned, emotionally charged, confiding interaction between a trained, socially sanctioned healer and a sufferer; aims to change the sufferer's feelings, attitudes and behaviour.
  • Norcross (1990): the informed and intentional application of clinical methods and interpersonal stances derived from established psychological principles, to help people modify behaviours, cognitions, emotions and other characteristics in directions the participants deem desirable.
  • Strupp: a learning process in which a trained person uses the relationship to help the patient overcome distress and develop more adaptive functioning.

Key elements common to all definitions

  • A trained, professional therapist.
  • A help-seeking client.
  • A psychological (non-medical) method.
  • A planned, systematic, theory-based process.
  • A confiding, therapeutic relationship.
  • The aim of change (symptom relief, behaviour change, growth).

Psychotherapy vs counselling vs medical treatment

  • Psychotherapy vs counselling:
    • Counselling: usually normal developmental, situational and adjustment problems; more present- and problem-focused; shorter.
    • Psychotherapy: deeper, more enduring or pathological problems and personality reconstruction; usually longer.
    • The overlap is large; the distinction is mostly one of degree.
  • Psychotherapy vs medical treatment:
    • Psychotherapy works through communication, relationship and learning rather than drugs.
    • Both are often combined (e.g. for depression or psychosis).
  • Psychotherapy vs talking to a friend (secondary notes):
    • It is a unilateral ("one-way") professional relationship — the client's problems are discussed, not the therapist's needs.
    • It is circumscribed by limits on time, frequency, content and intimacy.
    • Confidentiality is assured (within ethical and legal limits), so the client can speak freely.
    • It must not be compromised by unethical dual relationships (friend, colleague, lover).

Four major factors psychotherapy shares (secondary notes)

  • A relationship in which the client trusts that the therapist is competent and cares about their welfare.
  • The expectancy / hope, shared by therapist and client, that benefit will result.
  • An underlying conceptual framework giving a rationale and procedures, requiring active participation of both.
  • A practice setting socially defined and accepted as a place of healing.

2. Objectives (Goals) of Psychotherapy

Kleinke (1994): six fundamental therapeutic goals (secondary notes)

  • Overcoming demoralisation and gaining hope.
  • Enhancing mastery and self-efficacy.
  • Overcoming avoidance.
  • Becoming aware of one's misconceptions.
  • Accepting life's realities.
  • Achieving insight.

The broader list of goals

  • Relieve symptoms and distress (anxiety, depression, obsessions, etc.).
  • Modify maladaptive behaviour, thoughts and emotions (change).
  • Gain insight into the origin and meaning of problems (esp. psychodynamic work).
  • Improve interpersonal relationships and social functioning.
  • Strengthen coping, problem solving and adaptive defences; improve self-esteem and self-efficacy.
  • Resolve inner conflicts and past trauma; foster emotional expression and regulation.
  • Promote personal growth, self-actualisation and a sense of meaning.
  • Prevent relapse and improve quality of life; in severe illness, maintain functioning and prevent hospitalisation.
  • Concrete life outcomes (e.g. finding a job or a partner, staying out of hospital, staying alive) — the list of possible goals is effectively endless (secondary notes).

Whose goals? (goals can differ — secondary notes)

  • Goals may differ for the patient, the significant others in the patient's life, and the psychologist (e.g. the patient wants reduced anxiety, the therapist wants increased insight, the spouse wants greater independence).
  • Grounded in beneficence and non-maleficence: help with the problems, help the person feel better, and do no harm.
  • Short-term symptom relief (often CBT) and deeper work (often psychodynamic) may pull in different directions; CBT may seek to reduce excess emotion while the patient wants to remove all negative emotion.
  • Because goals and who sets them can be uncertain or in conflict, goal-setting makes informed consent in therapy genuinely complex (see Topic 6).
Where WHO fits (supported)
  • WHO's rights-based, recovery-oriented view frames broader objectives: restoring functioning, participation and quality of life, not only symptom removal (consistent with mhGAP and task-shifting goals discussed under evidence-based practice).

3. Issues in Training Professional Therapists

Who may practise (India)

  • Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) Act, 1992 (amended 2000) regulates training and registration of clinical psychologists (supported).
    • The RCI-recognised M.Phil in Clinical Psychology (two years, with supervised clinical practice) is the entry qualification.
    • Practitioners must hold a Central Rehabilitation Register (CRR) number.
  • Mental Healthcare Act (MHCA), 2017 recognises the clinical psychologist as a mental health professional.

Components of training

  • Theoretical knowledge (psychopathology, theories of therapy, assessment).
  • Supervised clinical practice (direct observation, audio/video review, case conferences).
  • Personal therapy / self-awareness work (manage countertransference and blind spots).
  • Learning ethics and law.
  • Research literacy and evidence-based practice.
  • Cultural competence.
  • Lifelong learning and continuing education.

Competencies expected

  • Relationship (alliance) skills; assessment and case formulation.
  • Intervention skills in at least one major model.
  • Ethical decision making; record keeping.
  • Working with diversity; use of supervision; reflective practice.

Issues and challenges

  • Wide variation in quality, duration and supervision of training; shortage of trained supervisors.
  • Over-reliance on Western models with limited cultural adaptation.
  • Whether personal therapy for trainees should be mandatory.
  • Gate-keeping: judging suitability (empathy, maturity, integrity) and remediating or dismissing unfit trainees.
  • Training vs licensing gap: unregulated practice by untrained persons; need to protect the public.
  • Therapist stress, burnout, vicarious traumatisation, compassion fatigue; self-care and peer support.
  • Boundaries and dual relationships in trainee-supervisor and small-community settings.
  • Training in tele-therapy and its ethics.
  • Practising within the limits of one's competence; knowing when to refer.

4. Ethical and Legal Issues in Therapy Work

Core ethical principles (APA Ethical Principles)

  • Beneficence and non-maleficence: do good; avoid harm.
  • Fidelity and responsibility: trust, professional and scientific responsibility, accountability.
  • Integrity: honesty, accuracy, truthfulness; no deception or exaggerated claims.
  • Justice: fairness, equal access to quality services; no discrimination.
  • Respect for people's rights and dignity: privacy, confidentiality, autonomy, self-determination.
IACP — SECONDARY / paraphrased
  • The IACP (Indian Association of Clinical Psychologists) is the national professional body of clinical psychologists in India and is generally understood to publish a code of ethics for its members.
  • Its code of ethics was NOT accessible and is absent from the extracted sources, so it is cited here only as SECONDARY / paraphrased general knowledge. No clause numbers, quotations or dates are asserted. Its broad themes (competence, confidentiality, consent, avoiding exploitation, professional conduct) overlap with the APA principles and the RCI code of conduct and should be read as such.

Major ethical issues

  • Competence: practise only within the limits of training; refer when needed; keep updated.
  • Informed consent: obtained before assessment and treatment (see Section 6).
  • Confidentiality and its limits: private information shared only with consent; exceptions below.
  • Dual (multiple) relationships and boundaries:
    • Avoid social, business, financial or sexual relationships with clients.
    • Sexual intimacy with a client (or former client, at least for a specified period) is unethical and prohibited.
    • Avoid exploitation and misuse of power; manage gifts, self-disclosure, touch and social media contact carefully.
  • Fees and contracts: clear and honest about fees, missed sessions and payment; do not exploit.
  • Record keeping and data security.
  • Termination and referral: do not abandon clients; terminate when the client no longer needs or benefits; provide referrals and handover.
  • Cultural sensitivity and respect for diversity (gender, caste, religion, sexuality, disability, socio-economic status).
  • Supervision and consultation: use regularly; seek help in ethical dilemmas.
  • Advertising and claims: avoid false or unsubstantiated claims about outcomes.
  • Research ethics: consent, no harm, confidentiality, ethics committee approval.

Ethical decision-making steps

  • Identify the problem and the people affected.
  • Consult codes and law; consult supervisor / colleagues.
  • Consider options and consequences.
  • Decide and act; document; evaluate.
Where ethical guidance comes from (secondary notes)
  • Professional bodies' codes and guidelines define professional identity and duties.
  • Legal statutes and case law show how courts have resolved dilemmas (illustrative, not legal advice; the law is not always an ethically sufficient guide).
  • The therapist must still reason for themselves; the overarching duty is to make the best-quality decision that can be made.
  • Tjelveit (1999) notes many different styles of ethical reasoning (e.g. casuistry, virtue ethics, feminist ethics, communitarianism, pragmatic, narrative) that a therapist may draw on.
  • Classic dilemmas cluster in three domains: the goals/objectives of therapy; the boundaries between the therapist's different identities; and the social/political frameworks of practice.

The four sources of the duty to safeguard confidentiality (secondary notes)

  • The ethical codes of the mental health professions.
  • Professional licensure regulations / confidentiality and privilege statutes.
  • The common law (case law has carved out protection for psychotherapists, though less stringent than attorney-client privilege).
  • Constitutional guarantees (a right of privacy is inferred rather than explicit).
  • Framed here as general / secondary professional and legal background (US-oriented), not Indian statute; for India see MHCA 2017 and Puttaswamy (2017) below. Jaffe v. Redmond (1996): the US Supreme Court recognised psychotherapist-patient communications as confidential in federal trials.

Why informed consent matters even in a "talking" treatment (secondary notes)

  • All psychotherapies have risks and benefits the client needs to understand; benefits are often emphasised while risks are under-stated.
  • Possible risks to disclose include: distressing transference reactions, regressive dependency, and temporary worsening ("feeling worse before feeling better", esp. in insight work).
  • Greater client participation in treatment decisions lowers the risk of harmful treatment; in some jurisdictions failure to obtain consent is treated as negligence.

Boundary crossings vs boundary violations (secondary notes)

  • Crossings (may be unintended, by either party):
    • lateness/earliness, altered or cancelled sessions without notice;
    • self-disclosure (verbal or non-verbal);
    • discussing patient material with others (even with consent);
    • jokes or arguments; accidental contact outside sessions; any physical contact.
  • Violations: intended and initiated by the therapist; serious, exploitative, and unethical (e.g. sexual contact).
  • The prohibition on sexual contact traces back to the Hippocratic Oath: the professional working with a patient cannot also be that patient's lover.

Limits of confidentiality

  • Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California (1976) established a duty to warn and protect identifiable third parties from serious threats of violence by a client.
  • In India:
    • MHCA 2017 protects confidentiality of information about mental illness and treatment (disclosure allowed with consent, for the treatment, for safety, or by court order).
    • POCSO Act, 2012 makes reporting of known or suspected child sexual abuse mandatory.
    • Right to Privacy recognised as a fundamental right by the Supreme Court in Puttaswamy (2017).

Legal issues in therapy work (India)

  • RCI Act, 1992: regulates training, registration and conduct; practising without registration is an offence.
  • Mental Healthcare Act, 2017 (rights-based law):
    • Rights of persons with mental illness: access to mental healthcare; community living; protection from cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment; equality and non-discrimination; information; confidentiality; access to medical records; personal contacts and communication; legal aid; and complaints about services.
    • Advance directive and nominated representative.
    • Capacity to make treatment decisions is presumed unless shown otherwise.
    • Suicide attempt presumed under severe stress and not punishable (Section 115); the government must provide care and rehabilitation.
    • Restrictions on ECT (none without anaesthesia and muscle relaxant; not for minors); prohibition of sterilisation as treatment; limited use of restraints.
  • Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016 (includes mental illness and intellectual disability).
  • POCSO Act, 2012 (mandatory reporting) and the Juvenile Justice Act (care of children).
  • Consumer Protection Act and civil/criminal law on negligence (professional malpractice); courts may seek records or testimony.
  • Tele-mental health: Telemedicine Practice Guidelines (2020) and tele-psychiatry guidance apply.
  • Legal vs ethical: law sets the minimum enforceable standard; ethics sets the higher aspirational standard.

5. Rights and Responsibilities in Psychotherapy

Client rights

  • Right to competent, respectful, non-discriminatory treatment.
  • Right to informed consent and to adequate information about therapy.
  • Right to confidentiality (within its legal limits).
  • Right to autonomy and self-determination, including the right to withdraw or refuse treatment without penalty.
  • Right to privacy and dignity; right to safety (no exploitation or harm).
  • Right to access one's records (consistent with MHCA 2017) and to make complaints about services.
  • Right to a clear contract (fees, frequency, duration, cancellation).

Client responsibilities

  • Attend sessions, be punctual, give notice of cancellation.
  • Participate actively and honestly; complete agreed homework / tasks.
  • Pay agreed fees; respect the therapeutic frame and boundaries.

Therapist responsibilities

  • Practise competently and within the limits of training; refer when needed.
  • Obtain valid informed consent; protect confidentiality and its limits.
  • Maintain boundaries; avoid dual relationships and exploitation.
  • Keep accurate, secure records; honour reporting obligations.
  • Provide continuity of care; avoid abandonment; arrange referral and handover.
  • Use supervision; engage in self-care; keep up to date (continuing education).

Confidentiality as a right (secondary notes)

  • Confidentiality may be understood as the client's right of "informed consent to disclosure": the therapist has an ethical duty to obtain the client's consent before disclosing treatment details to anyone, including those close to the client.
  • The right can create dilemmas when the client discloses that a third party is at risk (see Topic 4 limits), or when family/employers seek information or ask the therapist not to tell the client about their contact.
  • Use of case material for teaching / research is a separate, specific consent issue:
    • rising emphasis on client autonomy and ownership of personal material;
    • some journals require the client to have read and consented to the write-up;
    • consent sought after therapy ends can itself be intrusive.
Codes behind these rights/duties
  • APA Ethical Principles and the RCI code of conduct underpin these rights and responsibilities (supported).
  • IACP themes are broadly consistent (SECONDARY / paraphrased — no clause numbers asserted).

6. Consent and Assent (Including Minors)

Informed consent is the client's voluntary agreement to assessment or therapy after receiving and understanding adequate information. Consent is a process, not a one-time signature.

The principle behind consent (secondary notes)

  • The underpinning ethical principle is respect for autonomy: clients should be free to choose or refuse treatment for themselves.
  • For consent to be legally valid it must be:
    • voluntary;
    • given by a client competent to make that decision;
    • adequately informed, including about possible negative side-effects.
  • A specific difficulty in therapy: the therapist often cannot predict exactly what the client will experience or what the outcome will be, so it is good practice to warn that the process is not always comfortable and that many clients feel worse before they feel better (less so in CBT).

Elements of informed consent

  • Capacity (competence): ability to understand, appreciate, reason and express a choice.
  • Disclosure of information:
    • purpose and nature of therapy; methods; expected benefits;
    • possible risks and discomfort; alternatives (including no treatment);
    • duration; fees; confidentiality and its limits;
    • recording / observation; supervision; therapist's qualifications;
    • the right to withdraw without penalty.
  • Comprehension: simple, understandable language; check understanding.
  • Voluntariness: free from coercion or undue pressure.
  • Documentation: written (or recorded) consent, revisited when the plan changes.

Special issues

  • Minors (<18 years in India):
    • Legal consent comes from the parent or legal guardian.
    • The child's assent (affirmative, informed agreement to the extent of the child's developing capacity) is also sought.
    • Assent respects autonomy, increases cooperation and strengthens the alliance.
    • Guidelines (for example ICMR 2017) generally require assent for children roughly 7-18 years, with simpler language for younger children and more weight to the wishes of older, mature adolescents.
    • If a child refuses, explore concerns and, unless safety requires otherwise, respect it.
  • Adults lacking capacity: involve the nominated representative / legal guardian under MHCA 2017; still seek the person's views; use the least restrictive approach.
  • Separate, specific consent for recording, observation, supervision, research or publication; case material is anonymised.
  • Emergency (immediate risk to life) can justify action without full consent, with documentation.
  • Cultural context: family involvement is common in collectivist settings; still ensure the client's own consent and confidentiality.

Capacity and voluntariness can fluctuate (secondary notes)

  • The problem is usually not a global loss of competence but fluctuating capacity or rapidly changing decisions (e.g. recent trauma or bereavement, psychotic symptoms).
  • A child who agreed to therapy one day and refuses the next may be making an informed refusal — or showing that the work has begun; this needs careful judgement.
  • Voluntariness is strained when clients are pressured or "coerced":
    • family-pushed children or family members who agree only under pressure;
    • mandated / forensic clients (therapy as an alternative to prison or part of detention) — handled openly as part of the work from the start.

7. Planning and Recording of Therapy Sessions

Planning

  • Intake and assessment: presenting complaints, history, risk assessment, mental status, test results, diagnosis.
  • Case formulation: an integrated hypothesis explaining the problem using the "4 Ps":
    • Predisposing factors;
    • Precipitating factors;
    • Perpetuating factors;
    • Protective factors.
  • Treatment plan: goals, chosen therapy, frequency (usually weekly), session length (usually 45-50 minutes), expected duration, measures of progress, emergency plan, criteria for termination.
  • Therapeutic frame: time, place, fee, boundaries, cancellation policy, contact between sessions.
  • Session planning: prepare using previous notes; plan aims per session but stay responsive to the client's needs.

Structure of a typical session (e.g. CBT)

  • Brief check-in and mood check.
  • Bridge from the previous session.
  • Agenda setting (collaboratively).
  • Review of homework.
  • Work on agenda items (main therapeutic work).
  • Capsule summaries during the session.
  • Assign new homework.
  • Feedback from the client on the session.

Recording

  • Types of records: intake / case history; assessment reports and test data; treatment plan; consent forms; progress notes; process notes (private reflections, kept separate); risk assessments; referral and termination summaries; correspondence.
  • Formats of progress notes:
    • SOAP: Subjective, Objective, Assessment, Plan;
    • DAP: Data, Assessment, Plan;
    • BIRP: Behaviour, Intervention, Response, Plan.
  • Good records are timely, accurate, objective, factual, brief but complete, free of judgemental language, signed and dated.
  • Purposes: continuity of care, monitoring progress, supervision, communication with other professionals, evidence in legal/ethical review, research and quality improvement.
  • Security and confidentiality: secure storage (locked cabinets, password-protected files), restricted access, coded identifiers; retention per law or policy; safe disposal.
  • Audio/video recording needs explicit written consent; used only for the agreed purpose and destroyed as agreed.

Progress notes vs process notes (secondary notes)

  • A common practice: note briefly in the medical record that the session took place, and keep short notes of the session's main themes separately.
  • Process notes (the therapist's private reflections) are generally not shared with the client; reading them is unlikely to help the client.
  • The therapist does not "own" the notes outright: in some jurisdictions the client may have legal access to them unless access would harm the client or another person.
  • In CBT, therapist and client often share records of thoughts, feelings and behaviours (as with homework); caution where material could be misread out of context.

8. Structuring Therapy and Setting Goals

  • Structure gives therapy direction, safety and efficiency: a clear frame (contract), a clear agenda each session, phases, and regular review of progress.
  • Phases of therapy:
    • Beginning: engagement, assessment, formulation, contracting, goal setting.
    • Middle: active intervention and working through.
    • End: consolidation, relapse prevention, termination, follow-up.
  • Goal setting: collaborative (jointly negotiated), derived from the case formulation, expressed in the client's own words.
  • Because patient, therapist and significant others may hold different goals (see Topic 2), negotiating and clarifying goals is part of structuring therapy and of valid informed consent.

Characteristics of good goals (SMART)

  • Specific; Measurable; Achievable; Realistic/Relevant; Time-bound.
  • Also stated positively, under the client's control, and ordered by priority.

Types of goals and measurement

  • Ultimate vs intermediate vs immediate (session) goals.
  • Outcome goals (symptom relief, behaviour change) vs process goals (insight, alliance, skills).
  • Short-term vs long-term.
  • Goal Attainment Scaling (Kiresuk and Sherman, 1968): each goal scaled from -2 (much worse) to +2 (much better than expected) for individualised measurement.

Why structure and goals matter

  • Goal consensus is a component of the therapeutic alliance (Bordin) and predicts outcome.
  • Clear goals increase motivation and signal when therapy is complete.
  • Goals are revisited and revised as new information or progress emerges.

9. Pre- and Post-Assessment

Purpose

  • Pre-assessment (baseline): diagnosis, severity, risk, strengths, and a benchmark.
  • Post-assessment: evaluates change after therapy; follow-up checks maintenance.
  • Ongoing session-by-session monitoring (e.g. OQ-45, CORE-OM, Outcome Rating Scale / Session Rating Scale) gives feedback so therapy can be adjusted (feedback-informed treatment); reduces deterioration, especially in clients "not on track".

Methods

  • Structured and unstructured interviews; behavioural observation.
  • Self-report scales; clinician-rated scales; projective or other tests when relevant.
  • Collateral information; physiological measures; client diaries / self-monitoring.

Commonly used measures (from the backbone)

  • Depression: BDI-II, PHQ-9, HAM-D.
  • Anxiety: BAI, GAD-7, HAM-A.
  • OCD: Y-BOCS. PTSD: PCL-5 / CAPS-5.
  • General distress: SCL-90-R, GHQ.
  • Well-being / quality of life: WHO-5, WHOQOL-BREF.
  • Functioning: GAF / WHODAS 2.0. Global: CGI.
  • Target-complaint ratings; therapeutic alliance (WAI).

Evaluating change

  • Statistical significance (group level) vs clinical significance (individual level).
  • Reliable Change Index (RCI; Jacobson and Truax, 1991): the change must exceed measurement error (RCI > 1.96).
  • Clinically significant change: the client crosses a cutoff between the dysfunctional and functional populations.
  • Effect size (Cohen's d) and percentage improvement.
  • Single-case designs (AB, ABAB, multiple baseline) for individual evaluation.
Two different RCIs — do not confuse them
  • RCI (Act): Rehabilitation Council of India Act, 1992 — the regulator (Sections 3-4-10).
  • RCI (statistic): Reliable Change Index (Jacobson & Truax, 1991) — used here to judge whether an individual's change is reliable (> 1.96).

Good practice

  • Use reliable and valid measures suited to the client's language, culture and literacy.
  • Use the same measures before and after.
  • Interpret with caution (placebo, regression to the mean, practice effects, response bias).
  • Share results with the client and use them to plan further care.

10. Practice of Evidence-Based Therapies (EBP)

Definition and origins

  • APA Presidential Task Force (2006): EBP is the integration of the best available research with clinical expertise in the context of patient characteristics, culture and preferences (supported).
  • The concept came from evidence-based medicine (Sackett).

Empirically supported treatments (ESTs)

  • Division 12 (Society of Clinical Psychology, APA); Chambless and Hollon (1998) criteria:
    • well-established (≥ 2 good between-group studies by independent researchers, or a large series of single-case studies, showing superiority to placebo or another treatment, with manuals and clear samples);
    • probably efficacious.

Hierarchy of evidence

  • Systematic reviews / meta-analyses of RCTs; then RCTs; controlled trials without randomisation; cohort and case-control; single-case studies; expert opinion.
  • Efficacy (controlled conditions) vs effectiveness (real-world) vs clinical utility (feasible, acceptable, cost-effective).

Examples of well-supported treatments

  • CBT for depression, panic, social anxiety, GAD.
  • ERP for OCD; prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy and EMDR for PTSD.
  • Behavioural activation and IPT for depression; DBT for borderline personality disorder.
  • Family-based treatment for adolescent anorexia; parent management training for child conduct problems; CBT for insomnia.
  • Social skills training, family psychoeducation and cognitive remediation for schizophrenia.

Guidelines and scaling up (WHO / India)

  • NICE (UK), APA, and WHO mhGAP recommend specific treatments (supported).
  • In India, culturally adapted low-intensity interventions delivered by lay counsellors (e.g. the Healthy Activity Programme in the PREMIUM trials in Goa, and the Thinking Healthy Programme) show evidence-based care can be scaled up (task-shifting).

Steps of EBP in the clinic

  • Ask a clear clinical question.
  • Search for the best evidence.
  • Appraise it.
  • Integrate it with clinical expertise and the client's values.
  • Apply; evaluate the outcome.

The evidence debate and common factors

  • Eysenck (1952) claimed psychotherapy was no more effective than spontaneous remission.
  • Smith and Glass (1977) meta-analysed 375 studies and found a mean effect size of about 0.68 (the average treated person better off than about 75% of untreated persons).
  • The "Dodo bird verdict" (Rosenzweig; Luborsky): all bona fide therapies work about equally, pointing to common factors.
  • Lambert (1992) estimated contributions (approximate and debated, as the backbone frames them):
    • extratherapeutic / client factors ~40%;
    • relationship ~30%;
    • expectancy ~15%;
    • technique ~15%.
  • Balance: both specific (model-based) and common (relationship) factors matter; evidence-based techniques work best within a strong relationship.

The common factors (backbone)

  • Therapeutic relationship (alliance, empathy).
  • Client expectations and hope (placebo).
  • A healing setting and a credible rationale.
  • A credible ritual / set of procedures.
  • New learning experiences; a sense of mastery.
  • Wampold's contextual model argues these common factors, rather than specific techniques, explain most of the benefit of therapy.

Criticisms and challenges of EBP

  • RCT samples often unrepresentative (strict exclusion, no comorbidity); limited generalisability to diverse and Indian populations.
  • Manuals can reduce flexibility; researcher allegiance effects; publication bias.
  • Under-emphasis of common factors and the therapist's contribution.
  • Outcome focus on symptom measures rather than broader well-being or meaning.
  • Practice-based evidence and routine outcome monitoring complement RCTs; cultural adaptation and task shifting are important in India.
