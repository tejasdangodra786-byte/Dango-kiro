1Objectives of the clinical interview

The clinical interview is a purposeful, goal-directed professional conversation whose objectives are to build rapport, gather history, assess and diagnose, understand the person, plan and deliver help, manage risk, and meet ethical duties such as consent and confidentiality.

Characterisations by professional bodies and classic authors
  • Guideline The APA Dictionary of Psychology characterises an interview (a directed conversation held to gather information, assess or help) as a directed conversation conducted to obtain information or assess a person, and a clinical interview (an interview used to screen, diagnose and plan treatment) as one carried out to screen, diagnose, formulate and plan treatment. [American Psychological Association, APA Dictionary of Psychology]
  • Guideline The World Health Organization, in its mhGAP and related guidance, characterises good assessment as being conducted through respectful, empathic and collaborative communication, so that gathering clinical information and engaging the person are treated as a single task rather than two separate ones. [World Health Organization, mhGAP Intervention Guide]
  • Theoretical view Wolberg treats the interview as the principal instrument of psychotherapy, through which the therapist establishes a working relationship, understands the patient's dynamics and brings about change, so interviewing skill underpins the whole treatment. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Standard teaching frames the interview as differing from ordinary conversation by having a purpose, structure, asymmetrical roles and professional ethics, which is why it is planned and goal-directed rather than casual. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]

The main objectives (exam form)

  • The first objective is to establish rapport and a working relationship, because a therapeutic alliance (the trusting, collaborative bond needed for honest disclosure) makes the person feel safe enough to speak openly, and without it even expert technique yields little. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • A second objective is to collect information on the presenting complaints and on the personal, family, medical, psychiatric and social history, so the clinician builds a full picture of the person and their current functioning rather than isolated symptoms. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • A third objective is to assess and diagnose, using the gathered data against DSM or ICD criteria, which lets the clinician name the problem, compare it with known conditions and choose an evidence-based plan. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A fourth objective is to conduct the mental status examination (a structured snapshot of current mental functioning at interview), so that appearance, speech, mood, thought, perception, cognition, insight and judgement are observed and recorded systematically. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • A fifth objective is to understand the person's perspective, including their feelings, beliefs, explanatory model and expectations, because treatment that ignores how the client sees their own problem usually fails to engage them. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A sixth objective is to assess risk, chiefly of suicide, self-harm and harm to others, since every clinical interview must screen for safety and may need to shift immediately into crisis management when danger emerges. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • A seventh objective is to plan treatment, which means choosing the intervention, agreeing goals, and giving the person clear information so they can make an informed decision about the care offered. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • An eighth objective is to deliver help within the interview itself, because in a therapeutic interview (an interview that is itself a vehicle of change) the skilled conversation, support and motivation are part of the treatment, not merely preliminaries to it. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A ninth objective is to motivate and support the person, and approaches such as motivational interviewing use the conversation itself to strengthen the person's own reasons for change rather than lecturing them into it. [William R. Miller & Stephen Rollnick, Motivational Interviewing]
  • A tenth objective is to evaluate progress and outcome across later interviews, so the clinician checks whether the agreed goals are being met and adjusts the plan in the light of the person's response. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • An objective in some settings is to gather data for research, forensic or administrative purposes, in which case the interviewer must be especially clear about the altered purpose, the limits of confidentiality and the use of the information. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Running through all of these is the ethical objective of obtaining informed consent before the interview proceeds, and the [American Psychological Association, Ethics Code 2017, Std 3.10] APA Ethics Code 2017 requires that consent be sought in understandable language at the outset. [American Psychological Association, Ethics Code 2017, Std 3.10]
  • Professional code A further ethical objective is to protect confidentiality and to explain its limits, because the APA Ethics Code 2017 treats safeguarding information, and disclosing only where law or safety demands, as a core duty of the interview. [American Psychological Association, Ethics Code 2017, Std 4.01]
  • Guideline The interview also aims to respect the person's dignity and autonomy, which the WHO frames as delivering assessment and care within a relationship built on trust, respect and shared decision-making. [World Health Organization, mhGAP Intervention Guide]
  • The overarching objective is that assessment and engagement happen together, because the same skilled, respectful conversation both gathers reliable information and builds the relationship through which any later help will be delivered. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
Objectives at a glance
  • Relationship: build rapport and a working alliance so the person speaks freely.
  • Information: gather history and the mental status examination systematically.
  • Assessment: diagnose, formulate and assess risk (suicide, harm).
  • Action: plan and deliver help, motivate, and review outcome over time.
  • Ethics: informed consent and confidentiality run through every interview.
Mnemonic
RICHER
Rapport, Information (history and MSE), Classify and diagnose, Help and plan (with motivation), Ethics (consent and confidentiality), Risk and Review — the objectives of the clinical interview.

2Interviewing techniques and the stages of an interview

Interviewing technique is the deliberate way the clinician prepares, opens, conducts and closes the conversation, using a comfortable setting, a clear structure, good questioning and listening, and careful note-taking so that reliable information and a strong relationship both result.

  • The first technique is preparation, in which the clinician reviews the referral and records, arranges privacy and comfortable seating, protects the time from interruptions, and clarifies their own aims before the person enters. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Seating is arranged at a slight angle rather than directly behind a desk, because an open, non-confrontational arrangement lowers the power distance and signals that the interview is a shared, collaborative task. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The opening technique is to greet the person by name, introduce oneself and one's role, explain the purpose, time and confidentiality, obtain consent, and then begin with an open invitation such as asking what brings them in. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • A key early technique is putting the person at ease, since an anxious or guarded client discloses little, so warmth, unhurried pace and simple language in the first minutes pay off throughout the interview. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • In the body of the interview the clinician explores the presenting problem for onset, course, severity, precipitants and impact, then moves into relevant history, keeping the person's own account at the centre. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The main structuring technique is funnelling (opening with broad questions and then narrowing to specifics), which begins with open questions to let the person tell their story, then narrows with focused questions to pin down detail. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • An inverted funnel, moving from specific closed questions toward broader ones, is used for sensitive or shameful topics, because starting with concrete, answerable questions eases the person into harder material. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Throughout the body the clinician blends the core responding skills of clarification, paraphrasing, reflection of feeling, summarising and facilitation, which keep the person talking and confirm that they have been understood. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • A central technique is active listening, meaning the clinician attends fully, follows the person's topic, and resists the urge to plan the next question while the person is still speaking. [Gerard Egan, The Skilled Helper]
  • Theoretical view Wolberg stresses that the therapist's manner, conveyed through facial expression, gesture and tone, is itself a technique, because an attentive and accepting manner encourages disclosure as much as the words used. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • Note-taking is handled so that it does not break the relationship, so the clinician records enough to be accurate while keeping eye contact and engagement, and explains to the person why notes are being taken. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The closing technique is to summarise, check accuracy and invite additions, typically asking whether there is anything that has not been asked, which often surfaces important material held back until the end. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • At closing the clinician also gives feedback or information, plans next steps and fixes the next appointment, ending on time and on a supportive note so the person leaves feeling contained rather than abruptly dismissed. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The final stage, after the interview, is to record notes promptly, formulate, plan and reflect on one's own reactions, because memory fades quickly and the clinician's responses can themselves be useful clinical data. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Guideline Across every stage the guiding rule is that structure serves the relationship, so the clinician flexibly adapts pace and depth to the person while still covering the ground the interview is meant to cover. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
The five stages
  • Preparation: review records, arrange privacy and comfortable seating, clarify aims.
  • Opening: greet, introduce, explain purpose and confidentiality, gain consent, open invitation.
  • Body: explore problem then history; funnel from open to specific; listen and respond.
  • Closing: summarise, check, invite additions, give information, plan, end on time.
  • After: record promptly, formulate, plan, and reflect on your own reactions.
Mnemonic
POBCA
Preparation, Opening, Body, Closing, After — the five stages that organise interviewing technique; remember them in order to never skip consent at the start or risk-check and summary at the end.

3Types of interview

Interviews are classified by purpose, such as intake, diagnostic, case-history, mental status, crisis, therapeutic, research and forensic, and by structure and direction, ranging from structured to unstructured and from directive to non-directive, with each type suited to a particular clinical aim.

Types by purpose

  • The intake or initial interview is the first contact, and it establishes the reason for referral, the presenting problem, expectations, basic screening and risk, setting the agenda for everything that follows. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The diagnostic interview is aimed at arriving at a diagnosis by systematically checking the person's symptoms against DSM or ICD criteria, and it may draw on standardised instruments to do so reliably. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The case-history or anamnesis (the systematic account of the person's past and development) interview gathers a detailed developmental, family, educational, occupational, medical and social history, often drawing on informants such as parents or spouse. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The mental status examination is an interview that produces a structured snapshot of current mental functioning, covering appearance, speech, mood and affect, thought, perception, cognition, insight and judgement. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The crisis interview is brief, focused and directive, and its priorities are to assess safety, stabilise the person and link them to resources, deferring deeper exploration until the emergency has passed. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The therapeutic interview treats the conversation itself as a means of change, as in counselling and motivational interviewing, so that the skilled dialogue delivers help rather than merely collecting data. [William R. Miller & Stephen Rollnick, Motivational Interviewing]
  • Research interviews apply a fixed protocol so results can be compared across people, while forensic interviews serve legal questions and demand special care about the altered purpose and the limits of confidentiality. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Other recognised types include employment or selection interviews, informant interviews with relatives, stress interviews and group interviews, each shaped by who is present and what decision the interview must inform. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]

Types by structure and direction

  • By structure, interviews run from structured, with fixed questions in a fixed order, through semi-structured, with core questions the interviewer may probe and rephrase, to unstructured, which is open and led by the interviewee. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Theoretical view Structured diagnostic interviews such as the SCID (Structured Clinical Interview for DSM disorders) and the brief MINI standardise both the questions and the scoring, which makes diagnosis far more reliable and comparable across clinicians. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • By direction, interviews are directive when the interviewer controls the topic and pace, and non-directive when the client leads, with the person-centred tradition after Rogers favouring a non-directive, following stance. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • A directive style suits crisis work, risk assessment and structured diagnosis, whereas a non-directive style suits exploratory, insight-oriented and relationship-building work where the person's own narrative matters most. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The clinician usually blends types within a single meeting, for example opening non-directively to build rapport, then moving to a more structured, directive risk assessment when safety must be established. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The choice of type follows the purpose and setting, so an epidemiological survey calls for a fully structured interview while a first psychotherapy session calls for a flexible, largely unstructured and relationship-focused one. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Across settings the semi-structured interview is the most widely used compromise, because it preserves comparability and completeness while leaving room for rapport, probing and the person's unique concerns. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
Mnemonic
I-DC-MART
Intake, Diagnostic, Case-history, Mental status, crisis, therApeutic, Research and forensic, plus Types by structure/direction — the families of interview grouped by purpose and by how structured and directive they are.

4Characteristics of structured, semi-structured and unstructured interviews

Structured interviews use fixed questions scored by rules and so are highly reliable but less flexible, unstructured interviews are open and rich but less reliable and more prone to bias, and semi-structured interviews combine the strengths of both and are preferred for most clinical and research work.

  • A structured interview uses fixed question wording and order with decision rules for scoring, which removes most interviewer variation and makes the method standardised enough for lay interviewers in large surveys. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The great strength of structured interviews is high reliability and comparability, because asking everyone the same questions in the same way means differences in the answers reflect the person, not the interviewer. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Structured interviews also give completeness and reduced bias, since every criterion is covered in order, so the clinician cannot accidentally skip an area or let a first impression steer the questioning. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Their limitations are rigidity and weaker rapport, because the fixed script can feel mechanical and interrogative, may miss the person's unique concerns, and often needs cultural adaptation to fit the setting. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Fully or highly structured instruments such as the DIS, CIDI and MINI are typical examples, and they are used chiefly for research, epidemiology and standardised diagnosis where comparability matters most. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • An unstructured interview is open and flexible, with the general topics set but the specific course determined by the interviewee, so the conversation can follow whatever the person brings as most important. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The strengths of the unstructured interview are flexibility, depth and rapport, because following the person's lead allows rich, detailed material and the discovery of unexpected issues a script would never reach. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Its weaknesses are low reliability and vulnerability to bias, including the halo effect (letting one strong impression colour the whole judgement), first-impression error, confirmation bias and anchoring, which distort what the interviewer hears. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Unstructured interviews are also hard to compare across people and can miss symptoms that were simply never raised, which is why they are weaker for formal diagnosis despite their clinical richness. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The semi-structured interview keeps a set of core questions but lets the trained interviewer probe, rephrase and follow up, so it combines the coverage of a structure with the responsiveness of a conversation. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Semi-structured instruments such as the SCID-5, SCAN, K-SADS, ADIS-5 and CAPS-5 achieve good reliability with trained clinicians while still allowing the clinical judgement that pure structure removes. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Because it balances reliability against rapport and depth, the semi-structured interview is widely preferred for clinical diagnosis and for most clinical research, offering the best of both extremes. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • A practical point is that training demand rises from the structured end, where little training is needed, through the semi-structured middle, to the unstructured end, which needs considerable clinical skill to do well. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • The choice therefore turns on purpose, with structured interviews chosen when reliable, comparable diagnosis is paramount and unstructured interviews chosen when exploration, rapport and the person's own story come first. [Richard S. Sharf, Theories of Psychotherapy and Counseling]
  • Guideline Whatever the structure, the ethical frame is identical, so informed consent, confidentiality and respect, as set out by the APA Ethics Code 2017, apply equally to a rigid survey and to an open therapeutic conversation. [American Psychological Association, Ethics Code 2017, Std 3.10]
Mnemonic
FRRRT
Flexibility, Reliability, Rapport, Richness and Training — the five features on which structured, semi-structured and unstructured interviews differ; reliability rises and flexibility falls as you move toward structure.

5Interviewing skills and the micro-skills hierarchy

Interviewing skills are the small, specific, trainable behaviours, organised by Ivey as a micro-skills hierarchy built on attending and the basic listening sequence, through which the clinician shows attention, draws out the story, and communicates accurate understanding.

  • Theoretical view Ivey's micro-skills are small, specific communication skills that, trained one at a time and then combined, make up competent interviewing, giving teaching and supervision a concrete vocabulary. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • The hierarchy rests on a foundation of attending behaviour, because unless the clinician first shows the person that they are being listened to, no later skill such as questioning or reflection will work well. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • Theoretical view Egan's SOLER captures attending as Squarely facing the person, an Open posture, Leaning slightly toward them, appropriate Eye contact, and a Relaxed, natural manner. [Gerard Egan, The Skilled Helper]
  • A core listening skill is the open-ended question, which begins with what, how or tell me about and invites elaboration, giving the person control and encouraging a fuller, more spontaneous account. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • Closed questions, answerable with a yes, no or short fact, are used to pin down specific detail, diagnosis and risk, but too many in a row turn the interview into an interrogation that shuts the person down. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Encouragers, or minimal prompts such as a nod, a mm-hmm or repeating a key word, are small facilitation skills that keep the person talking without diverting their flow or imposing the clinician's agenda. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • Paraphrasing restates the content of what was said in the clinician's own words, which checks accuracy and shows the person that the facts of their story have been heard correctly. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • Reflection of feeling names the emotion beneath the content, which demonstrates empathy, helps the person label and own their feelings, and often deepens the disclosure that follows. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Summarising pulls together the main themes at transitions and at the end, which organises the material, checks the clinician's understanding, and gives the person a chance to correct or add. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • Theoretical view Ivey groups the first cluster of these skills as the basic listening sequence, namely open and closed questions, encouragers, paraphrase, reflection of feeling and summary, which together draw out the person's story. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • Above the listening skills sit the influencing skills, including interpretation, feedback, self-disclosure, logical consequences and directives, which are used later and more sparingly once the story is understood. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • Theoretical view In motivational interviewing the core skills are summarised as OARS, namely Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections and Summaries, which keep the conversation person-centred and draw out the person's own motivation. [William R. Miller & Stephen Rollnick, Motivational Interviewing]
  • Observation skills sit alongside the listening skills, because the clinician reads the person's non-verbal cues and incongruities at the same time as hearing their words, and adjusts the next response accordingly. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • A unifying idea is intentionality, meaning the skilled interviewer can choose flexibly among many responses and switch when one is not working, rather than relying on a single habitual style. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • The overarching skill is to sequence the micro-skills well, so that attending and listening come first to build the story and trust, and influencing skills follow only once enough understanding and rapport are in place. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
The micro-skills hierarchy
  • Foundation: attending behaviour (Egan's SOLER) and observation.
  • Basic listening sequence: open and closed questions, encouragers, paraphrase, reflection of feeling, summary.
  • Influencing skills (used later): interpretation, feedback, self-disclosure, directives.
  • Motivational interviewing core: OARS (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries).
  • Intentionality: choose and switch skills flexibly to fit the person.
Mnemonic
SOLER + OARS
SOLER (Squarely face, Open posture, Lean in, Eye contact, Relaxed) for attending, and OARS (Open questions, Affirmations, Reflections, Summaries) for responding — the two acronyms that carry the interviewing micro-skills.

6Open-ended questions, clarification, reflection, facilitation and confrontation

These are the core responding skills of the interview: open-ended questions that invite the story, clarification that makes vague statements specific, reflection that mirrors content and feeling, facilitation that keeps the person talking, and confrontation that gently points out discrepancies once rapport can bear it.

Open-ended questions

  • An open-ended question begins with what, how, could or tell me about and invites elaboration, so instead of a yes or no the person gives a fuller, more spontaneous account in their own words. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Open questions encourage exploration, give control to the person and build rapport, which is why skilled interviewers open each new area with one before narrowing down with more specific questions. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • The clinician avoids leading, double-barrelled and stacked questions, and is wary of why questions, which can sound judgemental and provoke defensiveness or intellectualisation rather than honest reflection. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]

Clarification

  • Clarification seeks to make a vague or ambiguous statement specific, for example asking whether 'always' really means every day, so the clinician does not quietly assume a meaning the person never intended. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Clarification checks the interviewer's understanding and signals careful listening, and it is especially important around risk, timelines and symptoms, where a loose word could change the whole clinical picture. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]

Reflection (content and feeling)

  • Paraphrasing, or reflection of content, restates what the person said in the clinician's own words, which confirms accurate hearing and gently invites the person to continue or correct the account. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]
  • Reflection of feeling names the emotion beneath the words, such as noting that the person sounds humiliated and angry, which demonstrates empathy and helps the person recognise and own the feeling. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Reflection of meaning goes a step further to mirror the person's values and beliefs, and in motivational interviewing well-chosen reflections are used deliberately to draw out the person's own change talk. [William R. Miller & Stephen Rollnick, Motivational Interviewing]
  • Summarising is an extended reflection that gathers several statements together at a transition or at the end, which organises the material, checks understanding, and shows the person that their whole account has been followed. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]

Facilitation (encouragers)

  • Facilitation uses minimal verbal and non-verbal prompts, such as mm-hmm, go on, a nod, an expectant pause or echoing a key word, to keep the person talking without steering the content. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Facilitation works because it rewards disclosure and shows interest, so the person feels accompanied rather than interrogated, and it costs the clinician almost nothing in words while adding a great deal in flow. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]

Confrontation

  • Confrontation means pointing out a discrepancy, for instance between words and actions, between two statements, or between stated feeling and visible behaviour, so that a contradiction the person has avoided is gently named. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Confrontation is not an attack; it is a caring, tentative, specific and gentle challenge, offered as an observation with an invitation to explore, such as noting that the person says they are fine yet looks close to tears. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Crucially, confrontation is used only after rapport is established, because a premature or harsh challenge, delivered before trust can bear it, breeds defensiveness and can rupture the alliance. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Well-timed confrontation can be powerful, since it helps the person see a blind spot and take responsibility, but it must always leave them room to respond rather than cornering them into agreement. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
Five core responding skills
  • Open-ended questions: invite elaboration; avoid leading and 'why' questions.
  • Clarification: make vague statements specific; check understanding.
  • Reflection: mirror content (paraphrase), feeling and meaning to show empathy.
  • Facilitation: minimal prompts (mm-hmm, nod, go on) to keep the person talking.
  • Confrontation: gentle, caring challenge of discrepancies, only after rapport.
Mnemonic
OCR-FC
Open questions, Clarification, Reflection, Facilitation, Confrontation — the five core responding skills; the order also hints at timing, since open questions and gentle skills come first and confrontation comes last, only once rapport can bear it.

7Silences in the interview

Silence is an active interviewing tool, not an empty gap: different silences carry different meanings, from reflective and emotional to resistant or anxious, and the skilled clinician reads each one, waits the right amount, and responds rather than rushing to fill it.

  • Silence is best understood as an active and meaningful part of the interview, because the pause itself communicates, and a clinician who rushes to fill every gap can cut off the person's most important disclosures. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • A thoughtful or reflective silence occurs when the person is thinking or working something through, and here the clinician's task is simply to wait and allow it, protecting the space for the person to continue. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • An emotional silence arises when the person is overwhelmed, tearful or moved, and the clinician responds with a warm, unhurried presence and perhaps a gentle reflection rather than a new question. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • A resistant silence signals unwillingness, hostility or resistance (conscious or unconscious reluctance to engage with the process), and it is explored with curiosity rather than forced, since pushing usually deepens the standoff. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • An anxious or awkward silence reflects discomfort or not knowing what to say, where the clinician offers reassurance and a gentle open invitation to ease the person back into speaking. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • A confused silence means the person does not understand or does not know how to answer, so the clinician rephrases the question more simply rather than repeating it or letting the gap grow. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The clinician may also use a strategic silence of their own, a deliberate pause that gives the person room to go deeper, because people often fill a respectful silence with the very material that matters most. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • A practical guide is to allow a pause of roughly five to ten seconds before intervening, long enough to let the person think but not so long that the silence tips into feeling like rejection. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • While waiting, the clinician observes the non-verbal signals, since posture, facial expression, tearfulness or agitation usually reveal which kind of silence this is and therefore how to respond. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • When a response is needed, the clinician uses encouragers, a gentle reflection such as noting that it seems hard to say, or an open invitation, rather than firing in a fresh question that changes the subject. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Theoretical view Gabbard notes that in psychodynamic work silence is often rich material, a form of resistance or communication to be understood, so the therapist tolerates it and reflects on what it may be expressing. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • A benefit of allowing silence is that it gives the person time to think and signals acceptance, which frequently leads to deeper disclosure than a stream of questions ever would. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • The main risk is that a prolonged, unaddressed silence may be experienced as coldness or rejection, so the clinician stays warmly present and does not simply let the person flounder. [Glen O. Gabbard, Long-Term Psychodynamic Psychotherapy: A Basic Text]
  • Silence carries strong cultural meaning, so the clinician respects that comfort with pauses varies across cultures and does not read a culturally normal silence as resistance or incomprehension. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The overarching principle is to treat silence as information to be read and used, timing the response to the kind of silence rather than reflexively filling every gap with talk. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
Reading silences
  • Reflective: the person is thinking; wait and protect the space.
  • Emotional: the person is overwhelmed; stay warmly present, gentle reflection.
  • Resistant: explore with curiosity, do not force.
  • Anxious / confused: reassure or rephrase simply; offer an open invitation.
  • Rule of thumb: allow about 5 to 10 seconds; read non-verbals; respect culture.
Mnemonic
TEARS
Thoughtful, Emotional, Anxious, Resistant and Strategic — the main kinds of silence; read which one it is from the non-verbal signals, wait about 5 to 10 seconds, then respond rather than rushing to fill the gap.

8Verbal and non-verbal components of interviewing

Communication in the interview runs on two channels: verbal components such as content, language and paralanguage, and non-verbal components such as facial expression, posture, interpersonal distance and touch, and the skilled clinician reads both, watches for congruence between them, and interprets patterns rather than isolated cues.

Verbal components

  • The content is what is actually said, including the facts, themes, beliefs and concerns the person reports, and it is the explicit layer the clinician records and checks for accuracy. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Language matters because the clinician uses simple, clear words and avoids jargon, borrowing the person's own vocabulary and metaphors so that explanations land and the person feels understood. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
  • Speech characteristics such as rate, tone, pitch, volume and fluency are themselves clinical data, since pressured, slowed, hesitant or monotonous speech can point to mood, anxiety or thought disorder. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Paralanguage (the vocal features around words, such as sighs, pauses and tone) carries meaning beyond the words, so sighs, pauses, laughter or a tremor in the voice often reveal feeling the content alone conceals. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The style of questions and responses is a verbal component too, because the balance of open and closed questions and the warmth of the clinician's wording shape how much the person discloses. [Allen E. Ivey, Mary Bradford Ivey & Carlos P. Zalaquett, Intentional Interviewing and Counseling]

Non-verbal components

  • Kinesics (communication through body movement and expression) covers facial expression, eye contact, gestures, posture and head nods, together with fidgeting, tension or psychomotor change, all of which the clinician observes continuously. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Eye contact is a key non-verbal cue, useful for judging rapport, engagement and affect, since lowered eyes may accompany depression and a shifting gaze may accompany anxiety, though its meaning varies by culture. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Theoretical view Proxemics (the use of interpersonal distance), described by Edward T. Hall, distinguishes intimate, personal, social and public zones, with the personal zone of roughly one and a half to four feet suiting most interviews. [Edward T. Hall, The Hidden Dimension (proxemics)]
  • Haptics (communication through touch) must be handled cautiously and only with consent, because although a touch can convey support, it can also be intrusive or be misread, especially across gender and culture. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Appearance and the environment send non-verbal messages too, so the person's grooming, and the clinician's seating, lighting, privacy and even a visible clock, all shape how safe and respected the person feels. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Chronemics (the use and meaning of time) covers punctuality, pace and how time is used, and starting on time, not rushing and ending on time all communicate respect and reliability to the person. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • The most important non-verbal principle is congruence, because non-verbal behaviour often reveals feelings the words conceal, as when a person smiles while describing a tragedy, flagging a mismatch worth gently exploring. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • The clinician interprets patterns, not isolated cues, and avoids over-reading a single gesture, since a crossed arm or a pause means little alone but much when it fits a consistent pattern. [Nitin Sethi, Psychiatric History Taking]
  • Culture strongly shapes non-verbal meaning, so in some Indian and other contexts direct eye contact may read as disrespectful, a head wobble may signal assent, and expected interpersonal distance differs, all of which the clinician respects. [Gerald Corey, Theory and Practice of Counseling and Psychotherapy]
  • Theoretical view Wolberg underlines that the clinician's own non-verbal behaviour is a technique, since an attentive facial expression, a nod or a well-judged gesture conveys acceptance and encourages the person to continue. [Lewis R. Wolberg, The Technique of Psychotherapy]
Two channels, one message
  • Verbal: content, language (simple, no jargon), speech characteristics, paralanguage, style.
  • Non-verbal: kinesics, eye contact, proxemics (Hall), haptics, appearance, chronemics.
  • Congruence: watch for words and body not matching (smiling while sad).
  • Interpret patterns, not single cues; avoid over-reading.
  • Always read non-verbal cues through the person's culture.
Mnemonic
CLiPS + KEPHAC
Verbal = CLiPS (Content, Language, Paralanguage, Speech characteristics, Style); non-verbal = KEPHAC (Kinesics, Eye contact, Proxemics, Haptics, Appearance, Chronemics) — read both channels and check they are congruent.
