What a “model of illness” means

A model of illness is a way of explaining why a disorder happens — what causes it, what makes it more likely, and how those causes lead, step by step, to the symptoms. For each disorder we ask three linked questions:

  • Etiology (the cause) — the underlying disease or root cause (e.g. a specific brain protein building up).
  • Pathophysiology (how it damages the body) — the chain of biological events from the cause to the symptoms (the “mechanism”).
  • Risk / causal factors — the things (genes, age, lifestyle, environment) that raise or lower the chance of getting the illness.
The F00–F09 group is special. These are the “organic” mental disorders — organic means they have a known physical cause in the brain or body (a disease, injury, toxin or chemical upset), rather than being “functional” disorders where no single physical lesion is found. So for this cluster the models are strongly biological, but psychological and social factors still shape who develops them, how fast, and how severe.

Plain-language promise & scope. Difficult terms are explained in brackets the first time they appear. This is an educational synthesis of research for study; it is not medical, diagnostic or treatment advice. It covers etiology/models — classification detail lives in the separate F00–F09 ICD-10/ICD-11 handbook.

The biopsychosocial frame — the master model

Almost every modern model of any mental disorder uses the biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977): illness arises from the interaction of biological, psychological and social factors, not one alone. Even for “organic” brain disease, this frame explains why two people with the same brain pathology can have very different outcomes.

Cognitive reserve — the single most useful psychosocial idea here

Cognitive reserve means the brain's built-up “spare capacity” — from education, mentally demanding work and an active social/intellectual life — that lets a person tolerate more brain damage before symptoms appear. Two brains can have the same amount of Alzheimer's pathology, yet the one with higher reserve stays functional longer. This is why the same biological disease produces different clinical pictures, and why education and lifelong mental activity are protective.

The common biological mechanisms behind F00–F09

Across the organic cluster, a small set of mechanisms recur. Recognising them makes every individual disorder easier to understand — most dementias are a specific combination of these.

“Mixed pathology” is the rule, not the exception. Recent large brain-bank studies show that most older people with dementia have more than one of these processes at once (e.g. Alzheimer + vascular + Lewy body together). So the neat ICD categories are, biologically, often overlapping — a key modern insight (Continuum 2022; Mayo Clinic reviews 2023–2025).

F00 · Dementia in Alzheimer's disease — models of causation

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the commonest cause of dementia (a progressive loss of memory and thinking severe enough to affect daily life). Its causation is now understood as a multi-hypothesis, cascade problem — several linked processes, unfolding over 15–20 years before symptoms appear. No single hypothesis fully explains it, and the field increasingly favours an integrated model (Springer Mol Neurobiol 2025 latest).

The hallmark pathology (what is found in the brain)

Two microscopic hallmarks define AD (Nature/PMC reviews 2024–2025 latest):

  • Amyloid-beta plaques — sticky clumps of a protein fragment (amyloid-beta, or Aβ) that build up between neurons.
  • Neurofibrillary tangles — twisted threads of a protein called tau that build up inside neurons when tau becomes “hyperphosphorylated” (over-loaded with phosphate tags, which makes it fall off its normal scaffold and clump).

· The amyloid cascade hypothesis STRONG (but debated)

classic Hardy & Higgins 1992 · latest integrated reviews PMC 2024–2025

The amyloid cascade hypothesis is the most influential model. In plain terms: the build-up of amyloid-beta is the first domino that sets off everything else.

1. APP (a normal brain protein) is cut abnormally → excess amyloid-beta
→
2. Amyloid-beta clumps into plaques between neurons
→
3. Triggers tau tangles inside neurons + inflammation
→
4. Synapses (connections) fail; neurons die
→
5. Brain shrinks → memory loss & dementia
Why it matters & why it's debated. This hypothesis drove the new anti-amyloid antibody drugs (lecanemab, donanemab) that clear plaques and modestly slow decline — strong support that amyloid is causal. But amyloid removal only slows, not stops, the disease, and some people have heavy amyloid without dementia — so amyloid is necessary but not sufficient alone. Hence the shift to integrated models (PMC 2024; Springer 2025 latest).

· Tau, inflammation, cholinergic & other hypotheses

Tau / tauopathy hypothesis STRONG

latest Targeting tau in AD PMC 2023

Tau tangles correlate with symptoms better than amyloid does: the spread of tau through the brain tracks closely with where and when neurons die and which abilities are lost. Many now see amyloid as the trigger and tau as the executioner that actually spreads the damage (tau can propagate cell-to-cell, “prion-like”).

Cholinergic hypothesis MODERATE (classic basis of current drugs)

classic Bartus 1982

AD destroys neurons that make acetylcholine (a brain chemical essential for memory and attention). This loss explains much of the memory impairment and is the basis of cholinesterase-inhibitor drugs (donepezil etc.), which boost remaining acetylcholine. It explains symptoms and treatment but not the root cause.

Neuroinflammation hypothesis STRONG / rising

latest Integrated view of amyloid, tau & inflammation PMC 2024–2025

The brain's immune cells (microglia) try to clear amyloid but, when chronically over-activated, release inflammatory chemicals that accelerate neuron death. Inflammation is now seen as a core amplifier linking amyloid and tau, not a side-effect — and a major drug target.

Mitochondrial / oxidative-stress & vascular hypotheses MODERATE

latest PMC 2022–2025

Failing mitochondria (the cell's power stations) and a build-up of free radicals (damaging reactive molecules — “oxidative stress”) injure neurons and interact with amyloid/tau. Vascular damage (see F01) very commonly co-exists and worsens AD — the “two-hit” vascular contribution.

Other emerging hypotheses EMERGING

latest Springer Mol Neurobiol 2025

Recent reviews also weigh metal dyshomeostasis (imbalance of brain metals like copper/zinc/iron), autophagy–lysosomal failure (the cell's “waste-disposal” system breaking down so junk proteins accumulate), and infectious triggers (e.g. herpes virus, gum-disease bacteria as possible seeds) — all still being tested.

Risk & protective factors (who gets it — the biopsychosocial layer)

The strongest single risk factor is age. Genetics range from rare deterministic mutations to the common risk gene. Crucially, an estimated ~40–45% of dementia is attributable to modifiable factors (Lancet Commission), which is where psychology and society meet biology.

Remember F00 — “A-T-I-C”

Amyloid (the trigger) → Tau (the executioner that spreads) → Inflammation (the amplifier) → Cholinergic loss (explains memory symptoms & the drugs). Over it all: age + genes (APOE) vs cognitive reserve + modifiable lifestyle.

The key subtype insight: the younger the onset, the more the model tilts toward single-gene, amyloid-overproduction causation; the older the onset, the more it tilts toward impaired clearance + APOE + vascular/lifestyle and mixed pathology.

F01 · Vascular dementia — the blood-supply model

Vascular dementia (VaD) is dementia caused by damage to the brain's blood supply — the second commonest cause of dementia and, with AD, part of most “mixed” dementias. The core idea is simple: if brain tissue does not get enough blood (and therefore oxygen and glucose), it is injured or dies, and cognition declines. It accounts for at least 20–40% of dementia (PMC 2022 latest).

1. Vascular risk factors (high BP, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking)
→
2. Blood-vessel damage: strokes (large) or small-vessel disease (chronic)
→
3. Reduced blood flow + blood-brain barrier breakdown (the brain's protective filter leaks)
→
4. Tissue injury, white-matter damage, inflammation
→
5. Cognitive decline → vascular dementia

Current mechanistic model (latest research)

latest Vascular Dementia: Pathophysiology to Therapeutic Frontiers PMC 2025; Nature reviews 2026

Modern reviews describe VaD as a set of processes that converge on brain-cell injury:

  • Endothelial dysfunction — the inner lining of blood vessels stops working properly, so vessels can't regulate flow.
  • Blood-brain barrier (BBB) breakdown — the protective filter between blood and brain leaks, letting harmful substances in.
  • Chronic neuroinflammation and oxidative stress (immune over-activation and damaging free radicals).
  • Impaired glymphatic clearance — the brain's waste-drainage system (the “glymphatic” system) fails, so toxins accumulate (an emerging idea linking VaD and AD).
  • White-matter demyelination — loss of the insulating sheath around nerve fibres, slowing brain “wiring”.
  • Synaptic failure — the connections between neurons stop working.

Genetic contribution MODERATE

latest PMC 2025

Susceptibility depends partly on genes — notably NOTCH3 mutations (which cause CADASIL, an inherited small-vessel disease) and various vascular risk gene variants.

The clinical fingerprint of the model

Because the cause is vascular events, VaD classically shows a “stepwise” decline (sudden drops after each stroke, then plateaus) rather than the smooth glide of AD — and early on, executive function (planning, attention, speed) suffers more than pure memory. This directly reflects the model: damage follows the blood vessels, not a spreading protein.

Remember F01 — “Vessels → Voids”

Vascular risk factors damage vessels → strokes/small-vessel disease → brain tissue starved → stepwise decline, executive-first. It is the most preventable dementia: control blood pressure, sugar, cholesterol, smoking.

F02 · Dementia in other diseases classified elsewhere — overview

F02 groups dementias caused by specific, named diseases. Each has its own model, but they cluster into three model-types:

  • Proteinopathies (a specific protein misfolds): Pick/FTD (tau, TDP-43), Parkinson/Lewy body (α-synuclein), CJD (prion protein), Huntington (huntingtin).
  • Infective: HIV dementia.
  • Others: metabolic, endocrine, toxic, deficiency states.

· F02.0 Frontotemporal dementia (Pick's disease) STRONG

latest AD & FTD pathophysiology review PMC 2024–2025

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD) is caused by degeneration of the frontal and temporal lobes (the front and side parts of the brain that control personality, behaviour and language). Because these areas run social behaviour and language, FTD's model predicts its signature: personality/behaviour change or language breakdown come first, with memory relatively spared early — the opposite of AD.

  • Protein model: abnormal build-up of tau (in some subtypes; “Pick bodies” are tau clumps) or TDP-43 (a different misfolding protein) — so FTD is a mixed group of proteinopathies.
  • Genetic model: a strongly heritable dementia — mutations in MAPT (the tau gene), GRN (progranulin) and C9orf72 account for many familial cases (the C9orf72 expansion also links FTD to motor neurone disease).
  • Age: a leading cause of younger-onset dementia (often 45–65).

Remember

“Front = personality & language first, memory later” — tau/TDP-43 proteinopathy of the frontal & temporal lobes; young onset; strongly genetic.

· F02.1 Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD) — the prion model STRONG

CJD is caused by a prion — a normal brain protein (PrP) that misfolds into an abnormal, infectious shape and then forces neighbouring normal proteins to misfold too, spreading like a chain reaction. This “protein-only” infectious model is unique and was Nobel-recognised.

  • Mechanism: misfolded prion protein accumulates, punches holes in brain tissue (giving the classic “spongiform”, sponge-like appearance), causing rapid neuron death.
  • Course: a rapidly progressive dementia (weeks to months) with prominent myoclonus (sudden muscle jerks) — the model explains the speed: it is a self-amplifying chain reaction.
  • Forms: sporadic (most common, no known cause), genetic (PRNP gene), and acquired/transmissible (e.g. variant CJD linked to contaminated beef; iatrogenic from medical exposure).

Remember

“Rapid dementia + myoclonus + spongy brain” — a self-propagating misfolded prion protein.

· F02.2 Huntington's disease — the single-gene model STRONG

Huntington's disease (HD) is the clearest purely genetic model in the whole cluster: it is caused by a single dominant gene (the HTT gene) with an expanded “CAG repeat” (a stretch of DNA code that is abnormally repeated too many times). One copy is enough to cause the disease (autosomal dominant), so a child of an affected parent has a 50% chance.

  • Mechanism: the faulty gene makes an abnormal huntingtin protein that becomes toxic and destroys neurons, especially in the basal ganglia (deep brain structures controlling movement) and later the cortex.
  • “Anticipation”: the more CAG repeats, the earlier and more severe the onset — and repeats tend to lengthen across generations.
  • Clinical fingerprint: the model predicts the triad — chorea (involuntary dance-like movements, from basal-ganglia damage), cognitive decline, and psychiatric symptoms (depression, irritability, high suicide risk).

Remember

“One dominant gene, CAG repeats → chorea + dementia + psychiatric symptoms” — the textbook single-gene dementia.

· F02.3 Dementia in Parkinson's disease & Lewy body dementia — the α-synuclein model STRONG

latest Alpha-synuclein reviews PMC 2024–2025; Mayo neuropathology 2025

Parkinson's disease dementia (PDD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) share one core model: the misfolding and clumping of a protein called α-synuclein into deposits named Lewy bodies inside neurons. Because these accumulate in movement, alertness and perception systems, the model predicts the signature features.

1. α-synuclein misfolds & clumps into Lewy bodies
→
2. Spreads (prion-like) through brainstem → midbrain → cortex
→
3. Kills dopamine neurons (movement) & disrupts cortex (thinking/perception)
→
4. Parkinsonism + fluctuating cognition + visual hallucinations
  • PDD vs DLB — the “1-year rule”: same pathology, timing differs. If movement problems (parkinsonism) come first and dementia arrives >1 year later → Parkinson's disease dementia. If dementia comes first or within 1 year of parkinsonism → dementia with Lewy bodies.
  • Signature features (predicted by the model): fluctuating alertness, recurrent visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder (acting out dreams), and severe sensitivity to antipsychotics (a crucial safety point — dopamine-blockers can cause dangerous reactions).
  • Mixed pathology: latest neuropathology (Mayo 2025 latest) shows Lewy pathology very often co-occurs with amyloid and tau, which accelerates decline.

Remember

“α-synuclein Lewy bodies → parkinsonism + fluctuations + visual hallucinations; NEVER give strong antipsychotics”. The 1-year rule separates PDD from DLB.

· F02.4 HIV-associated dementia — the infective/inflammatory model MODERATE

Here the cause is an infection: the HIV virus enters the brain (carried in by infected immune cells) and drives dementia not mainly by infecting neurons directly, but by triggering chronic inflammation.

  • Mechanism: HIV infects the brain's immune cells (microglia and macrophages), which then release neurotoxic inflammatory chemicals and viral proteins that injure neurons — a mainly subcortical pattern (slowing, apathy, motor and memory problems).
  • Modern context: with effective antiretroviral therapy, severe HIV dementia is now much rarer, replaced by milder “HIV-associated neurocognitive disorders” — a good example of how treating the cause changes the model's outcome.

Remember

“Virus in immune cells → inflammation → subcortical dementia” — largely preventable/treatable with antiretrovirals.

· F02.8 Dementia in other specified diseases — the “reversible causes” group

This category is clinically vital because it contains many potentially reversible dementias — where treating the cause can improve cognition. The model is “a body/brain problem starves or poisons the neurons”.

CauseModel (plain)
Vitamin B12 / folate / thiamine deficiencyNeurons lack an essential building block/co-factor → dysfunction (thiamine lack → Wernicke-Korsakoff, see F04).

Why this matters: the model here delivers a hopeful message — always screen for reversible causes (bloods, imaging), because unlike the proteinopathies, some of these can be halted or reversed.

F03 · Unspecified dementia — the model when the cause is unknown

F03 is used when dementia is clearly present but the specific disease cannot (yet) be identified. There is no separate model — the true cause is one (or a mix) of the F00–F02 mechanisms above; it is simply not yet pinned down. In practice, many F03 cases in older people turn out to be mixed pathology (AD + vascular + Lewy) — which is exactly why a single label is hard to assign.

Model takeaway: “unspecified” is a statement about our knowledge, not about a distinct cause. The likeliest underlying model is mixed neurodegenerative + vascular.

F04 · Organic amnesic syndrome — the memory-circuit model STRONG

The organic amnesic (memory-loss) syndrome is dementia's “focused” cousin: a severe memory impairment with other thinking largely intact, caused by damage to the brain's specific memory circuit — the hippocampus and the connected structures of the medial temporal lobe and diencephalon (the Papez / limbic memory circuit).

  • Core deficit: inability to form new long-term memories (anterograde amnesia) ± loss of some recent past memories (retrograde amnesia), with preserved immediate attention and old skills.
  • Classic cause — Korsakoff syndrome: chronic thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency, usually from alcohol misuse, damages the mammillary bodies and thalamus (memory-circuit hubs). Often preceded by Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion + eye-movement problems + unsteadiness) — a medical emergency that is reversible with prompt thiamine. A hallmark is confabulation (unintentionally filling memory gaps with invented details).
  • Other causes: hypoxia (oxygen lack), herpes encephalitis, head injury, thalamic stroke, brain tumours.

Remember F04

“Memory circuit knocked out, rest intact” — classically thiamine deficiency → Korsakoff (with confabulation); give thiamine early to prevent it.

F05 · Delirium — the “acute brain failure” model

Delirium is an acute (sudden), fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness caused by a medical problem — think of it as temporary, reversible brain failure under stress. Unlike dementia (slow, chronic), delirium comes on over hours to days and typically clears when the cause is treated. Its model is captured by a famous equation: vulnerability × insult.

The core biological hypotheses (why the brain “fails”)

latest Mechanisms of delirium in critical illness Frontiers 2024; Pathophysiology & biomarkers of delirium PMC 2024

Delirium is a “final common pathway” that many causes converge on. The leading, interacting hypotheses:

1. Neuroinflammation hypothesis STRONG

A bodily illness (infection, surgery, trauma) releases inflammatory signals (cytokines) that cross into the brain, activate microglia, and disrupt neuron function — the leading model for delirium after infection or operations.

2. Neurotransmitter-imbalance hypothesis STRONG

Classically a deficit of acetylcholine (needed for attention) plus an excess of dopamine — which is why anticholinergic drugs (that block acetylcholine) commonly trigger delirium, and why dopamine-excess produces the agitated, hallucinating form.

3. Oxidative-stress / energy-failure hypothesis MODERATE

Low oxygen or impaired brain metabolism means neurons can't make enough energy → widespread dysfunction. Explains delirium in hypoxia, sepsis and metabolic upset.

4. Network-disconnection & stress hypotheses EMERGING

Imaging shows delirium as a breakdown of brain-network connectivity; chronic stress hormones (cortisol) and disrupted sleep/circadian rhythm also contribute.

Predisposing & precipitating factors (from systematic reviews)

latest Predisposing & precipitating factors systematic review, PMC 2023

Predisposing (who is vulnerable)

  • Older age
  • Pre-existing dementia / cognitive impairment
  • Frailty & multiple medical conditions
  • Sensory impairment (poor sight/hearing)
  • Previous delirium; depression

Precipitating (the triggers)

  • Infection (esp. urinary, chest); sepsis
  • Surgery & anaesthesia (post-operative delirium)
  • Medications (anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids) & polypharmacy
  • Metabolic upset, dehydration, low oxygen, pain, constipation, urinary retention
  • Alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal; ICU environment, sleep deprivation

Remember F05 — delirium = vulnerability × insult

A brittle brain (old, demented, frail) + a trigger (infection, drugs, surgery, metabolic upset) → acute inflammation + acetylcholine deficit/dopamine excess → sudden, fluctuating inattention. Usually reversible — find and fix the cause.

F06 · Other mental disorders due to brain damage/disease — overview

F06 is a crucial and often-forgotten category: psychiatric symptoms produced directly by a physical brain or body problem. The overarching model is “a physical lesion or chemical upset in a particular brain system generates a symptom that looks psychiatric” — so the same symptom (hallucinations, mania, anxiety) has an organic cause rather than a primary psychiatric one. This is why every first-episode psychosis, mania or severe anxiety deserves screening for an organic cause.

Remember F06

“Psychiatric symptom, physical cause” — hallucinations, delusions, mania, depression or anxiety produced directly by brain disease, endocrine upset, drugs or metabolic problems. Always screen for the organic driver.

F07 · Personality & behavioural disorders due to brain disease/injury — the “lesion changes character” model

F07 covers lasting changes in personality and behaviour caused by brain damage — the model is that injury to specific brain regions (especially the frontal lobes) removes the normal “brakes” on behaviour.

  • F07.0 Organic personality disorder: after frontal-lobe damage (head injury, stroke, tumour, infection), a person may show disinhibition (loss of social restraint), apathy, irritability, poor judgement, emotional shallowness — the classic “frontal lobe syndrome”. The historic case of Phineas Gage (a railway worker whose personality changed after a rod destroyed his frontal lobe) is the founding example: the frontal lobe governs personality and impulse control.
  • F07.1 Postencephalitic syndrome: behavioural change remaining after recovery from brain infection (encephalitis).
  • F07.2 Postconcussional syndrome: headache, dizziness, poor concentration, irritability and fatigue after head injury — a mix of physical (mild brain injury) and psychological (stress, expectation) factors, a genuinely biopsychosocial model.

Remember F07

“Damage the frontal lobe → change the personality” (disinhibition/apathy) — Phineas Gage is the archetype. Postconcussional syndrome blends brain injury with psychological factors.

F09 · Unspecified organic or symptomatic mental disorder

F09 is the residual code: there is clear evidence of an organic/symptomatic cause (a physical brain or body problem is producing the mental disorder) but it cannot be assigned to F00–F07. There is no distinct model — the true mechanism is one of those already described, simply not yet specified.

Model takeaway: like F03, F09 reflects the limits of current information, not a unique cause. The clinical duty is to keep searching for the specific — and possibly reversible — organic driver.

Three unifying messages

  • 1. Protein or plumbing or poison. Most F00–F09 disorders are a misfolded protein (AD, FTD, Lewy, CJD, HD), a blood-supply problem (vascular), or a direct insult (delirium, HIV, deficiency, toxins, injury).
  • 2. Biology loads the gun; psychosocial factors pull the trigger and shape the outcome. Cognitive reserve, education, vascular lifestyle, social support and prompt medical care change who becomes ill, how fast, and how severely.
  • 3. Mixed & modifiable. Real patients usually have mixed pathology, and a large share of risk (and some causes entirely) is modifiable or reversible — the most hopeful theme in the whole cluster.
