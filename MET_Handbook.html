Motivational Interviewing (MI)Motivational Enhancement Therapy (MET)
A communication style / clinical method — a way of being with people to evoke their own motivation. Not time-limited; it has no fixed number of sessions. A brief, manualised treatment that applies MI and adds a key ingredient: structured, personalised assessment feedback delivered motivationally. In Project MATCH it was 4 sessions over 12 weeks.
Spirit: partnership, acceptance, compassion, evocation. Spirit of MI + systematic feedback (the "E" = enhancement) + a negotiated change plan.

For opioids assess overdose risk and arrange naloxone and medications for opioid use disorder (OUD) where indicated. MET complements appropriate medical and addiction treatment.

1 · Introduction & Theoretical Foundation

Definition, purpose & rationale

MET is a brief, person-centred, directive counselling approach that mobilises the patient's own motivation and resources for change. Its purpose is not to teach coping skills (that is CBT) but to resolve ambivalence and build commitment so the patient initiates and sustains change. The rationale: motivation is one of the strongest predictors of treatment engagement and outcome, and it can be influenced by the clinician's style.

Person-centred, collaborative treatment

The therapist is a partner, not an expert fixing a passive patient. Change is drawn out of the person (evocation), not installed. The patient is the authority on their own life.

Motivation is a changeable state, not a trait

"Unmotivated" describes a moment in an interaction, not a fixed personality. The clinician's style can increase or decrease it.

Ambivalence

Feeling two ways ("I want to quit and I don't") is the normal core of addiction, not denial or pathology. MET works through ambivalence rather than treating it as an obstacle.

Intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation

Extrinsic (court, family, job) can get a person in the door; intrinsic reasons (their own values, health, children) sustain change. MET converts external pressure into internal reasons.

Change talk vs sustain talk

Change talk = the patient's own statements favouring change. Sustain talk = statements favouring the status quo. MET selectively evokes and reinforces change talk.

Self-efficacy & personal responsibility

Belief that change is possible predicts it. The responsibility and choice to change stay with the patient — this reduces resistance.

Empathy & alliance

Accurate, non-judgemental empathy is the single most consistent therapist factor linked to better substance-use outcomes.

Why confrontation backfires

Arguing, blaming, labelling ("you're an addict in denial"), and lecturing provoke psychological reactance: the patient defends the status quo and talks themselves into continued use. In MET, if the patient is arguing for not changing, the therapist is doing something wrong — step back, reflect, emphasise autonomy. "Roll with resistance" rather than meet it head-on.

Core clinical frameworks (useful, not diagnostic)

Caution: Stage, readiness, and the importance/confidence rulers are clinical frameworks and conversation tools, not diagnostic tests. A single ruler number is never a diagnosis and must not label a patient as "resistant" or "unmotivated."

Decisional balance weighs the pros/cons of using vs changing. Importance & confidence rulers (0–10) quickly map why change matters and whether the person believes they can — two independent dimensions that need different responses.

2 · Assessment Before MET

In the Project MATCH model, a structured assessment precedes MET, and its results are fed back to the patient in Session 1. Good assessment is itself motivational when done in MI style.

Instruments commonly used

WHO ASSIST, AUDIT (alcohol), Fagerström (nicotine), timeline follow-back, readiness ruler / SOCRATES, and a drinker/user profile for feedback. SUPPLEMENTARY In Indian de-addiction settings, combine with a clinical history, physical exam, and labs (LFTs, viral serology for injectors).

Sample clinical documentation (de-identified)

28-y/o male, inhaled "smack"/heroin ~0.5 g/day ×3 yrs, also smokes 20 cig/day. Last use 6 h ago. Cravings 8/10, triggered by peer group & evening boredom. One prior detox (relapsed in 3 wks). Withdrawal: body aches, insomnia; no seizures; no injecting, no reported overdose. Consequences: debt, conflict with wife, missed work. ⚠ Flag immediately for medical/psychiatric care: suicidal ideation, overdose risk, severe alcohol/benzo withdrawal risk (seizure/DT history), pregnancy, acute medical illness.

3 · MET Structure & Session-by-Session Guide

MANUAL MODEL Project MATCH MET = pre-treatment assessment battery → 4 individual sessions over 12 weeks (Sessions 1–2 in the first fortnight; Sessions 3–4 as follow-through/booster at ~weeks 6 and 12). This structure is not sacred: in de-addiction OPDs the themes below can be delivered in fewer or more contacts. Adapt order and number to your programme and the patient's readiness — do not force every patient into four fixed sessions.

Assessment battery — structured, in MI style
↓
Session 1 — Engagement + personalised feedback + evoke reasons for change
↓
Session 2 — Explore ambivalence, develop discrepancy, strengthen change talk
↓
Session 3 — Negotiate a concrete change plan; identify triggers & coping
↓
Session 4 (when appropriate) — Consolidate, relapse-prevention, continuing care
Session 1 · Engagement, Assessment Feedback & Motivation

1. Objectives

  • Build a collaborative, non-judgemental alliance.
  • Deliver personalised assessment feedback (the "enhancement").
  • Elicit the patient's own concerns and reasons for change.
  • Begin developing discrepancy; map importance & confidence.

2. Clinical rationale

Objective feedback given without argument lets the patient draw their own conclusions, raising intrinsic motivation while preserving autonomy.

3. Preparation

Compile assessment results into a simple feedback sheet (use levels vs norms, risk markers, consequences). Have importance/confidence rulers ready.

4. Step-by-step

  1. Welcome; set agenda collaboratively; clarify confidentiality & limits.
  2. Ask permission to share feedback: Elicit–Provide–Elicit.
  3. Present one finding at a time; pause; reflect the patient's reaction.
  4. Summarise; ask "What do you make of all this?"
  5. Use importance & confidence rulers; follow with "why that number?" and "why not lower?"
  6. Elicit reasons for change; end with a summary of their change talk.

5–9. Scripts, questions & responses

Therapist (asking permission): "Would it be okay if I shared what the assessment showed, and then you tell me what fits and what doesn't?"
Patient: "I guess."
Therapist (provide, neutral): "Your reported use puts you in the high-risk range compared with most men your age. How does that land for you?"
Patient: "I don't drink more than my friends." (sustain talk)
Therapist (double-sided reflection): "So on one hand this feels normal in your circle, and on the other, seeing it in the high-risk range is a bit of a surprise."
Patient: "Yeah… the liver thing did scare me a little." (change talk — reasons)
Therapist (reflect + evoke): "Your health matters to you. What else worries you?"

Change talk to listen for:

"The money's getting bad." "My daughter asked why I'm always tired."   Sustain talk: "It's the only way I relax." "Everyone I know uses."

10. Worksheet

Pair with #2 Reasons for Change, #6/7 Rulers, #8/9 Why this number.

11. Common mistakes

  • Lecturing from the feedback sheet; arguing with denial.
  • Pushing abstinence before the patient is ready.
  • Filling silences instead of reflecting.

12. Homework (optional)

Notice reasons for change during the week; optional #11 How use affects my life.

13. Documentation

Record importance/confidence, key change & sustain talk, agreed next step. (See §10.)

14. Move on when…

Rapport is established and the patient has voiced some concern — even small. If still precontemplative, keep eliciting; do not jump to planning.

Session 2 · Exploring Ambivalence & Strengthening Commitment

1. Objectives

  • Review the week.
  • Explore pros/cons of using and of changing (decisional balance).
  • Develop discrepancy between use and personal values.
  • Evoke & strengthen change talk; respond to sustain talk without arguing.
  • Explore confidence and barriers.

2. Rationale

Resolving ambivalence in the patient's own words shifts the balance toward change and increases commitment.

3. Preparation

Review Session 1 notes and any homework; have decisional-balance and values worksheets ready.

4. Step-by-step

  1. Open-ended review: "How have things been since we met?"
  2. Explore the good things and the less-good things about use (balance).
  3. Explore benefits/costs of changing.
  4. Connect to values ("What kind of father do you want to be?").
  5. Selectively reflect & summarise change talk; ask the key question: "Where does this leave you?"

5–9. Scripts

Therapist (eliciting both sides): "What are the good things about using — the things you'd miss?"
Patient: "It calms me down after work."
Therapist: "It helps you unwind. And the not-so-good side?"
Patient: "I'm broke, and my wife and I fight about it."
Therapist (develop discrepancy via values): "You've told me family is the most important thing to you. How does the fighting sit with that?"
Patient: "It's not who I want to be. I need to sort this out." (need + commitment)
Responding to sustain talk (no argument):
Patient: "I can stop any time I want."
Therapist (reflect + autonomy): "You're confident you have control over this — and it's completely your call what you do with it. If you did decide to test that, what might you try first?"

DARN change talk to evoke:

Desire "I want…" · Ability "I could…" · Reasons "because…" · Need "I have to…"

10. Worksheet

#3/4 Benefits & costs, #5 Decisional balance, #10 Values & goals, #14 Barriers.

11. Mistakes

  • Taking the "pro-change" side of an argument (patient then defends use).
  • Only logging cons of use — ignoring the real benefits the patient perceives.

12. Homework

Complete decisional balance; notice one value that conflicts with use.

13–14.

Document shift in change talk. Move to planning when the patient signals readiness ("I need to do something").

Session 3 · Developing a Change Plan

1. Objectives

  • Confirm readiness & chosen goal.
  • Identify high-risk situations/triggers.
  • Explore coping strategies & supports.
  • Negotiate realistic, specific steps & treatment options; address adherence barriers.

2. Rationale

A plan the patient authors (not one prescribed) carries commitment. Planning only works once readiness is present.

3. Preparation

Review change talk so far; have the Change Plan and First-Three-Steps worksheets ready; know local referral options (OST/MOUD, detox, groups).

4. Step-by-step

  1. Recap motivation: "Last time you said… where are you now?"
  2. Clarify the goal in the patient's words (abstinence or reduction).
  3. Map triggers & high-risk situations.
  4. Brainstorm coping options; the patient chooses.
  5. Agree first concrete steps, who/what/when; discuss referrals & adherence barriers.
  6. Affirm; summarise the plan back.

5–9. Scripts

Therapist (key question): "Given everything we've talked about, what do you think you'll do?"
Patient: "I want to stop the smack. I'll talk to the doctor about the medicine." (commitment + taking steps)
Therapist (elicit plan, menu of options): "Great. What's the very first step this week — the doctor's appointment, telling your wife, or changing your evening routine? What feels doable?"
Patient: "Book the doctor and avoid my old group in the evenings."
Therapist (anticipate barrier): "If a friend turns up with it anyway, what's your plan?"

10. Worksheet

#13 Triggers, #15 Coping & support, #16 Change plan, #17 First three steps, ★ Short- & long-term goals.

11. Mistakes

  • Writing the plan for the patient.
  • Over-ambitious steps that set up failure.
  • Ignoring medical/pharmacotherapy coordination for opioids/alcohol.

12. Homework

Carry out the first step; keep a trigger log.

13–14.

Document the plan verbatim. Proceed to Session 4 if consolidation/continuing care is appropriate.

Session 4 · Consolidation & Continuing-Care Planning include only when appropriate

1. Objectives

  • Review progress & setbacks without judgement.
  • Reinforce self-efficacy; affirm effort.
  • Surface remaining ambivalence; revisit the plan.
  • Strengthen relapse-prevention; arrange follow-up/continuing care/referral.

2. Rationale

Early change is fragile; consolidating gains and planning for lapses protects against discouragement and dropout.

3. Preparation

Review the change plan and interim progress; have relapse-prevention and continuing-care worksheets ready.

4. Step-by-step

  1. Review: "What's gone well? What's been hard?"
  2. Affirm concrete successes; attribute them to the patient's effort.
  3. Normalise any lapse; extract learning.
  4. Update plan; identify ongoing supports.
  5. Arrange continuing care (OST clinic, group, follow-up date, family involvement).

5–9. Scripts

Therapist (after a lapse): "You used once last week and then stopped yourself. That slip doesn't erase three good weeks — what helped you get back on track?"
Patient: "I called my brother instead of my old friends."
Therapist (affirm + build efficacy): "You reached for support at the exact right moment. That's a skill you can use again."

10. Worksheet

#18 Progress review, #19 Learning from a lapse, #20 Continuing-care plan.

11. Mistakes

  • Treating a lapse as failure/shaming.
  • Ending without a concrete follow-up pathway.

12–14.

Homework: keep continuing-care appointments. Document outcome, residual ambivalence, referrals, follow-up plan. Revisit earlier themes if ambivalence has resurged.

4 · Motivational Interviewing Skills Used in MET

The engine of MET is OARS delivered in the MI spirit. For each skill: Purpose → Therapist example → Patient response → Appropriate follow-up.

OARS — the core micro-skills

O — Open questions. Purpose: invite elaboration, not yes/no.
T: "What's it like for you on a day you don't use?" → P: "Restless, honestly." → Follow-up: reflect: "Hard to settle without it."
A — Affirmations. Purpose: recognise genuine strengths/effort (not flattery).
T: "You came today even though it's uncomfortable — that takes resolve." → P: "Didn't think of it that way." → Follow-up: "What does that say about how you handle hard things?"
R — Reflective listening. Purpose: show understanding; steer gently by what you choose to reflect.
T: "You're worried this is slipping out of your control." → P: "Exactly." → Follow-up: deepen: "And part of you wants to grab the wheel back."
S — Summaries. Purpose: collect change talk, show listening, transition.
T: "So the money, your health, and your daughter are all pulling you toward change, while the one pull back is how it helps you relax. Did I get that right?" → P: "Yeah." → Follow-up: key question: "Where does that leave you?"

Reflection types

TypePurposeExample
SimpleRepeat/rephrase content.P: "I'm tired of it." → T: "You've had enough."
ComplexAdd meaning / feeling / guess.P: "I'm tired of it." → T: "Part of you is ready for something different."
Double-sidedHold both sides of ambivalence; end on change side."You enjoy how it relaxes you, and you're worried about your health."
Amplified (use sparingly)Reflect sustain talk slightly over-stated so patient self-corrects.P: "It's not that bad." → T: "So there's really no downside at all." → P: "Well, the debt is bad…"

Other key skills

  • Emphasising autonomy: "It's up to you — nobody can decide this for you." → reduces reactance.
  • Eliciting change talk: evocative questions — "Why might you want to change? How would you do it if you decided to? What are the best reasons?"
  • Responding to sustain talk: reflect, emphasise autonomy, reframe, shift focus — never argue.
  • Developing discrepancy: hold up the gap between current use and the patient's own goals/values.
  • Importance & confidence rulers: "0–10, how important/confident?" then "Why not lower?" to pull change talk.
  • Elicit–Provide–Elicit: ask permission & what they know → give focused info neutrally → ask what they make of it. The MET way to give feedback/advice.
  • Supporting self-efficacy: recall past successes; affirm small steps; "You've done hard things before."

5 · Change Talk & Sustain Talk — DARN-CAT

Change talk is the patient's own language favouring change. Preparatory change talk = DARN; mobilising change talk = CAT. Evoking and reflecting it predicts behaviour change.

CodeMeaningSubstance-use example
D — DesireWant, wish, like."I want to be clean for my kids."
A — AbilityCan, could, able."I could probably cut the evening dose."
R — ReasonsSpecific if/then arguments."If I stop smoking, my chest will clear up."
N — NeedMust, have to, got to."I have to do something — the debt is out of hand."
C — CommitmentWill, going to, promise."I'm going to talk to the doctor on Monday."
A — ActivationReady, willing, prepared."I'm ready to give it a real try."
T — Taking stepsAlready acting."I threw out my stash yesterday."

Recognising & responding to sustain talk — without confrontation

Patient: "Smoking is the only thing that calms my nerves."
Therapist (reflect, don't challenge): "Right now it's your main way to cope with stress."
Patient: "Yeah."
Therapist (open the door gently): "If you did decide to change someday, what other things have helped you relax, even a little?" → patient supplies alternatives (change talk).
Patient: "My family forced me here. I don't have a problem."
Therapist (autonomy + reflect): "You're here because of them, not because you've decided anything — and whether this is a problem is yours to judge. While you're here, what would make this hour worth your time?"
Patient: "I suppose getting them off my back… and I am tired of the arguments."

Principle: evoke the patient's own motivation rather than supply arguments. When you hear sustain talk, the move is almost always reflect → emphasise autonomy → ask an evocative question, never "but you should…".

7 · Confidence Ruler (0–10)

Purpose: gauge belief in ability (self-efficacy). When: throughout. Instructions: "If you decided to change, how confident are you that you could?"

012345678910

Therapist review: importance and confidence are different — high importance + low confidence needs skill/support building, not more reasons. Difficulties: if 0, explore what one tiny step could lift it.

8 · Why Am I at This Number?

Purpose: pull out the reasons behind the ruler score (change talk). When: right after rulers. Instructions: "You said [N]. What makes it that high and not 0?"

Example (importance 6): "Because my health scares me and my wife is at her limit."

Therapist review: every answer here is change talk — reflect and summarise it. Difficulties: redirect from "why so low" (which pulls sustain talk) to "why not lower".

9 · Why Not a Lower Number?

Purpose: same, deliberately phrased to evoke change talk. When: paired with #8. Instructions: "Why a 6 and not a 3?"

Example: "A 3 would mean I don't care — but I do, especially about my daughter."

Therapist review: amplify. To raise a number: "What would it take to move from a 6 to an 8?" Difficulties: never ask "why not higher?" — it elicits sustain talk.

Purpose: anchor change in what matters to the patient (develops discrepancy). When: Session 2. Instructions: list what matters most, then note how use fits (or doesn't).

★ 21 · My Short-Term Goals in Therapy

Purpose: set small, concrete, achievable goals for the next few days to weeks that build momentum and early success. When: Session 3 onward, alongside the Change Plan (#16). Instructions: write 2–4 goals you can realistically reach soon. Keep them SMART — Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.

Short-term = next 1–4 weeks. These are stepping stones toward your long-term goals. It's fine to aim for reduction or stabilisation, not only abstinence, if that's where you are.

How the therapist reviews: check goals are the patient's own, specific, and realistically scaled for an early win; link each to a trigger/coping option (#13/#15) and to a long-term goal; review attainment at the next contact and affirm effort. Use the confidence ruler (#7) on each goal — if confidence <7, shrink the goal. Common difficulties & how to respond: Goals too big/abstract ("be a better person") → help break into a concrete behaviour this week. Patient only wants abstinence immediately but confidence is low → honour the aim, add a smaller stepping-stone goal so success is likely. Patient sets a therapist-pleasing goal → "What would you actually find useful this week?" Goal not met → treat as information, adjust downward, avoid blame.

★ 22 · My Long-Term Goals in Therapy

Purpose: connect day-to-day change to the bigger life the patient wants — the "why" that sustains effort over months. When: Session 3–4 and continuing care; revisit periodically. Instructions: picture 3, 6, and 12 months ahead. What do you want your life to look like? Link each long-term goal to the value behind it and to the short-term steps that build toward it.

Long-term = 3 months to a year+. These are usually about life, not just the substance — relationships, work, health, self-respect. Long-term goals give short-term goals their meaning.

How the therapist reviews: use long-term goals to develop discrepancy (gap between current use and the desired life) and to anchor motivation when it dips; ensure each long-term goal is tied to a value (#10) and to concrete short-term goals (#21) so the path is visible; revisit and update at follow-ups. Common difficulties & how to respond: No long-term vision ("I can't think past today") → start with values (#10) and one hope for the future, however modest. Goals feel unrealistic/overwhelming → affirm the aspiration, then focus on the next short-term step. Only externally-imposed goals (court/family) → explore whether any personal benefit overlaps ("getting the court off your back and keeping your licence to work") to build intrinsic motivation. Setback shakes long-term goals → reframe as a detour, not a dead end; the destination can stay the same.

8 · Common Clinical Challenges

For each, a short dialogue showing empathy, reflective listening, autonomy support, and follow-up. The underlying move is almost always: reflect → affirm autonomy → evoke, don't argue.

"I don't have a problem."
T: "From where you stand, this isn't a problem. I'm not here to label you. What made others feel it might be?" → P: "My wife keeps on about the money." → T: "So money is one thing even you can see is tight."
"I can stop whenever I want."
T: "You feel in control of this — and it really is your choice. If you ever did test that, what might you try first?"
"My family forced me to come."
T: "You're here for them, not because you've decided anything — and that's honest. While you're here, what would make this hour worth it for you?"
"Substance use is the only thing that helps me."
T: "Right now it's your main way to cope — that's important to understand. What else has ever given you even a bit of that relief?"
"I don't want to quit."
T: "You're not looking to quit, and I won't push that. Is there anything about your use you would want to be different — even a little?"
"I tried before and failed."
T: "That stung. And you did stay clean three weeks last time — that's not nothing. What worked, even for a while?"
"I'll think about it."
T: "It's a big decision and you want to weigh it — fair enough. What might you turn over in your mind this week?"
Missed sessions.
T (on return, warm, no scolding): "Good to see you back — I'm glad you came. What's been happening?" Explore barriers (transport, cravings, ambivalence) collaboratively.
Minimal participation.
T: "You're pretty quiet today — that's okay. Would it be more useful to just sit with one thing, or would you rather we stop early?" Offer control; reduce pressure.
Anger / defensiveness.
T: "I've touched a nerve, and I'm sorry — that wasn't my aim. This is your call entirely. What would be helpful to talk about?" Reflect feeling, drop the agenda, restore autonomy.
Conflicting family expectations.
T: "Your family wants one thing and you're not sure you want the same. Both of those are real. What do you want for yourself here?" Avoid siding against the patient; consider separate family session.
A lapse after making a change plan.
T: "You slipped once and then stopped yourself — that recovery matters. Slips are common and they don't erase your progress. What can we learn from it?" → use Worksheet #19.

9 · Case Vignettes & Role-Play (fictional)

Case 1 — Opioid/smack user, ambivalent about treatment

Initial assessment: 29-y/o male, inhaled heroin ~0.5 g/day ×4 yrs, 15 cig/day; one prior detox (relapsed). Cravings 8/10. No injecting, no OD. Importance 6/10, confidence 3/10. Supportive wife; job at risk.

Motivational formulation: Contemplation; health & family are strong intrinsic pulls; low confidence is the main obstacle, not low importance.

T: "You've told me your health and your wife matter most — and that using is clashing with both." → P: "Yeah… I do want to stop, I just don't think I can." → T: "You want to — the question is whether you can. Last time you managed three weeks. What helped?" → P: "Staying away from my old group."

Change talk: "I want to stop." "My wife deserves better." Sustain talk: "I don't think I can." "Withdrawal is hell."

Worksheet selection: #12 Strengths/successes & #7 Confidence ruler (target low confidence), #13 Triggers, #16 Change plan, ★#21 short-term goals.

Session goals: raise confidence; coordinate medical review for OUD medication & naloxone; one small early-success goal.

Documentation: "Contemplation→preparation. Importance 6, confidence 3→5 after affirming past success. Referred for buprenorphine assessment. Plan: short-term goal to attend appointment."

Next step: Session 3 change plan once medication started.

Case 2 — Alcohol user, recognises consequences, low confidence

Initial assessment: 42-y/o male, ~750 ml spirits/day ×8 yrs; raised LFTs; marital strain; two failed quit attempts; one withdrawal with tremor (no seizure). Importance 8/10, confidence 2/10.

Formulation: High importance, very low confidence; needs efficacy-building and medically supervised withdrawal.

T: "You're very clear this matters — an 8. What makes you doubt you can do it?" → P: "I always go back." → T (reframe + affirm): "You've started several times — that's persistence, not failure. This time we add medical support for the withdrawal so it's safer." → P: "If the withdrawal was handled, I'd try again."

Worksheet: #12 Strengths, #7 Confidence, #15 Coping/support, #20 Continuing care, ★#22 long-term goals (rebuild trust at home).

Goals: arrange supervised detox; build confidence; connect to support group.

Documentation: "Preparation. Importance 8, confidence 2→4. Referred for inpatient-assisted withdrawal given tremor history. Long-term goal: family trust."

Next step: coordinate detox, then relapse-prevention planning.

Case 3 — Attending under family pressure, no personal reason to change

Initial assessment: 23-y/o, daily cannabis + occasional alcohol; brought by parents; "I'm only here because of them." Importance 2/10, confidence 7/10.

Formulation: Precontemplation; extrinsic pressure only. Task: engage, build rapport, evoke any personal concern — no planning yet.

T: "You're here for your parents, not because you've decided anything — and I respect that it's your choice. What would make this worth your while today?" → P: "Just getting them to back off." → T: "The constant pressure is exhausting. If the fighting eased, what difference would that make for you?" → P: "I'd actually have some peace… and maybe focus on my studies." (first personal reason)

Change talk: "I'd have peace." "Focus on studies." Sustain talk: "I don't have a problem." "Everyone my age smokes."

Worksheet: #10 Values/goals, #2 Reasons, rulers #6/#7 — no change plan yet.

Goals: engagement; one personal value surfaced; leave the door open.

Documentation: "Precontemplation. Attends under family pressure. Began to voice a personal interest (studies/peace). No plan imposed. Plan: continue engagement."

Next step: re-invite; explore studies/autonomy as intrinsic motivators.

Therapist

  • Elicit change talk; never argue with sustain talk.

  • Emphasise autonomy at least once.

  • Play the assigned stage realistically; include genuine ambivalence.

Observer

  • Tally OARS use and change-talk vs sustain-talk moments.

  • Give one specific affirmation and one suggestion in feedback.

12 · Evidence, Limitations & Ethical Practice

Evidence (summary)

MET and MI have a substantial evidence base in substance-use treatment. In Project MATCH, 4-session MET produced drinking outcomes broadly comparable to longer CBT and Twelve-Step Facilitation — notable given fewer sessions. Reviews and the Cochrane literature on MI/MET report benefits for engagement, retention, and reduced use, though effect sizes vary by substance, population, setting, and comparison condition, and some effects are modest or short-lived without follow-up. (Prioritise the original Project MATCH reports, Miller & Rollnick, and Cochrane reviews as verifiable sources; figures above are summarised, not exact statistics.)

What MET can do: improve engagement/retention, strengthen motivation and commitment, reduce use for many, and serve as a low-intensity entry point. What it cannot do: replace detox, pharmacotherapy, or management of severe dependence; guarantee abstinence; or "cure" addiction through motivation alone.

Limitations

  • Outcomes vary across substances (e.g., evidence stronger for alcohol/cannabis engagement than for some stimulant outcomes) and populations.
  • Effects depend heavily on treatment context and follow-up/continuing care.
  • Motivation is necessary but not sufficient — pair with medical treatment and skills where indicated.

Ethical practice

  • Informed consent & confidentiality — explain purpose, voluntariness, and limits (risk to self/others, legal mandates).
  • Respect for autonomy — the patient chooses goals; avoid coercion and confrontation.
  • Non-stigmatising language — "a person who uses heroin," not "an addict/junkie"; describe behaviour, not identity.
  • Cultural sensitivity — adapt to family structure, religion, gender, and local norms (important in Indian de-addiction settings).
  • Appropriate referral & multidisciplinary care — coordinate with physicians, psychiatry, social work, and community/mutual-help resources.
  • Do not overstate efficacy or claim motivation alone resolves dependence.

★ One-Page Quick-Reference Guide

MET session workflow

  1. Assess (structured, MI style)
  2. S1: Engage + personalised feedback (E-P-E) + evoke reasons
  3. S2: Explore ambivalence, develop discrepancy, build change talk
  4. S3: Negotiate change plan + triggers/coping + short-term goals
  5. S4: Consolidate, relapse-prevention, continuing care

OARS

Open questions · Affirmations · Reflective listening · Summaries

Golden rules

  • Evoke, don't install. Reflect, don't argue.
  • Emphasise autonomy & choice.
  • If the patient defends use, change your approach.
  • Ask permission before advice (Elicit–Provide–Elicit).

Change talk — DARN-CAT

Preparatory: Desire · Ability · Reasons · Need
Mobilising: Commitment · Activation · Taking steps

Rulers (0–10)

Importance: "How important is change?" Confidence: "If you decided, could you?"
Then: "Why that number?" and "Why not lower?" (both pull change talk). Never ask "why not higher?"

Change planning

Goal (patient's words) → reasons → steps → supports → how you'll know it's working → obstacles & responses → short-term goals that build toward long-term goals.

⚠ Always: screen OD/withdrawal/suicide risk; coordinate medical care & (for opioids) naloxone + MOUD.
