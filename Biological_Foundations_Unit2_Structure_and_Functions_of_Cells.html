Cells of the Nervous System — Neurons

Neurons are the signalling cells that receive, process and pass on information across the whole nervous system.

  1. Neurons are the elementary information-processing cells of the nervous system, electrically excitable able to produce tiny electrical signals cells that receive and rapidly transmit chemical or electrical messages across the body.
  2. A typical neuron has three core parts, the cell body (soma), the branching dendrites and a single long axon, and this layout lets one cell gather many inputs yet send one focused output signal.
  3. The soma the main body of the cell that holds the nucleus contains the nucleus, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, lysosomes and mitochondria, so most of the cell's housekeeping and manufacturing work happens here.
  4. The nucleus stores genetic material inside chromosomes and transcribes DNA into RNA copying the gene instructions into a message, which then leaves through nuclear pores to guide protein synthesis in the cytoplasm.
  5. Dendrites are fine branching filaments that carry information toward the cell body, and their many dendritic spines tiny bumps on the branches greatly increase the surface area available for synaptic connections.
  6. The axon behaves like the wire of an electrical cable, starting at the axon hillock the cone-shaped base where the axon leaves the soma and carrying the signal away toward another neuron, a muscle or a gland.
  7. A fatty myelin sheath an insulating fatty wrapping around the axon encloses many axons in segments, and this insulation sharply increases the speed at which nerve impulses travel along the fibre.
  8. Gaps in the myelin called nodes of Ranvier bare spaces between the insulating segments let the signal be recharged as it jumps along the axon, keeping the impulse strong over long distances.
  9. Inside the soma, Nissl granules clusters of rough endoplasmic reticulum and free ribosomes build proteins, while mitochondria act as the powerhouse supplying the energy the busy neuron constantly needs.
  10. The Golgi apparatus the cell's packaging and dispatch centre processes peptides, proteins and neurotransmitters into vesicles, and microtubules then transport these packages to where the neuron needs them.
  11. By function, neurons are grouped as sensory (afferent) neurons that carry input to the CNS, motor (efferent) neurons that drive muscles and glands, and interneurons that connect the two inside the central nervous system.
  12. Sensory (afferent) neurons convert a stimulus from the outside world into an internal electrical signal and then carry that signal inward toward the spinal cord or brain for processing.
  13. Motor (efferent) neurons sit in the CNS and control muscles through the neuromuscular junction the contact point where a nerve meets a muscle, and they are multipolar with one axon and many dendrites.
  14. Interneurons act as a local broker between sensory and motor neurons, handling signals nearby so that quick reflex-like responses do not always have to travel all the way back to the spinal cord.
  15. By shape and number of processes, neurons are classed as unipolar, bipolar or multipolar one, two, or many extensions leaving the cell body, a structural scheme that reflects how each cell is wired into its circuit.
  16. Neurons can also be named by the neurotransmitter they release, such as cholinergic or glutamatergic cells, which links a cell's chemical identity directly to the kind of message it delivers to its partners.
  17. Neurons are enclosed by a plasma membrane the thin outer skin of the cell and, because they rarely divide once mature, damage to them tends to be long-lasting, which is why neuronal health matters so much for behaviour.

Supporting Cells (Glia)

Glial cells are the non-signalling support cells that feed, insulate, defend and hold the nervous system together.

  1. Glial (neuroglial) cells are the support cells that hold the nervous system together, providing physical integrity, nutrition and a stable homeostasis a steady internal environment around the neurons.
  2. Glia outnumber neurons by roughly a three-to-one ratio, which underlines that healthy brain function depends not only on neurons but heavily on the supporting cells that surround and service them.
  3. Beyond simple support, glia help regulate the chemical environment around neurons and take part in cortical development, axon and dendrite growth, making them active partners in building the nervous system.
  4. Astrocytes are star-shaped cells of the brain and spinal cord that keep the chemical environment stable by clearing surplus ions such as potassium and chloride from around the nerve cells.
  5. Astrocytes also recycle neurotransmitters reclaim and reuse chemical messengers that were released during synaptic transmission, so signalling chemicals are not wasted and the synapse is reset for the next message.
  6. Astrocyte end-feet swollen tips of the astrocyte wrap around and support the endothelial cells of the blood-brain barrier, helping shield the brain from harmful blood-borne substances while normal metabolism continues.
  7. The brain holds two kinds of astrocyte, protoplasmic astrocytes in grey matter and fibrous astrocytes in white matter, and together they are the most numerous glial cells of the central nervous system.
  8. Oligodendrocytes in the CNS form the myelin sheath fatty insulation around axons, and because one oligodendrocyte can coat up to about fifty axons they are highly efficient insulating cells.
  9. Myelin from oligodendrocytes lets the action potential jump between nodes of Ranvier gaps in the insulation, so myelinated axons conduct far faster than unmyelinated ones and myelin loss impairs reasoning and performance.
  10. Microglia are the resident immune cells of the CNS and act as specialised macrophages cells that engulf and digest debris, guarding and supporting neurons throughout the brain and spinal cord.
  11. Microglia carry out phagocytosis swallowing and clearing waste, make up roughly fifteen to seventeen percent of CNS cells, and multiply in response to brain injury to contain the damage.
  12. Microglia drive the inflammatory response to brain trauma and are implicated in neurodegenerative disorders such as multiple sclerosis, motor neurone disease and Alzheimer's disease, and are key targets of HIV-1 in the CNS.
  13. Radial glial cells are a temporary population in the developing CNS that act both as precursor cells and as a scaffold a guiding framework along which young neurons migrate to their correct positions.
  14. Schwann cells are the PNS counterpart of oligodendrocytes, and each Schwann cell insulates one single stretch of a peripheral axon with myelin to speed conduction outside the brain and spinal cord.
  15. Non-myelinating Schwann cells are important for the survival and maintenance of peripheral axons, showing that even glia which do not insulate still keep neurons alive and functioning healthily.
  16. A newly recognised glial type, the oligodendrocyte precursor cell, takes part in synaptic signalling in the hippocampus, revising the older idea that glia only ever served passive supporting roles.

The Blood–Brain Barrier

The blood-brain barrier is the selective gate that protects the brain while still letting essential nutrients pass.

  1. The blood-brain barrier is a protective mechanism that keeps a stable environment for the brain by shielding it from harmful substances carried in the circulating blood.
  2. It works by differential permeability letting some things through but not others, so blood-borne toxins and many drugs are kept out while the brain's normal metabolism continues undisturbed.
  3. The barrier is built mainly from the tightly joined endothelial cells the cells lining the blood vessels of brain capillaries, whose close seals stop substances from leaking freely between the blood and brain tissue.
  4. Astrocyte end-feet wrap around these capillary walls and help form and support the barrier, which is why the barrier is described as a partnership between blood vessels and surrounding glial cells.
  5. The barrier allows passive diffusion free drifting across the membrane of small gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide, so the brain can breathe at the cellular level without any special transport system.
  6. Essential molecules that cannot simply diffuse, such as glucose and certain amino acids, are moved across by selective transport special carrier proteins that ferry them through to feed the demanding brain tissue.
  7. The brain is metabolically expensive, using about twenty-five percent of the body's glucose and roughly seventy-five percent of its oxygen, so a steady, well-guarded blood supply is vital to normal brain function.
  8. A serious fall in blood supply to the brain can cause a stroke brain damage from loss of blood flow, showing how dependent the brain is on continuous, properly regulated circulation.
  9. Lipid-soluble fat-dissolving substances can slip through the plasma membranes of the barrier more easily than water-soluble ones, which is why some drugs reach the brain readily while others are largely blocked.
  10. Stress increases the permeability of the barrier, so under strain even some harmful chemicals that are normally excluded may cross into the brain, a point of real clinical importance.
  11. The barrier's selective nature explains why treating brain disease is difficult, because many useful drugs are rejected at the barrier and never reach the neurons that need them.
  12. A clear clinical illustration is L-DOPA (levodopa), the precursor of dopamine, which is given in Parkinson's disease precisely because it can cross the barrier whereas dopamine itself cannot.
  13. The barrier is described as a semipermeable only partly letting things through structure formed with extracellular fluid that separates the circulating blood from the brain and the extracellular fluid of the central nervous system.
  14. By keeping ions and messenger molecules at controlled levels, the barrier protects the delicate electrical environment neurons need, so signalling across the brain stays reliable and is not disturbed by swings in blood chemistry.
  15. In short, the blood-brain barrier is a finely balanced gatekeeper that blocks threats, admits fuel and protects the brain's internal setting, making it central to both brain health and drug design.
  16. Because the barrier guards brain chemistry so strictly, damage or leakage of the barrier is linked to swelling, infection and several neurological conditions, underlining its role as a frontline protector of the central nervous system.
Sourced definition — Blood Brain Barrier (book's Key Terms and Definitions)
“Blood brain barrier is a protective element with semipermeable architecture formed by extracellular fluid that separates the circulating blood from the brain and extracellular fluid in the central nervous system. Blood brain barrier system allows passage of small molecules including gases such as oxygen and carbon dioxide by passive diffusion; as well also promote the selective transportation of essential nutrients or molecules such as, water, glucose and amino acids, which cannot diffuse due to absence of required polarity.”
Source: Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior (ed. Barre Vijaya Prasad, 2020), Key Terms and Definitions.

Communication Within a Neuron — Membrane (Resting) Potential

At rest a neuron holds a steady negative voltage inside, ready to fire the moment it is stimulated.

  1. The resting membrane potential is the steady voltage across a neuron's membrane while the cell is at rest and not sending a signal, and this chapter gives its value as about −70 mV inside relative to outside.
  2. This resting state is called polarised charged, with a difference between inside and outside because the inside of the neuron is negative compared with the fluid surrounding the cell.
  3. The negativity arises from an uneven spread of ions charged particles such as sodium, potassium and chloride across the membrane, so charge is not balanced between the inside and the outside.
  4. The neuron's cytoplasm is a watery, salty, potassium-rich solution, while sodium ions are more concentrated outside, and this mirror-image arrangement sets up the resting voltage the cell holds.
  5. The plasma membrane the cell's outer skin is selectively permeable, meaning it lets some ions cross more easily than others, which is essential for building and keeping the resting potential.
  6. At rest the membrane is far more permeable to potassium (K+) than to sodium, so potassium tends to leak outward down its gradient and leaves the inside of the cell more negative.
  7. A separate sodium-potassium pump a protein that actively swaps the two ions using energy pushes sodium out and potassium in, maintaining the concentration differences that underlie the resting potential. (Standard physiology texts.)
  8. This pumping requires energy from ATP supplied by mitochondria, which is why the resting potential is an active, energy-dependent state rather than something the cell gets for free.
  9. The resting potential represents a form of stored electrical energy, like a charged battery, that the neuron can release quickly when it is stimulated strongly enough to fire.
  10. A stimulus that makes the inside less negative moves the voltage toward zero, a change called depolarisation, whereas a stimulus that makes it more negative is called hyperpolarisation becoming even more negative than rest.
  11. The special voltage that must be reached to trigger a signal is the threshold, given in this chapter as about −55 mV, the tipping point beyond which the neuron is committed to firing.
  12. Below threshold the neuron stays quiet, so the resting potential also acts as a filter that prevents weak or random inputs from accidentally setting off a full nerve impulse.
  13. The resting potential is not fixed forever but is actively maintained, constantly rebalanced by ion channels and pumps so the cell is always poised and ready to respond to the next stimulus.
  14. Because the voltage sits between the negative resting level and the threshold, the neuron behaves like a loaded trigger, holding its charge until an input is strong enough to push it past the tipping point.
  15. Understanding the resting potential matters clinically because drugs, toxins and ion imbalances that alter membrane charge can change how easily neurons fire and so directly affect mood, movement and behaviour.
  16. Overall, the resting potential is the stable baseline, set by ion gradients and the sodium-potassium pump, from which every action potential begins and to which the neuron always returns after firing.

Communication Within a Neuron — The Action Potential

The action potential is the brief, all-or-none electrical spike that carries the nerve impulse along the axon.

  1. When the nervous system transmits information, a resting neuron produces an action potential, a short-lived event in which the membrane voltage rapidly rises and then falls, also called the nerve impulse the travelling electrical signal.
  2. An action potential only fires once the voltage reaches the threshold of about −55 mV, the tipping point; once this value is reached, the impulse is generated in a committed, all-or-none way.
  3. Depolarisation the inside becoming less negative begins when a stimulus, for example at a touch receptor, opens sodium (Na+) channels and positive ions rush into the negatively charged neuron.
  4. As sodium floods in, the inside of the neuron becomes positive and the membrane potential climbs steeply, which is the sharp upstroke seen on a recording of the action potential.
  5. Next, potassium (K+) channels open and potassium ions move out of the cell, a flow that reverses the rising charge and begins to bring the membrane voltage back down.
  6. The brief peak created as sodium enters and then potassium leaves is called the spike potential the tall, narrow burst of voltage, the characteristic shape of a single nerve impulse.
  7. Repolarisation returning toward the negative resting value happens as sodium channels close back to their resting state, driving the membrane potential back toward about −70 mV.
  8. Because the potassium channels stay open slightly too long, the voltage briefly overshoots past −70 mV to nearly −80 mV, a dip called hyperpolarisation more negative than the normal resting level.
  9. Ion concentrations are then restored and the cell settles back to its −70 mV resting membrane potential, ready to respond again once a new stimulus arrives.
  10. Immediately after firing there is an absolute refractory period a short time when no new impulse is possible, during which the neuron cannot generate another action potential no matter how strong the stimulus.
  11. This is followed by a relative refractory period during which a new action potential can occur, but only if the stimulus is stronger than usual to overcome the lingering hyperpolarisation.
  12. In myelinated axons the impulse jumps between nodes of Ranvier bare gaps in the insulation, a leaping conduction that makes signalling much faster than in unmyelinated fibres.
  13. The action potential is essentially self-regenerating, being recharged at each node as it travels, so the signal keeps its full strength over long distances along the axon.
  14. Because of the all-or-none rule, information is coded not by the size of each spike but by the frequency of impulses, so a stronger stimulus produces more action potentials per second rather than bigger ones.
  15. The refractory periods also ensure the impulse travels in one direction only, from the cell body toward the axon terminal, preventing the signal from running backward along the fibre.
  16. Clinically, anything that disturbs sodium or potassium channels, such as local anaesthetics or certain toxins, can block the action potential, which is how nerve conduction and sensation can be switched off for medical purposes.

Communication Between Neurons — Neurotransmitters

At the synapse, chemical messengers carry the signal from one neuron across the gap to the next.

  1. A synapse is the junction where one neuron communicates with the next, and because it is a thin gap neurons do not physically merge but instead signal across it chemically.
  2. The synapse has three parts, the presynaptic ending the sending side that releases the message, the postsynaptic ending that receives it, and the narrow synaptic cleft the gap between them, about 20 nm wide in between.
  3. Neurotransmitters are the chemical messengers synthesised by neurons and released across the synapse, and they are stored in tiny sacs called vesicles membrane bubbles holding the chemical in the terminal.
  4. When the impulse arrives, calcium (Ca2+) channels open and calcium entering the terminal drives the vesicles to fuse with the membrane in a process called exocytosis releasing contents by the sac merging with the cell wall.
  5. The released neurotransmitter crosses the cleft by diffusion drifting across the gap and binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, opening ion channels and changing that cell's local voltage.
  6. If the binding causes depolarisation the result is an excitatory response, whereas if it causes hyperpolarisation making the cell more negative the result is an inhibitory response that quietens the next neuron.
  7. Ionotropic (channel-linked) receptors give fast, direct responses because the neurotransmitter itself opens an ion channel, while metabotropic (G-protein-linked) receptors act indirectly through second messengers internal relay chemicals for slower, longer effects.
  8. GABA is the major inhibitory neurotransmitter of the CNS, found at thirty to forty percent of all synapses, and its deficiency is linked to seizures and anxiety disorders.
  9. Glycine is a simple amino-acid inhibitory neurotransmitter of the spinal cord, brain stem and retina, and the poison strychnine causes convulsions by blocking glycine's calming action.
  10. Glutamate is the main excitatory neurotransmitter and is central to learning and memory, while aspartate is another excitatory transmitter that activates NMDA receptors a specific glutamate-type receptor.
  11. Serotonin (5-HT) helps regulate mood, anger, aggression, sleep, appetite and body temperature, which is why SSRIs selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, a class of antidepressant are used to treat depression.
  12. Dopamine works along four brain pathways, and its shortage causes Parkinson's disease while its excess is linked to schizophrenia, making it one of the most clinically important transmitters.
  13. Noradrenaline is an excitatory monoamine from the locus coeruleus that plays a key role in attention and arousal, as well as heart rate, blood pressure and digestive processes.
  14. Acetylcholine (ACh), the first neurotransmitter ever identified, acts in both the PNS and CNS, and its deficiency is one of the causes of Alzheimer's disease with its memory loss.
  15. The synapse is the main site of action of most psychoactive drugs, for example cocaine blocks dopamine reuptake reabsorption of the transmitter so its effect is intensified in the synaptic cleft.
  16. Signalling is kept precise by stopping it promptly, as transmitters are cleared by reuptake back into the neuron or by enzymes, and autoreceptors self-sensing receptors give feedback that controls further release.

Neuromodulators and Hormones

Beyond fast synapses, neuromodulators and hormones tune behaviour more slowly and over wider reaches of the body.

  1. Neuromodulators are a slower mode of chemical communication, released by neurons but travelling larger distances and spreading more widely through the nervous system than ordinary point-to-point transmitters.
  2. Many neuromodulators are neuropeptides short chains of amino acids linked by peptide bonds, protein-like molecules that act alongside classical neurotransmitters rather than replacing them.
  3. Rather than simply exciting or inhibiting one cell, neuromodulators adjust the general state of many neurons, shaping broad conditions such as alertness, attentiveness and anxiety.
  4. Neuropeptides typically work by raising or lowering the release of other neurotransmitters or by altering how strongly postsynaptic cells respond, so they fine-tune signalling rather than carry it alone.
  5. Hormones are a third mode of communication in the body, chemical substances secreted by the endocrine glands ductless glands that release into the blood or by cells in organs such as the stomach and kidneys.
  6. Hormones fall into three chemical classes, amino-acid derivatives, peptides and proteins, and steroids fat-based hormones made from cholesterol, and this chemistry shapes how each hormone acts on its targets.
  7. Hormones are released into the extracellular fluid and carried in the bloodstream to distant target cells, so unlike synaptic signals they can influence the whole body at once.
  8. Because many neurons carry hormone receptors, hormones can directly change behaviour; for example testosterone, a sex hormone, increases aggressiveness in male animals.
  9. The adrenal glands release hormones for the stress response the body's reaction to threat, preparing the body to react quickly during demanding or frightening situations.
  10. The hypothalamus controls sleep, hunger and appetite, acting as a key link between the nervous system and the endocrine system that keeps basic drives regulated.
  11. The ovaries and testicles (gonads) control female and male characteristics by secreting the sex hormones that govern reproduction and the development of secondary sexual traits.
  12. The pancreas releases insulin the hormone that lowers blood sugar and so controls the level of glucose in the body, a balance that is essential for steady energy supply to the brain.
  13. The parathyroid glands retain and regulate the body's calcium levels, while the pineal body secretes melatonin to maintain sleep cycles and other daily biological rhythms.
  14. The pituitary gland is often called the master gland because it helps maintain the other endocrine glands, while the thymus supports the body's immune system.
  15. The thyroid gland maintains the body's metabolism the rate at which the body uses energy, including bone growth and heat production, so it influences overall energy and activity levels.
  16. Melatonin, derived from serotonin in the pineal gland, governs the sleep-wake cycle, being made in darkness and kept low in daylight, which shows how a single molecule can act as both transmitter-related signal and hormone.
