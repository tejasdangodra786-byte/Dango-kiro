The one big idea you must state in the exam

ICD-10 grouped everything with a physical brain/body cause under the word "organic" (meaning: caused by a diagnosable brain or body disease — not imaginary), in the block F00–F09, "Organic, including symptomatic, mental disorders". ICD-10

ICD-11 drops the word "organic" (because every mental disorder has a biological basis) and splits the block into two clearer groups ICD-11:

1. Neurocognitive disorders (thinking/memory is the MAIN problem)
delirium, mild neurocognitive disorder, amnestic disorder, the dementias → codes 6D70–6D86.
2. Secondary mental/behavioural syndromes ("secondary" = caused by another medical illness)
mood, psychosis, anxiety, catatonia, personality change due to physical illness → codes 6E60–6E69.
🧭 One-line answer: "ICD-11 replaced the ICD-10 'organic' block with two clearer groups — Neurocognitive disorders (cognition-led) and Secondary mental syndromes (symptom-led, caused by a medical illness) — kept the same diseases, added severity codes and behaviour codes, and still needs two codes (syndrome + cause)."

Dementia — the general concept (applies to F00–F03 / 6D80–6D85)

Before the specific dementias, both classifications give one general rule for what "dementia" means.

ICD-10 General definition of dementia

A syndrome due to disease of the brain, usually chronic (long-lasting) or progressive (slowly worsening), with disturbance of many higher brain functions — memory, thinking, orientation (knowing time/place/person), comprehension (understanding), calculation, learning, language and judgement. Consciousness is NOT clouded (alertness is normal — this separates dementia from delirium). Thinking decline is often joined by decline in emotional control, social behaviour or motivation, and it reduces daily activities (washing, dressing, eating, toilet).

ICD-11 General definition of dementia

An acquired (develops later, not present from birth) chronic condition with decline from a previous level in 2 or more cognitive domains (thinking areas: memory, attention, executive function/planning, language, social cognition, psychomotor speed, seeing-and-space skills), severe enough to reduce independence in daily activities. If memory/thinking problems happen only during a depressive episode, dementia is usually NOT diagnosed.

Exam trap: depression in older people can look like dementia — pseudodementia (depression pretending to be dementia; it improves with antidepressants). Always screen mood.
🧭 Dementia signature: acquired + lasting + many-area thinking decline + clear alertness + loss of daily independence.

F00 · Dementia in Alzheimer's disease → ICD-11 6D80

ICD-10 F00 (.0 early, .1 late, .2 atypical/mixed, .9 unspecified)
ICD-11 6D80 (.0 early, .1 late, .2 mixed w/ stroke, .3 mixed w/ other, .Z)
Status Kept (renamed slightly)
Headline change Severity & behaviour coded separately; a "mixed with stroke" type added.

1Introduction

The commonest cause of dementia. A slow brain-wasting disease that erases memory first, then spreads to language, orientation and reasoning. Insidious onset (begins so gradually nobody can name the day it started) and slowly worsens over years; it is irreversible (cannot be cured or reversed). ICD-10

2Etiology (causes)

The exact cause is unknown, but the brain shows: amyloid plaques (sticky protein clumps between brain cells) and neurofibrillary tangles (twisted "tau" protein threads inside cells), with death of brain cells in the hippocampus (the brain's memory-saving centre) and temporal/parietal cortex, and a fall in acetylcholine (a memory chemical messenger). ICD-10 K&S

  • Genetic: early-onset can run in families (APP, PSEN1, PSEN2 genes); APOE ε4 (a risk gene) raises late-onset risk.
  • Down syndrome: almost all develop Alzheimer changes early (extra chromosome 21 carries the amyloid gene).
  • Age is the biggest risk; vascular risk factors and low "cognitive reserve" add to it.

3Epidemiology

  • Accounts for about 50–60% of all dementias — the most common type. K&S
  • Prevalence roughly doubles about every 5 years after age 65.
  • Late-onset (after 65) is far more common than early-onset (before 65).
  • Slightly more common in women (partly because they live longer).

4Symptoms — easy to spot + ⭐ unique

Easy to spot

  • Memory loss first — forgets recent talks, repeats questions, misplaces things.
  • Word-finding trouble → later aphasia (impaired language).
  • Gets lost; apraxia (can't do learned actions like dressing).
  • Poor judgement/planning; apathy or low mood.
  • Alertness stays normal until very late.

⭐ Unique clues — "see this → think Alzheimer"

  • ★ Memory-FIRST + smooth slow slide over years.
  • ★ Anosognosia (patient does not realise/denies the problem) — family worried, patient not.
  • ★ Loses recent memory but keeps old memories longer.
  • ★ MRI: hippocampal/temporal-lobe shrinkage.
  • ★ Down syndrome patient developing dementia early.

5Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 & ICD-11)

ICD-10 diagnostic guidelines

  1. Dementia present (meets the general dementia rule).
  2. Insidious onset with slow deterioration (no sudden or stepwise start).
  3. No other cause — nothing on history/exam/tests to suggest another brain or body disease (thyroid, B12 lack, hydrocephalus, stroke) or a substance.
  4. No sudden onset and no early focal signs (one-sided weakness, sensory loss, visual-field loss) — these point to vascular/other causes.

Sub-types: F00.0 early-onset (<65, faster, early aphasia/apraxia); F00.1 late-onset (>65, slower, memory main); F00.2 atypical/mixed (incl. mixed with vascular); F00.9 unspecified.

ICD-11 essential (required) features

  • Meets the requirements for dementia (≥2 cognitive domains, reduced independence).
  • Insidious onset and gradual worsening; usually early, prominent memory impairment (though non-memory-first types are recognised).
  • No other disease/substance better explains the dementia.
  • Specify onset (early/late) and, if present, a mixed type (with cerebrovascular disease = 6D80.2, or other = 6D80.3). Add severity (mild/moderate/severe) and BPSD codes if needed.

6Easy way to remember

💡 "A-B-C of Alzheimer"

Amyloid plaques + Anterograde memory loss + Alert · Brain shrinks (hippocampus) · Choline (acetylcholine) low → so give Cholinesterase inhibitors. Signature: slow + memory-first + alert + no early focal signs.

7Treatment

Pharmacological (medicines) K&S

AimDrug (class)How it works + caution
Mild–moderate cognitionCholinesterase inhibitors — donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamineRaise acetylcholine (the memory chemical); modest help; side-effects nausea, slow heart.
Moderate–severe cognitionMemantine (NMDA antagonist)Calms over-active glutamate signalling; usually well tolerated.
Behaviour/psychosis (BPSD)Treat cause first; antidepressant for depression; antipsychotic only if serious riskAntipsychotics raise stroke/death risk in dementia — lowest dose, shortest time.

Psychological / non-drug

  • Cognitive stimulation therapy (group activities to exercise thinking); reality orientation; structured daily routine.
  • Environmental changes for safety and orientation (labels, clocks, calendars).
  • Behavioural approach to agitation (find triggers); music/reminiscence therapy.
  • Caregiver support & education, safety planning, advance care planning.

Prognosis: progressive and irreversible; medicines slow symptoms, not the disease. ICD-10

F01 · Vascular dementia → ICD-11 6D81

ICD-10 F01 (.0 acute, .1 multi-infarct, .2 subcortical, .3 mixed, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6D81 Dementia due to cerebrovascular disease
Status Renamed / reorganised
Headline change Renamed; old sub-types not kept as separate codes.

1Introduction

Dementia caused by cerebrovascular disease (disease of the brain's blood vessels — strokes or long-term small-vessel damage). Where blood supply is cut off, brain tissue dies, so thinking drops. The second most common dementia after Alzheimer, and the two often occur together (mixed dementia). ICD-10 K&S

2Etiology

  • Large-vessel strokes, many small infarcts (areas of dead tissue from blocked blood flow), or diffuse small-vessel white-matter disease.
  • Risk factors are the classic vascular ones: high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, atrial fibrillation (irregular heartbeat that throws clots), smoking.

3Epidemiology

  • Second most common dementia; commonly co-exists with Alzheimer pathology. K&S
  • More common where vascular risk factors are common; rises with age.
  • A key opportunity: controlling risk factors can slow progression.

4Symptoms — easy to spot + ⭐ unique

Easy to spot

  • Slowed thinking; poor planning/attention (often before memory).
  • Focal signs (one-sided weakness, brisk reflexes, upgoing toe).
  • Walking/gait problems early.
  • Mood changes and depression.

⭐ Unique clues — "see this → think Vascular"

  • ★ Stepwise decline (drops suddenly, stays flat, drops again — like stairs) or starts right after a stroke.
  • ★ Focal signs early + gait problems early.
  • ★ Planning & speed hit before memory (opposite of Alzheimer).
  • ★ Emotional lability / "emotional incontinence" (sudden crying/laughing they can't control).
  • ★ Strong vascular risk factors; MRI shows infarcts/white-matter disease.

5Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 & ICD-11)

ICD-10 diagnostic guidelines

  1. Dementia present.
  2. Uneven ("patchy") deficits — some functions hit hard, others spared (unlike the all-over decline of Alzheimer).
  3. Evidence of focal brain damage: at least one of — one-sided limb weakness; brisk tendon reflexes; extensor plantar response (toe goes up when sole scratched); pseudobulbar palsy (poor face/throat muscle control).
  4. Evidence of cerebrovascular disease from history/exam/tests judged to be causing it.

Sub-types: F01.0 acute onset (after a stroke); F01.1 multi-infarct (mainly cortical, stepwise); F01.2 subcortical (small-vessel, can mimic Alzheimer); F01.3 mixed.

ICD-11 essential features

  • Meets dementia requirements.
  • Cognitive deficits judged to be a direct result of cerebrovascular disease — supported by a time link to stroke(s) and/or imaging showing significant vascular damage.
  • Course may be sudden/stepwise (after strokes) or gradual (small-vessel disease). Add severity + BPSD codes as needed.

6Easy way to remember

💡 "STEPS"

Stepwise decline · Time-linked to strokes · Executive/speed hit first · Patchy deficits + focal signs · Stroke risk factors. Signature: stepwise/post-stroke + focal signs + vascular risks.

7Treatment

Pharmacological K&S

  • Control vascular risk factors (the key): blood-pressure drugs, statins, glucose control, antiplatelets/anticoagulants (blood-thinning drugs to prevent more strokes).
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors/memantine have a limited role (more useful in mixed dementia); treat depression.

Psychological / non-drug

  • Cognitive rehabilitation (retraining/compensating for lost skills); physiotherapy for gait; mood support; caregiver education.

Prognosis: variable — secondary prevention can stabilise it; progresses if vascular disease continues.

F02 · Dementia in other diseases → ICD-11 6D82 (Lewy), 6D83 (FTD), 6D85 (others)

ICD-10 F02 (.0 Pick, .1 CJD, .2 Huntington, .3 Parkinson, .4 HIV, .8 other)
ICD-11 6D82 Lewy body · 6D83 Frontotemporal · 6D85 other diseases (.0–.Y)
Status Split / reorganised
Headline change Lewy body got its own code; Pick's widened to frontotemporal; the rest are 6D85 sub-codes.

1Introduction

Dementias that are part/result of a specific named disease diagnosed elsewhere. You always give two codes — the dementia + the causing disease. ICD-10

2–4Each disease: cause · who · symptoms + ⭐ unique clue

⭐ Dementia with Lewy bodies (6D82) — the "3 core features" disease

Cause: Lewy bodies (clumps of "alpha-synuclein" protein inside brain cells). Who: older adults; third-commonest degenerative dementia. Unique clues:

  • ★ Fluctuating thinking/alertness (a good hour then a very confused hour).
  • ★ Detailed visual hallucinations (clearly seeing people/animals that aren't there).
  • ★ Parkinsonism (slowness, stiffness, tremor) starting around the same time as the dementia.
  • ★ REM-sleep behaviour disorder (acting out dreams — kicking/shouting in sleep).
  • ★★ DANGER — neuroleptic (antipsychotic) sensitivity (antipsychotics cause severe, even fatal, reactions — AVOID). K&S

⭐ Frontotemporal dementia (6D83) — "personality first, memory later"

Cause: shrinkage of the frontal + temporal lobes (tau or TDP-43 protein). Who: younger onset (50s–60s). Unique clues:

  • ★ Behaviour/personality change FIRST — disinhibition (rude/tactless/impulsive acts), apathy (no motivation), loss of empathy.
  • ★ Memory relatively okay early (opposite of Alzheimer).
  • ★ Language forms: progressive non-fluent aphasia (slow, effortful speech) or semantic dementia (loses word meanings). (ICD-11 needs a 2nd code 8A23.)

⭐ Other 6D85 dementias — one give-away clue each

Disease (code)Unique "give-away" clue + simple meaning
Prion / CJD (6D85.5)VERY rapid dementia (weeks–months) + myoclonus (sudden muscle jerks); fatal fast. (prion = infectious mis-folded protein)
Huntington (6D85.1)Chorea (quick dance-like involuntary movements) + parent had it (inherited) + early psychiatric change.
Parkinson (6D85.0)Known Parkinson's (tremor, stiffness, slowness) for years first, then dementia.
HIV (6D85.3)Subcortical pattern (slowed thinking & movement, apathy); improves with anti-HIV drugs.
Normal-pressure hydrocephalus (6D85.6)"Wet, wacky, wobbly" = incontinence + dementia + odd "magnetic" gait; REVERSIBLE with a shunt (a tube to drain excess brain fluid).
Pellagra (6D85.8)3 Ds = Dementia + Diarrhoea + Dermatitis (skin rash), from niacin (vitamin B3) deficiency; reversible.
Down syndrome (6D85.9)Early Alzheimer-type dementia in a person with Down syndrome.

5Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 & ICD-11)

ICD-10

Dementia must be present (general rule) plus clear evidence that a specific named disease (Pick, CJD, Huntington, Parkinson, HIV, etc.) is causing it. Code the dementia and the disease.

ICD-11

Meets dementia requirements plus the specific disease is judged the direct cause; assign the right stem code (6D82 Lewy, 6D83 FTD, or a 6D85 sub-code) with the underlying-disease code, and severity/BPSD as needed.

6Easy way to remember

💡 Pattern-match the give-away

Fluctuation + visions + parkinsonism = Lewy · young + behaviour-first = FTD · fast + jerks = CJD · chorea + family = Huntington · wet/wacky/wobbly = NPH (fixable!) · 3 Ds = Pellagra.

7Treatment (shared principles)

  • Pharmacological: treat the underlying disease where possible — anti-HIV drugs (HIV), shunt (NPH), niacin (pellagra); cholinesterase inhibitors can help Lewy body/Parkinson dementia; AVOID antipsychotics in Lewy body dementia. K&S
  • Psychological/non-drug: disease-specific rehabilitation, safety, structured care, and caregiver support.
  • Prognosis: ranges from reversible (NPH, HIV on treatment, pellagra) to rapidly fatal (CJD); most degenerative types progress.

F03 · Unspecified dementia → ICD-11 6D8Z

ICD-10 F03
ICD-11 6D8Z (cause unknown/unspecified)
Status Kept (catch-all)
Headline change Equivalent residual category.

1Introduction

Dementia is clearly present (meets the general rule) but the exact cause is not yet known or does not fit a named type. It is a temporary/placeholder label. ICD-10 ICD-11

2–4Cause / who / symptoms

Cause: not yet identified. Symptoms: general dementia picture (multi-area decline, clear alertness, loss of independence) without features that pin down a specific disease.

5Diagnostic criteria

Diagnose dementia by the general rule; use "unspecified" only when a specific type cannot be assigned after assessment.

⭐ Exam point

★ "Unspecified" should never end the workup — always keep screening for reversible causes (DEMENTIA-R: thyroid, B12, NPH, depression, drugs) before accepting it.

6–7Remember & treatment

Remember: "unknown ≠ untreatable — keep looking." Treatment: general dementia care + carer support while investigations continue.

F04 · Organic amnesic syndrome → ICD-11 6D72 (Amnestic disorder)

ICD-10 F04 (not induced by alcohol/drugs)
ICD-11 6D72 (.10 alcohol, .11 sedatives, .12 other, .13 inhalants, .2 unknown)
Status Renamed
Headline change "Organic" dropped; alcohol/drug memory loss now sub-codes here.

1Introduction

A striking, memory-ONLY disorder: the person cannot form new memories, while attention and general cleverness stay normal. It is neither dementia (only memory is hit) nor delirium (the person is alert). ICD-10

2Etiology

  • Damage to memory circuits: hippocampus (memory-saving centre), mammillary bodies & thalamus (deep memory-relay parts), fornix.
  • Causes: thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency (Wernicke–Korsakoff — often alcohol-related), low oxygen, head injury, herpes encephalitis (a brain viral infection), stroke of memory areas, carbon-monoxide poisoning. K&S

3Epidemiology

Exact rates vary by cause; the classic form is Korsakoff from chronic heavy alcohol use with poor nutrition. More common where alcohol misuse and malnutrition are common.

4Symptoms — easy to spot + ⭐ unique

Easy to spot

  • Anterograde amnesia (can't make NEW memories after onset) — forgets things minutes later.
  • Variable retrograde amnesia (loses OLD memories before onset), usually recent past more than distant.
  • Immediate recall preserved (can repeat a number right now).
  • Attention, perception, general IQ normal; alert.

⭐ Unique clues — the classic exam link

★★ Alcohol (use or withdrawal) → thiamine deficiency → Wernicke → Korsakoff.

  • Wernicke encephalopathy (the acute, reversible emergency) = confusion + ataxia (unsteady walk) + ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus (abnormal eye movements). Give thiamine FAST.
  • If untreated → Korsakoff syndrome (the chronic memory-loss stage).
  • ★ Confabulation (making up false memories to fill gaps — NOT lying on purpose) — the give-away of Korsakoff.

5Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 & ICD-11)

ICD-10 diagnostic guidelines

  1. Memory impairment — poor recent memory (can't learn new material) ± loss of older memories.
  2. Preserved immediate recall (shows attention is intact — not delirium).
  3. No general decline of intellect and no clouding of consciousness (not dementia, not delirium).
  4. Objective evidence (history/exam/tests) of a brain cause. (Alcohol/drug forms coded in F10–F19 in ICD-10.)

ICD-11 essential features

  • Prominent memory impairment beyond what's expected for age, causing significant functional problems.
  • Other thinking areas relatively preserved (this separates it from dementia).
  • Not only during delirium; not better explained by another mental disorder.
  • Evidence of a medical or substance cause; pick the sub-code (e.g. alcohol = 6D72.10).

6Easy way to remember

💡 "CAN'T remember, MAKES it up"

CAN'T learn new (anterograde) + MAKES up stories (confabulation) + normal attention/IQ + alcohol/thiamine history = Korsakoff. Also: Wernicke triad = "COAT" = COnfusion, Ataxia, T = eye signs. Give thiamine.

7Treatment

Pharmacological

  • Urgent parenteral thiamine (vitamin B1 by injection) whenever Wernicke is suspected — prevents/limits Korsakoff. Treat the underlying cause. K&S

Psychological / non-drug

  • Compensatory memory strategies (memory aids, diaries, alarms, routines); environmental supports; supportive rehabilitation; treat alcohol dependence.

Prognosis: early thiamine can reverse Wernicke; established Korsakoff memory loss is often permanent.

F05 · Delirium → ICD-11 6D70

ICD-10 F05 (.0 not on dementia, .1 on dementia, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6D70 (.0 disease, .1 substance, .2 multiple, .Y/.Z)
Status Renamed / reorganised
Headline change Sorted by CAUSE now, not by "with/without dementia".

1Introduction

A sudden, come-and-go "brain failure": over hours the person's attention (ability to focus) and awareness (knowing what's around them) break down, caused by a physical illness. It is a medical emergency and usually reversible if the cause is fixed. ICD-11

2Etiology

Almost any acute body/brain problem — infection (urine, chest, sepsis), drugs (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids), metabolic/electrolyte upset, low oxygen, withdrawal from alcohol/sedatives, pain, urinary retention, constipation, after surgery. Predisposing: older age, existing dementia, sensory impairment, frailty. K&S

3Epidemiology

  • Very common in hospital — pooled prevalence about 36% at discharge in older inpatients. Other
  • In general-hospital patients ≥65: ~17% delirium alone, ~8% delirium-on-dementia. Other
  • Delirium superimposed on dementia (DSD) ranges ~22–89% (pooled ~49%). Other
  • Often under-diagnosed (especially the quiet, hypoactive type); raises later dementia risk.

4Symptoms — easy to spot + ⭐ unique

Easy to spot

  • Sudden confusion; can't focus/hold attention.
  • Disorientation (loses track of time/place); poor recent memory.
  • Visual hallucinations/illusions (seeing wrong things).
  • Restless or drowsy; disturbed sleep; fearful/irritable.

⭐ Unique clues — "see this → think Delirium"

  • ★ Attention is the hallmark — bedside: months backwards; a delirious patient fails it.
  • ★ Fluctuates within a day + sundowning (worse in evening/night).
  • ★ Hypoactive type is the trap (quiet, drowsy, withdrawn — mistaken for depression, often missed).
  • ★ Hallucinations usually VISUAL (unlike schizophrenia = voices).
  • ★★ Alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal → delirium tremens (severe withdrawal delirium with shakes, sweats, fast heart) — the ONE delirium treated with benzodiazepines.

5Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 & ICD-11)

ICD-10 — features needed in ALL areas

  1. Consciousness & attention — reduced clarity of awareness; can't direct/focus/sustain/shift attention.
  2. Cognition — distorted perception (illusions/visual hallucinations), muddled thinking, poor recent memory, disorientation.
  3. Psychomotor — over-active or under-active, unpredictable shifts.
  4. Sleep–wake — insomnia/reversal, night worsening.
  5. Emotion — fear, irritability, low mood, perplexity.
  6. Onset/course — rapid onset, fluctuating, usually < 6 months.

Sub-types: F05.0 not on dementia; F05.1 on dementia; F05.8 other; F05.9 unspecified.

ICD-11 essential features

  • Disturbance of attention + awareness.
  • Acute onset (hours–days), a change from baseline, tending to fluctuate during the day.
  • With other cognitive and/or emotion/behaviour/sleep changes.
  • Direct result of a medical condition, substance/medication, or multiple factors (pick the sub-code). Can be superimposed on dementia (code both).

6Easy way to remember

💡 "DELIRIUM" quick picture

Disordered attention · Electrolyte/metabolic · Level of consciousness fluctuates · Illusions (visual) · Rapid onset · Infection · Unstable emotion · Medications. Signature: sudden + fluctuating + attention gone + physically unwell.

7Treatment

Pharmacological K&S

  • Treat the cause — the real cure (antibiotics for infection, correct electrolytes/oxygen, stop offending drugs).
  • Drugs for symptoms only if severe distress/danger: low-dose antipsychotic short-term. Benzodiazepines are first-line ONLY for alcohol/sedative withdrawal delirium (otherwise they worsen delirium). Avoid antipsychotics in Lewy body dementia.

Psychological / non-drug (first-line)

  • Reorientation (clocks, calendars, familiar faces); good lighting; day–night cues; sensory aids (glasses/hearing).
  • Hydration/nutrition, early mobilisation, sleep hygiene, avoid restraints and deliriogenic drugs, involve family.

Prognosis: often reversible with prompt treatment, but linked to longer stays, higher mortality and later dementia; hypoactive and DSD do worse. Other

F06 · Other mental disorders due to brain/body disease → ICD-11 6E6x (secondary) + 6D71

ICD-10 F06 (.0 hallucinosis, .1 catatonic, .2 delusional, .3 mood, .4 anxiety, .5 dissociative, .6 emotionally labile, .7 mild cognitive)
ICD-11 6E61/6E62/6E63/6E65/6E67 (secondary syndromes) + 6D71 mild NCD
Status Moved / reorganised
Headline change Psychiatric symptoms caused by illness become "secondary syndromes"; mild cognitive disorder becomes 6D71.

1Introduction

Here the MAIN problem is a psychiatric symptom (hallucinations, delusions, mood change, anxiety, catatonia, dissociation) that is caused by a physical illness or drug — not a primary mental illness — and thinking is fairly normal. ICD-11 calls these secondary syndromes ("secondary" = a symptom that is the result of another medical condition). ICD-10 ICD-11

2Etiology

Brain diseases (tumour, epilepsy, stroke, encephalitis), endocrine problems (thyroid, cortisol), drugs (steroids, dopaminergics), metabolic disturbance — the same broad list as the general etiology framework, but here they produce a symptom syndrome rather than dementia/delirium. K&S

3Epidemiology

Common in medically ill and neurological patients; often under-recognised as "secondary". Mild neurocognitive disorder (6D71) is frequent in older adults and some cases progress to dementia.

4Symptoms + ⭐ unique · with sub-mapping

ICD-10ICD-11Simple meaning + ⭐ unique clue
F06.0/.2 hallucinosis/delusional6E61 secondary psychoticHallucinations/delusions from illness. ⭐ VISUAL hallucinations + clear mind + a medical cause → secondary, not schizophrenia.
F06.1 catatonic6E67 secondary catatoniaCatatonia (frozen/mute or wildly over-active movement) from a medical cause.
F06.3 mood (.30 manic, .31 bipolar, .32 depressive, .33 mixed)6E62 secondary mood⭐ First-ever mania in an older adult, or depression starting exactly with a new illness/steroid → secondary mood syndrome.
F06.4 anxiety6E63 secondary anxiety⭐ Sudden panic + racing heart from hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid) or phaeochromocytoma (an adrenaline-releasing tumour).
F06.5 dissociative6E65 secondary dissociativeDissociation from a medical cause.

⭐ Master rule — when to suspect a "secondary/organic" cause instead of a primary mental illness

  • ★ Wrong age (first psychosis or first mania in an older adult).
  • ★ Visual hallucinations (rather than voices).
  • ★ Symptoms started exactly with a new illness, drug, or abnormal lab.
  • ★ Abnormal exam/tests, fluctuating thinking, or poor response to usual psychiatric treatment.

5Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 & ICD-11)

ICD-10

A psychiatric syndrome (psychotic/mood/anxiety/etc.) with evidence of a brain or systemic disease/dysfunction judged to be causing it, and a time relationship between the disease and the symptoms. Code the syndrome + the cause.

ICD-11

Symptoms characteristic of the primary disorder (psychosis, mood, anxiety, etc.) judged to be the direct result of a medical condition/substance, not better explained by a primary mental disorder. Assign the secondary-syndrome code (6E6x) + the medical-cause code (order not meaningful). Mild NCD (6D71): objective mild decline not severe enough to reduce independence.

6Easy way to remember

💡 "Right symptom, WRONG story"

A textbook psychiatric picture appearing out of context (wrong age, visual hallucinations, tied to an illness/drug) = think secondary syndrome. Mild NCD = "slipping, but still independent".

7Treatment

  • Pharmacological: treat the physical cause (fix thyroid, stop offending drug) — symptoms often melt away; add symptom treatment as needed (antidepressant for secondary depression, cautious antipsychotic for secondary psychosis; for catatonia consider benzodiazepine/ECT). K&S
  • Psychological: psychoeducation, supportive therapy, and for mild NCD — cognitive strategies, risk-factor control, monitoring for progression.
  • Prognosis: often improves/reverses when the cause is treated; mild NCD is variable.

F07 · Personality & behaviour change due to brain disease → ICD-11 6E68

ICD-10 F07 (.0 personality, .1 postencephalitic, .2 postconcussional)
ICD-11 6E68 secondary personality change (.1/.2: no exact match)
Status Moved / renamed
Headline change "Organic personality disorder" → "secondary personality change".

1Introduction

After a brain injury/disease the person's personality changes from how they used to be — usually from frontal-lobe damage (front of the brain — controls judgement, impulse, planning). Thinking/memory can test near-normal, but they are "a different person". ICD-10

2Etiology

Frontal-lobe head injury, frontal tumour, stroke, encephalitis, or other frontal-system disease. K&S

3Epidemiology

Common after significant head injury and frontal pathology; a frequent reason families seek help ("he's not himself").

4Symptoms — easy + ⭐ unique

Easy to spot

  • Reduced ability to keep at a goal-directed task.
  • Altered emotions — lability (quick emotion swings), shallow cheerfulness, or apathy.
  • Poor judgement; altered social/sexual behaviour.

⭐ Unique clues

  • ★ Clear change from a previous baseline AFTER a brain event (NOT lifelong like a primary personality disorder).
  • ★ Disinhibition (rude, impulsive, inappropriate) OR apathy (flat, no drive).
  • ★ Poor executive function (planning, self-control) with fairly intact memory → "same intellect, different person".

The two post-injury syndromes: F07.1 postencephalitic (leftover behaviour/mood change after encephalitis (brain infection/inflammation); often improves) and F07.2 postconcussional (after head injury: headache, dizziness, tiredness, poor concentration, irritability, poor sleep). ⭐ In ICD-11 these have no exact code — handled in the neurology chapter + secondary syndromes.

5Diagnostic criteria (ICD-10 & ICD-11)

ICD-10 (F07.0)

A significant change from the usual premorbid pattern of behaviour after brain disease/damage, especially in emotions, needs and impulses — e.g. reduced goal-persistence, emotional lability/apathy, disinhibition, suspiciousness/over-inclusive thinking, altered rate of language, altered sexual behaviour.

ICD-11 (6E68)

A persistent personality change (departure from the person's previous pattern) judged to be the direct result of a medical condition/brain insult, not better explained by another mental disorder; code with the underlying condition.

6Easy way to remember

💡 "Same brain-power, different person"

Change from baseline + frontal cause + disinhibition/apathy + intact memory = organic/secondary personality change. Contrast: primary personality disorder is lifelong, no brain insult.

7Treatment

  • Pharmacological: treat the underlying condition; symptomatic drugs for aggression/lability if needed (used cautiously). K&S
  • Psychological: behavioural management, environmental structure, family psychoeducation, neuro-rehabilitation (retraining after brain injury).

F09 · Unspecified organic or symptomatic mental disorder → ICD-11 6E6Z

ICD-10 F09
ICD-11 6E6Z (secondary syndrome, unspecified)
Status Kept (catch-all)
Headline change Equivalent placeholder.

1Introduction

There IS a mental disorder attributable to a brain/body illness, but it cannot be put in a more exact box. A catch-all placeholder pending clarification. ICD-10 ICD-11

2–7In brief

Cause: a diagnosable brain/body condition. Symptoms: mixed/unclear organic mental picture. Criteria: evidence of an organic mental disorder that doesn't fit specific categories. Remember: a temporary label — keep investigating. Treatment: treat the underlying cause + symptom support.
