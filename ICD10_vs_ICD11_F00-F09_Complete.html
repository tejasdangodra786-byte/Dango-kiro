What is ICD?

The International Classification of Diseases (ICD) (a global system of codes and names for every disease and health condition) is developed and maintained by the World Health Organization (WHO). It serves as the international standard for diagnostic classification, enabling uniform recording and reporting of diseases for clinical communication, epidemiological surveillance (tracking disease patterns in populations), health-service planning, research comparisons across countries, morbidity and mortality statistics, and reimbursement coding.

ICD-10

The Tenth Revision of the ICD was endorsed by the World Health Assembly in 1990 and came into widespread use in 1994. Its chapter on mental and behavioural disorders was published with a dedicated clinical companion volume: the Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Guidelines (CDDG), commonly known as the "Blue Book" (WHO, 1992). This volume provides prototype-based diagnostic descriptions for each mental disorder, organised under alphanumeric F-codes (F00 through F99). It has been the principal international diagnostic classification in psychiatry for three decades.

ICD-11

The Eleventh Revision was adopted by the World Health Assembly in May 2019 and came into effect for member states from January 2022. For mental, behavioural and neurodevelopmental disorders (Chapter 06), the WHO released the Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements (CDDR), which replaced the CDDG. The CDDR provides a more consistently structured text for each disorder, organised under a new alphanumeric coding system (e.g. 6D80 rather than F00). Major structural improvements include: uniform headings across all disorder categories (essential features, additional clinical features, boundary with normality, course features, developmental presentations, culture-related features, sex- and gender-related features, boundaries with other disorders); the introduction of post-coordination (the ability to add extension codes for severity, provisional status, or differential diagnosis to a stem code); and a systematic approach to dimensional qualifiers within an otherwise categorical system.

The F00–F09 Block — "Organic, Including Symptomatic, Mental Disorders"

Meaning and scope in ICD-10

ICD-10 This block comprises a range of mental disorders grouped together on the basis of their common, demonstrable aetiology (cause) in cerebral disease (disease directly affecting the brain), brain injury, or other insult leading to cerebral dysfunction. The dysfunction may be primary — in diseases, injuries, and insults that affect the brain directly or with predilection — or secondary — in systemic diseases and disorders that attack the brain only as one of the multiple organs or systems of the body involved.

Two key terms define this block:

  • "Organic" (the syndrome can be attributed to an independently diagnosable cerebral or systemic disease or disorder). The use of this term does not imply that conditions elsewhere in the classification are "non-organic" in the sense of having no cerebral substrate; it means simply that the syndrome so classified can be attributed to an independently diagnosable disease.
  • "Symptomatic" (cerebral involvement is secondary to a systemic extra-cerebral disease or disorder), for example hepatic encephalopathy (brain dysfunction caused by liver failure) or thyroid-related cognitive change.

ICD-10 identifies two main clinical clusters within this block:

  1. Cognitive/sensorium cluster — the invariable and most prominent features are disturbances of cognitive functions (memory, intellect, learning) or disturbances of the sensorium (consciousness, attention). This cluster includes the dementias, delirium, and the amnesic syndrome.
  2. Symptomatic psychiatric cluster — the most conspicuous manifestations are in perception (hallucinations), thought content (delusions), mood and emotion, or overall personality and behaviour, while cognitive or sensory dysfunction is minimal or difficult to ascertain. This cluster includes organic hallucinosis, organic delusional disorder, organic mood disorders, organic anxiety disorder, organic personality change, and others.

In the majority of cases, the recording of a diagnosis requires the use of two codes: one for the psychopathological syndrome (the mental-disorder diagnosis) and another for the underlying disorder (the causal medical condition).

Alcohol- and drug-caused brain disorders, though logically belonging to this group, are classified under F10–F19 for practical reasons.

How ICD-11 reconceptualises this territory

ICD-11 ICD-11 deliberately retires the term "organic" because all mental disorders have a biological substrate; the term was considered to create a misleading distinction. The conditions formerly grouped under F00–F09 are distributed across two ICD-11 groupings:

  1. Neurocognitive disorders (acquired conditions where cognition is the principal affected area) — delirium (6D70), mild neurocognitive disorder (6D71), amnestic disorder (6D72), and the dementias (6D80–6D86). Code range: 6D70–6D86.
  2. Secondary mental or behavioural syndromes associated with disorders or diseases classified elsewhere (psychiatric presentations that are the direct result of a medical condition) — secondary psychotic (6E61), mood (6E62), anxiety (6E63), obsessive-compulsive (6E64), dissociative (6E65), impulse-control (6E66), catatonia (6E67), and personality change (6E68). Code range: 6E60–6E69.

General Concept of Dementia (Applicable to F00–F03 and 6D80–6D85)

Both ICD-10 and ICD-11 provide a general definition of dementia that sets the minimum requirement for diagnosing dementia of any specific type. The type-specific criteria are then applied on top of this general definition.

ICD-10 General description of dementia

Dementia is a syndrome due to disease of the brain, usually of a chronic (long-lasting) or progressive (gradually worsening) nature, in which there is disturbance of multiple higher cortical functions, including memory, thinking, orientation (awareness of time, place, and person), comprehension, calculation, learning capacity, language, and judgement. Consciousness is not clouded (the person's level of alertness and awareness is preserved — this is what distinguishes dementia from delirium). The impairments of cognitive function are commonly accompanied, and occasionally preceded, by deterioration in emotional control, social behaviour, or motivation.

Dementia produces an appreciable decline in intellectual functioning, and usually some interference with personal activities of daily living (basic self-care tasks such as washing, dressing, eating, personal hygiene, and toilet activities). How such a decline manifests itself will depend largely on the social and cultural setting in which the patient lives.

Important diagnostic caution built into ICD-10: special care should be taken to avoid false-positive identification. Motivational or emotional factors, particularly depression, in addition to motor slowness and general physical frailty, rather than loss of intellectual capacity, may account for failure to perform.

Duration: For a confident clinical diagnosis, the decline in memory and other cognitive functions should have been present for at least 6 months. If the period since the manifest onset is shorter, the diagnosis can only be tentative.

ICD-11 General definition of dementia

Dementia is an acquired (develops later in life, not present from birth), chronic condition characterised by significant cognitive impairment or decline from a previous level of cognitive functioning in two or more cognitive domains (memory, attention, executive function, language, social cognition, psychomotor speed, visuoperceptual or visuospatial abilities) that is sufficiently severe to interfere with performance or independence in activities of daily living.

If memory difficulties and other cognitive symptoms in older adults occur exclusively in the context of a depressive episode, a diagnosis of dementia is generally not appropriate. However, a depressive episode can be superimposed on dementia.

1. Introduction

Alzheimer disease is a primary degenerative cerebral disease (a disease in which brain cells progressively die, with the brain itself being the primary site of the disease) of unknown aetiology, with characteristic neuropathological (microscopic tissue changes in the brain) and neurochemical (changes in the brain's chemical messengers) features. It is the single most common cause of dementia worldwide. The onset is usually insidious (so gradual that neither the patient nor the family can identify when it began), and the disease develops slowly but steadily over a period of years — typically 8 to 10 years from diagnosis to death, though this can be as short as 2 to 3 years or considerably longer.

The clinical picture is dominated by a slowly progressive, amnestic (memory-predominant) dementia in which recent memory is affected first, followed by progressive involvement of language, praxis, visuospatial function, and executive function, while consciousness remains clear until the very late stages. ICD-10 notes that dementia in Alzheimer's disease is at present irreversible.

2. Aetiology and Pathophysiology

Neuropathological hallmarks

ICD-10 describes the characteristic brain changes: a marked reduction in the population of neurons, particularly in the hippocampus (the brain's principal memory-forming structure), substantia innominata, locus ceruleus, and the temporoparietal and frontal cortex; the appearance of neurofibrillary tangles (twisted intracellular threads made of hyperphosphorylated tau protein) made of paired helical filaments; neuritic (argentophil) plaques (extracellular deposits consisting largely of beta-amyloid protein); and granulovacuolar bodies. ICD-10

Neurochemical changes

There is a marked reduction in the enzyme choline acetyltransferase (the enzyme that manufactures the memory neurotransmitter acetylcholine), in acetylcholine itself, and in other neurotransmitters and neuromodulators. This cholinergic deficit (shortage of acetylcholine signalling) is the direct rationale for treatment with cholinesterase inhibitors. Kaplan & Sadock

Molecular and genetic mechanisms

  • Amyloid cascade hypothesis (the leading theory that abnormal accumulation of beta-amyloid triggers the disease): aberrant cleavage of amyloid precursor protein produces beta-amyloid, which aggregates into plaques, triggering tau hyperphosphorylation, neuroinflammation, synaptic loss, and neuronal death. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Early-onset familial Alzheimer disease (autosomal dominant, <5% of cases): mutations in the amyloid precursor protein gene (APP, chromosome 21), presenilin-1 (PSEN1, chromosome 14), and presenilin-2 (PSEN2, chromosome 1).
  • Late-onset Alzheimer disease: the apolipoprotein E epsilon-4 allele (APOE ε4) (a genetic variant that increases risk and lowers age of onset) is the strongest genetic risk factor; possession of two copies markedly increases risk.
  • Down syndrome (trisomy 21): because the APP gene lies on chromosome 21, virtually all individuals with Down syndrome develop Alzheimer-type neuropathology by middle age, and many develop clinical dementia early.

Risk and protective factors

  • Risk factors: advancing age (the single strongest risk factor), family history, APOE ε4, female sex, low educational attainment / low cognitive reserve (the brain's resilience built up through education and mental activity), midlife vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, obesity), head injury, hearing loss, social isolation, depression.
  • Protective factors: higher education, cognitive and physical activity, social engagement, treatment of vascular risk factors.

3. Epidemiology

  • Proportion of dementias: Alzheimer disease accounts for approximately 50–60% of all dementia cases, making it the most common cause of dementia. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Prevalence and age: prevalence rises steeply with age, approximately doubling every five years after age 65. It affects a small percentage of those aged 65–69 but a substantial proportion (often cited around one-third or more) of those aged 85 and over. Other evidence
  • Onset distribution: late-onset (after 65) is far more common than early-onset (before 65). Early-onset cases are more likely to be familial, progress more rapidly, and show prominent temporal and parietal features including dysphasia (language difficulty) and dyspraxia (difficulty performing skilled movements).
  • Sex: more common in women, partly attributable to their greater longevity.
  • Global burden: dementia (Alzheimer being the leading cause) is among the major causes of disability and dependency in older people worldwide, with numbers rising as populations age.
  • Indian context: community studies report dementia prevalence in older adults that increases sharply with age; estimates vary by region, screening instrument, and rural versus urban setting, so any figure should be quoted with its source and method. Other evidence

Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)

Depression, apathy, and anxiety early; agitation, aggression, wandering, disinhibition, delusions (commonly of theft), and hallucinations in later stages. In ICD-11 these are coded separately under 6D86.

Unique and easily identifiable clues — "recognise Alzheimer disease"

  • Memory-first, insidious, steadily progressive decline over years, with clear consciousness throughout.
  • Recent (episodic) memory is affected out of proportion to remote memory early on.
  • Anosognosia (the patient is unaware of, or denies, the deficit) — the family is worried while the patient minimises the problem.
  • No early focal neurological signs and no stepwise deterioration (which would suggest a vascular cause).
  • Neuroimaging shows medial temporal lobe and hippocampal atrophy.
  • A person with Down syndrome developing dementia in middle age is presumptively Alzheimer type.

5. Diagnostic Criteria (strictly from ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines

The following features are required for a diagnosis:

  1. Presence of dementia as described in the general criteria (decline in memory and other cognitive functions, clear consciousness, present for at least 6 months).
  2. Insidious onset with slow deterioration. While the onset is usually difficult to pinpoint in time, realisation by others that the defect exists may come suddenly. An apparent plateau may occur in the progression.
  3. Absence of clinical evidence, or findings from special investigations, to suggest that the mental state may be due to other systemic or brain disease which can induce a dementia (e.g. hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, niacin deficiency, neurosyphilis, normal-pressure hydrocephalus, subdural haematoma).
  4. Absence of a sudden apoplectic onset (sudden stroke-like onset), or of neurological signs of focal damage such as hemiparesis, sensory loss, visual field defects, and incoordination occurring early in the illness (although these phenomena may later be superimposed).

Subtypes:

  • F00.0 — with early onset: onset before age 65, with relatively rapid deterioration and marked multiple disorders of the higher cortical functions. Aphasia, agraphia (impaired writing), alexia (impaired reading), and apraxia occur relatively early. Family history of Alzheimer's disease is a contributory but not necessary factor.
  • F00.1 — with late onset: onset after age 65, usually in the late 70s or thereafter, with slow progression and memory impairment as the principal feature.
  • F00.2 — atypical or mixed type: dementias not fitting F00.0 or F00.1; mixed Alzheimer and vascular dementias are included here.
  • F00.9 — unspecified.

ICD-11 Essential (required) features

  1. All general diagnostic requirements for Dementia are met (acquired decline in two or more cognitive domains, interfering with independence in activities of daily living).
  2. Onset of symptoms is insidious, with a gradual progression in cognitive impairment.
  3. There is typically prominent impairment in memory that is present early in the course, though a minority present with non-amnestic (e.g. language, visuospatial, or executive) presentations.
  4. There is no evidence of mixed aetiology (i.e. no evidence that another systemic disease, brain disease, or substance is causing the cognitive decline), unless a mixed subtype is being coded.
  5. The pattern of onset, course, and clinical features is not better accounted for by another neurocognitive disorder, mental disorder, or the effects of a substance or medication.

Subtypes / qualifiers: 6D80.0 with early onset; 6D80.1 with late onset; 6D80.2 mixed type, with cerebrovascular disease; 6D80.3 mixed type, with other nonvascular aetiologies; 6D80.Z onset unknown or unspecified. Severity is coded with extension codes (XS5W mild, XS0T moderate, XS25 severe); behavioural or psychological disturbances are coded with 6D86.

6. Easy Way to Remember

Memory aid

The "4 A's" of Alzheimer capture the classic cortical deficits: Amnesia (memory loss, first), Aphasia (language), Apraxia (skilled movement), Agnosia (recognition). Add a fifth "A" — Anosognosia (lack of insight). The pathology mnemonic: "Plaques and Tangles, low Acetylcholine."

Clinical signature: insidious onset + steadily progressive + memory-first + alert + no early focal signs + hippocampal atrophy = Alzheimer disease.

Psychological and psychosocial Kaplan & Sadock

  • Cognitive stimulation therapy (structured group activities and discussions that exercise thinking and memory).
  • Reality orientation (repeatedly presenting time, place, and person information to reduce disorientation) and environmental modifications (clear signage, clocks, calendars, good lighting, safe layout).
  • Reminiscence therapy (using past memories, photographs, and music to stimulate engagement and mood).
  • Behavioural analysis of triggers for agitation; music and activity-based therapies.
  • Caregiver interventions: psychoeducation, training in communication and behaviour management, respite care, support for caregiver burden, and advance care planning.
  • Attention to physical health, nutrition, hydration, sensory aids (glasses, hearing aids), and sleep.

Prognosis: progressive and, at present, irreversible; treatments slow symptom progression rather than modify the underlying disease. Median survival from diagnosis is several years, shorter with early or rapidly progressive onset.

ICD-11 code 6D81 Dementia due to cerebrovascular disease
Status Renamed / reorganised
Key change ICD-10 vascular subtypes not preserved as separate stem codes

1. Introduction

Vascular dementia is dementia resulting from cerebrovascular disease (disease of the blood vessels supplying the brain — including strokes and chronic small-vessel damage). It results from infarction of the brain due to vascular disease, including hypertensive cerebrovascular disease. It is the second most common cause of dementia after Alzheimer disease, and the two frequently co-exist as mixed dementia. Onset is usually in later life. The distinguishing clinical feature is that the deficits reflect the location and extent of the vascular lesions, so the picture is often uneven ("patchy") and accompanied by focal neurological signs.

2. Aetiology and Pathophysiology

  • Large-vessel disease: territorial infarcts (areas of dead brain tissue caused by a blocked artery) from thromboembolism, producing multi-infarct dementia.
  • Small-vessel disease: chronic damage to small penetrating arteries causing lacunar infarcts (small deep strokes) and diffuse white-matter disease (damage to the brain's connecting fibre tracts), producing subcortical vascular dementia (Binswanger type).
  • Strategic infarcts: a single stroke in a critical location (e.g. thalamus, angular gyrus) can produce dementia.
  • Haemorrhage and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (amyloid deposition in blood-vessel walls, causing bleeding).
  • Risk factors: hypertension (the most important modifiable factor), diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidaemia, atrial fibrillation (an irregular heart rhythm that can throw clots to the brain), smoking, prior stroke or transient ischaemic attack, and older age. Kaplan & Sadock

3. Epidemiology

  • The second most common type of dementia (after Alzheimer disease); estimates commonly attribute a substantial minority of dementia cases to vascular or mixed vascular pathology. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Frequently co-exists with Alzheimer pathology (mixed dementia), especially in the very old.
  • More common in populations and regions with a high burden of vascular risk factors; higher in men in some series (reflecting vascular-disease distribution).
  • An important public-health point: because the risk factors are modifiable, a proportion of vascular dementia is preventable, and progression can be slowed by risk-factor control. Other evidence

4. Clinical Features and Symptoms

  • Cognitive: slowed processing speed (how quickly the person can think and respond), impaired attention, and executive dysfunction (difficulty planning, organising, and problem-solving) often exceed memory loss early — the reverse of Alzheimer disease.
  • Neurological: focal signs such as unilateral limb weakness, exaggerated tendon reflexes, extensor plantar response, gait disturbance, and pseudobulbar palsy (impaired control of the muscles of the face, throat, and tongue, causing difficulty with speech and swallowing and sudden uncontrolled crying or laughing).
  • Emotional: depression and emotional lability ("emotional incontinence" — sudden crying or laughing that the person cannot control) are common; insight and personality are relatively preserved until late.

Unique and easily identifiable clues — "recognise vascular dementia"

  • Stepwise deterioration (the decline occurs in sudden drops separated by plateaus, rather than a smooth slope), or onset in close temporal relationship to a stroke.
  • Focal neurological signs and gait disturbance appearing early in the illness.
  • Executive dysfunction and slowed processing dominate; memory may be relatively spared early.
  • "Patchy" cognitive profile — some functions markedly impaired, others preserved.
  • Prominent vascular risk factors and neuroimaging showing infarcts or extensive white-matter disease.

5. Diagnostic Criteria (strictly from ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines

  1. The general criteria for dementia must be met.
  2. Deficits in higher cognitive functions are unevenly distributed, with some functions affected and others relatively spared. Thus memory may be quite markedly affected while thinking, reasoning, and information processing may show only mild decline. Insight and judgement may be relatively well preserved.
  3. There is clinical evidence of focal brain damage, manifest as at least one of: unilateral spastic weakness of the limbs; unilaterally increased tendon reflexes; an extensor plantar response; pseudobulbar palsy.
  4. There is evidence from the history, examination, or tests of significant cerebrovascular disease, which may reasonably be judged to be aetiologically related to the dementia.

Subtypes: F01.0 vascular dementia of acute onset (rapidly after a succession of strokes or, occasionally, a single large infarct); F01.1 multi-infarct dementia (gradual accumulation of infarcts, predominantly cortical, with stepwise progression); F01.2 subcortical vascular dementia (history of hypertension, foci of ischaemic destruction in deep white matter; cerebral cortex usually preserved, which may contrast with the clinical picture resembling Alzheimer's disease); F01.3 mixed cortical and subcortical vascular dementia.

ICD-11 Essential (required) features

  1. All general diagnostic requirements for Dementia are met.
  2. There is evidence of cerebrovascular disease judged to be sufficient to account for the neurocognitive deficits, based on history (e.g. documented stroke), physical examination (focal neurological signs), and/or neuroimaging (evidence of significant infarction, haemorrhage, or small-vessel disease).
  3. The onset of the cognitive deficits is temporally related to one or more cerebrovascular events, or the pattern and course of decline (e.g. stepwise progression, or a subcortical pattern with prominent slowing and executive dysfunction) is consistent with cerebrovascular disease.
  4. The deficits are not better accounted for by another neurocognitive disorder or condition.

Severity coded with extension codes; behavioural or psychological disturbances coded with 6D86.

6. Easy Way to Remember

Memory aid — "STEP"

Stepwise decline · Temporally linked to strokes · Executive function and processing speed hit first · Patchy deficits with focal signs and vascular risk factors.

Clinical signature: stepwise or post-stroke onset + focal neurological signs + prominent vascular risk factors + executive/processing deficits ≥ memory = vascular dementia.

7. Treatment

Pharmacological Kaplan & Sadock

  • Secondary prevention is the cornerstone: aggressive control of vascular risk factors — antihypertensives, statins for hyperlipidaemia, glycaemic control in diabetes, and antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy (blood-thinning treatment to prevent further strokes) as indicated (e.g. anticoagulation for atrial fibrillation).
  • Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine have a limited and less-established role but may be used, particularly where Alzheimer pathology co-exists (mixed dementia).
  • Treat comorbid depression (SSRIs); manage emotional lability.

Psychological and psychosocial

  • Cognitive rehabilitation (structured retraining and compensatory strategies for lost cognitive skills).
  • Physiotherapy and occupational therapy for gait and functional impairment; speech therapy where relevant.
  • Mood-focused interventions; lifestyle change (smoking cessation, diet, exercise); caregiver education and support.

Prognosis: variable; stabilisation is possible if vascular risk factors are well controlled, but the condition progresses if cerebrovascular disease continues. Life expectancy is often shorter than in Alzheimer disease because of associated cardiovascular mortality.

Dementia with Lewy Bodies → ICD-11 6D82

ICD-10 No dedicated code (subsumed under F02.8 "other")
ICD-11 6D82 Dementia due to Lewy body disease
Status New stand-alone category in ICD-11
Key change Recognised as a distinct entity for the first time

1. Introduction

A progressive degenerative dementia caused by Lewy bodies (abnormal spherical deposits of the protein alpha-synuclein inside neurons). It is considered the third most common degenerative dementia after Alzheimer and frontotemporal disease. It occupies a clinical spectrum with Parkinson disease dementia, distinguished mainly by the timing of dementia relative to motor symptoms.

2. Aetiology and Pathophysiology

Widespread cortical and subcortical deposition of alpha-synuclein as Lewy bodies, with associated cholinergic and dopaminergic deficits. The prominent cholinergic loss underlies the marked cognitive fluctuations and responsiveness to cholinesterase inhibitors; dopaminergic loss underlies the parkinsonism. Kaplan & Sadock

3. Epidemiology

Estimated to account for a notable minority of degenerative dementias; onset usually in older adults; somewhat more common in men. Frequently under- or mis-diagnosed as Alzheimer disease or Parkinson disease.

4. Clinical Features and Symptoms

Unique and easily identifiable clues — the core features

  • Fluctuating cognition with pronounced variation in attention and alertness (lucid periods alternating with marked confusion, sometimes within the same day).
  • Recurrent, well-formed visual hallucinations (clearly seeing people, animals, or objects that are not there), often of people or small animals.
  • Spontaneous parkinsonism (slowness of movement, rigidity, and tremor arising without medication), typically emerging around the same time as the cognitive decline.
  • REM sleep behaviour disorder (physically acting out dreams — kicking, shouting, punching during sleep), which may precede the dementia by years.
  • Severe neuroleptic (antipsychotic) sensitivity — antipsychotic drugs can cause severe, sometimes life-threatening, reactions and must be used with extreme caution or avoided.

5. Diagnostic Criteria

ICD-10

ICD-10 does not provide a dedicated category; such cases were classified under F02.8 (dementia in other specified diseases classified elsewhere), diagnosed by meeting the general dementia criteria together with evidence of the underlying Lewy body disease.

ICD-11 Essential features

  1. General requirements for Dementia are met.
  2. The presentation is characterised by the core features of Lewy body disease: fluctuating cognition, recurrent visual hallucinations, spontaneous parkinsonism, and/or REM sleep behaviour disorder.
  3. The cognitive and other features are judged to be a direct consequence of Lewy body disease and are not better accounted for by another disorder.

6. Easy Way to Remember

Memory aid — "FVPR"

Fluctuation · Visual hallucinations · Parkinsonism · REM sleep behaviour disorder. And the safety rule: never give standard antipsychotics casually (neuroleptic sensitivity).

7. Treatment

  • Pharmacological: cholinesterase inhibitors (rivastigmine, donepezil) are often notably effective for cognition and hallucinations. Parkinsonism may be treated cautiously with levodopa (which can worsen hallucinations). Avoid or minimise antipsychotics; if unavoidable, use the lowest dose of an agent with low extrapyramidal risk. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Psychological: psychoeducation about fluctuations and hallucinations, environmental safety, structured routine, caregiver support, and management of REM sleep behaviour disorder.

Frontotemporal Dementia (formerly Pick's Disease) → ICD-11 6D83

ICD-10 F02.0 Dementia in Pick's disease
ICD-11 6D83 Frontotemporal dementia (requires second code 8A23, frontotemporal lobar degeneration)
Status Concept broadened
Key change From the single eponymous "Pick's disease" to the frontotemporal dementia family

1. Introduction

A group of progressive dementias caused by degeneration of the frontal and temporal lobes (the front and side regions of the brain, controlling personality, behaviour, and language). Compared with Alzheimer disease, onset is younger and the earliest features are change in personality/behaviour or in language, rather than memory. ICD-10 described this under Pick's disease, characterised by selective atrophy of the frontal and temporal lobes with Pick bodies.

2. Aetiology and Pathophysiology

Frontotemporal lobar degeneration with abnormal accumulation of tau or TDP-43 protein. A significant proportion is familial, with mutations in genes such as MAPT, GRN, and C9orf72. Kaplan & Sadock

3. Epidemiology

A leading cause of early-onset (before 65) dementia, with onset commonly in the 50s and 60s; roughly equal sex distribution; frequently a family history.

4. Clinical Features and Symptoms

Unique and easily identifiable clues

  • Younger onset (50s–60s).
  • Behavioural variant: early and prominent change in personality and social conduct — disinhibition (loss of normal social restraint — tactless, impulsive, or inappropriate behaviour), apathy (loss of motivation and interest), loss of empathy, compulsive or ritualistic behaviour, and dietary/oral changes.
  • Language variants: progressive non-fluent aphasia (speech becomes effortful, hesitant, and agrammatic) or semantic dementia (progressive loss of the meaning of words and objects).
  • Memory is relatively preserved early — the opposite of Alzheimer disease.
  • Neuroimaging shows focal frontal and/or temporal atrophy.

5. Diagnostic Criteria

ICD-10 (F02.0, Pick's disease)

A progressive dementia commencing in middle age (typically 50–60), characterised by slowly progressive changes of character and social deterioration, followed by impairment of intellect, memory, and language, with apathy, euphoria, and occasionally extrapyramidal phenomena. General dementia criteria plus evidence of the characteristic predominance of frontal-lobe features (euphoria, emotional blunting, coarsening of social behaviour, disinhibition) which may precede memory impairment.

ICD-11 (6D83) Essential features

  1. General requirements for Dementia are met.
  2. Onset is insidious with gradual progression, typically with early and prominent changes in behaviour and personality (behavioural variant) and/or progressive language impairment (language variants), with relative sparing of memory and visuospatial function early.
  3. The features are attributable to frontotemporal neurodegeneration and not better explained by another disorder. A second code (8A23) identifies the frontotemporal lobar degeneration.

6. Easy Way to Remember

Memory aid

"Personality first, memory later, and young." Frontal = behaviour/disinhibition/apathy; Temporal = language. Contrast sharply with Alzheimer's memory-first picture.

7. Treatment

  • Pharmacological: no cure; SSRIs may help behavioural symptoms (disinhibition, compulsions); cholinesterase inhibitors are generally not beneficial and may worsen behaviour; antipsychotics used cautiously for severe agitation. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Psychological: behavioural management, structured environment, caregiver education (behavioural symptoms are especially distressing to families), and safety planning.

Dementia in Creutzfeldt–Jakob Disease → ICD-11 6D85.5 (Prion Disease)

ICD-10 F02.1
ICD-11 6D85.5 Dementia due to prion disease
Status Moved to sub-code of 6D85
Key change Renamed to the aetiological term "prion disease"

1–3. Introduction, aetiology, epidemiology

A rare, rapidly progressive, and invariably fatal dementia caused by prions (infectious, abnormally folded proteins that induce normal brain proteins to misfold), producing spongiform encephalopathy (the brain develops a sponge-like appearance from microscopic holes). Rare (roughly one to two cases per million per year). Sporadic, genetic, and acquired (iatrogenic, variant CJD from contaminated beef) forms exist.

4. Clinical features / unique clues

Unique clues

  • Extremely rapid dementia progressing over weeks to a few months (much faster than other dementias).
  • Myoclonus (sudden, brief, shock-like involuntary muscle jerks), often startle-induced.
  • Rapidly evolving cerebellar, visual, and pyramidal signs; characteristic EEG and MRI changes; rapid progression to akinetic mutism and death.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10 (F02.1)

A progressive dementia with extensive neurological signs, due to specific neuropathological changes (subacute spongiform encephalopathy) presumed to be caused by a transmissible agent. Onset usually in middle or later life, with a typically rapid course. General dementia criteria plus the characteristic triad of rapidly progressive devastating dementia, pyramidal/extrapyramidal disease with myoclonus, and a characteristic (triphasic) EEG.

ICD-11 (6D85.5)

Dementia requirements met, judged to be a direct consequence of prion disease, supported by the clinical course and investigations. Coded with the underlying prion disease.

6–7. Remember & treatment

Memory aid

"Rapid dementia + myoclonus = think prion (CJD)."

Treatment: no disease-modifying treatment exists; management is palliative (comfort-focused) — symptom control (myoclonus, agitation), supportive nursing, and family support.

Dementia in Huntington's Disease → ICD-11 6D85.1

ICD-10 F02.2
ICD-11 6D85.1 Dementia due to Huntington disease
Status Moved to sub-code of 6D85
Key change Terminology only

1–3. Introduction, aetiology, epidemiology

An autosomal dominant (inherited such that each child of an affected parent has a 50% chance of inheriting it) neurodegenerative disease caused by an expanded CAG trinucleotide repeat (an abnormally long, repeated segment of DNA) in the huntingtin gene on chromosome 4, causing degeneration of the caudate nucleus and putamen (deep brain structures controlling movement). Onset typically in the 30s–50s. Positive family history is usual.

4. Unique clues

Unique clues

  • Chorea (rapid, irregular, dance-like involuntary movements).
  • Family history of the same condition (dominant inheritance).
  • Prominent early psychiatric and behavioural change (depression, irritability, personality change) often preceding the movement disorder; subcortical dementia pattern (slowed thinking, executive and memory-retrieval difficulty).
  • High suicide risk.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10 (F02.2)

Dementia occurring as part of a widespread degeneration of the brain in Huntington's disease. General dementia criteria plus the association of choreiform movement disorder, dementia, and a family history of Huntington's disease. Involuntary choreiform movements, typically of the face, hands, and shoulders, or in the gait, are early features. Dementia is characterised by predominant frontal-lobe features early, with relative preservation of memory until later.

ICD-11 (6D85.1)

Dementia requirements met and judged a direct consequence of Huntington disease (confirmed by family history and/or genetic testing). Coded with the Huntington disease diagnosis.

6–7. Remember & treatment

Memory aid

"Chorea + family history + psychiatric change = Huntington."

Treatment: no cure; chorea may be treated with agents such as tetrabenazine or antipsychotics; treat depression (SSRIs) given high suicide risk; genetic counselling for the family; psychological support and caregiver interventions. Kaplan & Sadock

Dementia in Parkinson's Disease → ICD-11 6D85.0

ICD-10 F02.3
ICD-11 6D85.0 Dementia due to Parkinson disease
Status Moved to sub-code of 6D85
Key change Terminology only

1–3. Introduction, aetiology, epidemiology

Dementia developing in a person with established Parkinson disease — a neurodegenerative disorder with loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra (a midbrain structure that produces dopamine for movement control) and alpha-synuclein pathology. A substantial proportion of Parkinson patients develop dementia as the disease advances, the risk rising with age and disease duration. Its relationship to dementia with Lewy bodies is close: the distinction rests on whether dementia begins well after (Parkinson disease dementia) or around the same time as (Lewy body dementia) the motor features.

4. Unique clues

Unique clues

  • Established Parkinson disease for years first (tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia), then dementia emerges.
  • Subcortical, dysexecutive pattern with slowed processing; visual hallucinations (often partly drug-related) common.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10 (F02.3)

A dementia developing in the course of established Parkinson's disease. General dementia criteria are met in a patient with Parkinson's disease; no particular distinguishing clinical features have yet been demonstrated.

ICD-11 (6D85.0)

Dementia requirements met, arising in the context of established Parkinson disease and judged to be a direct consequence of it.

6–7. Remember & treatment

Memory aid

"Motor first (years), dementia later = Parkinson disease dementia" (contrast Lewy body: dementia and motor features together).

Treatment: cholinesterase inhibitors (rivastigmine) can help cognition; optimise Parkinson medication balancing motor benefit against hallucinations; avoid strong antipsychotics; psychological and caregiver support. Kaplan & Sadock

1–3. Introduction, aetiology, epidemiology

Dementia caused by direct HIV infection of the brain (HIV-associated neurocognitive disorder). More common in advanced, untreated HIV; incidence of severe forms has fallen greatly with effective antiretroviral therapy. It is characteristically a subcortical dementia (a pattern of slowed thinking and movement, apathy, and memory-retrieval difficulty, from damage to deep brain structures).

4. Unique clues

Unique clues

  • Subcortical pattern: psychomotor slowing, apathy, social withdrawal, and impaired concentration and memory retrieval, often with motor slowing.
  • Occurs in a person with known HIV/AIDS; improves with antiretroviral treatment — a potentially treatable dementia.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10 (F02.4)

Dementia developing in the course of HIV disease, in the absence of a concurrent illness or condition other than HIV infection that could explain the clinical features. Typically complaints of forgetfulness, slowness, poor concentration, and difficulty in problem-solving and reading, with apathy and reduced spontaneity.

ICD-11 (6D85.3)

Dementia requirements met, judged to be a direct consequence of HIV infection, once other causes are excluded.

6–7. Remember & treatment

Memory aid

"HIV + subcortical slowing = HIV dementia; treat the HIV."

Treatment: antiretroviral therapy is central and can improve cognition; treat opportunistic infections; symptomatic and psychological support; treat comorbid depression. Kaplan & Sadock

F04 · Organic Amnesic Syndrome → ICD-11 6D72 (Amnestic Disorder)

ICD-10 code F04 · Organic amnesic syndrome, not induced by alcohol and other psychoactive substances
ICD-11 code 6D72 · .0 disease classified elsewhere, .1 psychoactive substances (.10 alcohol, .11 sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics, .12 other, .13 volatile inhalants), .2 unknown aetiology, .Y, .Z
Status Renamed ("organic" dropped)
Key change Substance-induced amnestic states now explicit sub-codes within the same category

1. Introduction

A syndrome of prominent, isolated impairment of memory in which recent and remote memory are affected while immediate recall (the ability to hold information for a few seconds, e.g. repeating a number just heard) is preserved, occurring in the setting of clear consciousness and without a general decline in intellect. It is neither a global dementia (because only memory is affected) nor a delirium (because the person is alert and attentive). The classic form is Korsakoff syndrome (the chronic amnesic state following thiamine deficiency, most often related to chronic alcohol use).

2. Aetiology and Pathophysiology

The syndrome results from bilateral damage to the memory circuits — the hippocampi, mammillary bodies (small paired structures at the base of the brain, part of the memory circuit), the medial thalamus, and the fornix. Causes include: Kaplan & Sadock

  • Thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency — Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome, most often from chronic alcohol misuse with malnutrition (the alcohol form is coded in the substance block in ICD-10 and as 6D72.10 in ICD-11).
  • Hypoxia / anoxia (lack of oxygen) — cardiac arrest, carbon monoxide poisoning (the hippocampus is highly vulnerable).
  • Head injury; herpes simplex encephalitis (damages medial temporal lobes); posterior cerebral artery stroke; rupture of an anterior communicating artery aneurysm; brain tumours affecting the third ventricle region.

3. Epidemiology

Rates depend on the underlying cause. Korsakoff syndrome is the most familiar form and occurs where chronic heavy alcohol use and nutritional deficiency are prevalent. It classically follows an episode of Wernicke encephalopathy, especially if thiamine treatment is delayed.

4. Clinical Features and Symptoms

  • Anterograde amnesia (marked inability to form new memories after the onset of the disorder) — the dominant feature; the person cannot learn new information and forgets events within minutes.
  • Retrograde amnesia (loss of memories laid down before the onset), usually with a temporal gradient (recent past lost more than the distant past).
  • Preserved immediate recall, attention, perception, and general intelligence; clear consciousness.
  • Disturbed sense of time and sequencing of events.

Unique and easily identifiable clues — the classic examination link

  • Alcohol use or withdrawal → thiamine deficiency → Wernicke encephalopathy → Korsakoff syndrome.
  • Wernicke encephalopathy (the acute, reversible emergency preceding Korsakoff) is a triad of confusion, ataxia (unsteady, uncoordinated walking), and ophthalmoplegia / nystagmus (weakness of the eye muscles / rhythmic jerking eye movements). It requires urgent thiamine.
  • Confabulation (unintentionally producing false or distorted memories to fill gaps, without any intention to deceive) is the characteristic give-away of Korsakoff syndrome.
  • Striking, isolated failure of new learning with an otherwise alert, articulate patient of normal general intelligence.

5. Diagnostic Criteria (strictly from ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines

For a definite diagnosis, all of the following are required:

  1. Presence of a memory impairment shown as a defect of recent memory (impaired learning of new material); an anterograde and retrograde amnesia, and a reduced ability to recall past experiences in reverse order of their occurrence.
  2. Absence of a defect in immediate recall (as tested, for example, by the digit span), of disturbance of attention and consciousness, and of global intellectual impairment.
  3. A history or objective evidence of an insult to, or disease of, the brain (especially involving bilaterally the diencephalic and medial temporal structures).

Confabulation, a marked lack of insight, and emotional changes (apathy, lack of initiative) may be additional but not invariable features. (Amnesic states due to alcohol or other psychoactive substances are classified in F10–F19.)

ICD-11 Essential (required) features

  1. Prominent memory impairment relative to expectations for age and general premorbid level of functioning, characterised by significant difficulty acquiring, learning, and remembering new information, sometimes with an inability to recall previously learned information.
  2. The memory impairment is severe enough to result in significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.
  3. The memory impairment occurs in the absence of other significant neurocognitive impairment (i.e. it is not part of a broader dementia) and does not occur exclusively during delirium.
  4. The features are judged to be the direct consequence of an underlying medical condition or the use of a substance/medication (assign the relevant sub-code, e.g. 6D72.10 for alcohol).

6. Easy Way to Remember

Memory aids

Wernicke triad = "CAN" — Confusion, Ataxia, Nystagmus (ophthalmoplegia). Korsakoff = "can't learn new + makes up stories (confabulation) + otherwise sharp." The whole sequence: alcohol → thiamine deficiency → Wernicke → Korsakoff. Treatment reflex: give thiamine before glucose.

7. Treatment

Pharmacological Kaplan & Sadock

  • Urgent parenteral thiamine (vitamin B1 given by injection) whenever Wernicke encephalopathy is suspected — this prevents or limits progression to Korsakoff syndrome and is a medical emergency. Thiamine should be given before or with glucose to avoid precipitating Wernicke.
  • Treat the specific underlying cause (e.g. herpes encephalitis, hypoxic injury); manage alcohol dependence.

Psychological and rehabilitative

  • Compensatory memory strategies (external memory aids such as diaries, alarms, notebooks, and consistent routines).
  • Errorless learning techniques; structured, familiar environment; occupational support; supportive psychotherapy; family education.

Prognosis: depends on cause and timeliness of treatment. Early thiamine can reverse Wernicke encephalopathy; established Korsakoff amnesia is frequently persistent and may be permanent, though some recovery can occur.

ICD-10 code F05 · .0 not superimposed on dementia, .1 superimposed on dementia, .8 other, .9 unspecified
ICD-11 code 6D70 · .0 due to disease classified elsewhere, .1 due to psychoactive substances including medications, .2 due to multiple aetiological factors, .Y other specified, .Z unknown/unspecified

1. Introduction

Delirium is an aetiologically non-specific organic cerebral syndrome (an acute brain dysfunction that can be produced by many different physical causes) characterised by concurrent disturbance of consciousness and attention, perception, thinking, memory, psychomotor behaviour, emotion, and the sleep–wake cycle. It has an acute onset and a fluctuating course, and it is a medical emergency that is usually reversible once the underlying cause is identified and treated. It is common, frequently missed (especially the quiet form), and associated with poor outcomes.

2. Aetiology and Pathophysiology

Delirium arises from a wide range of acute systemic or cerebral insults acting on a vulnerable brain, disrupting widespread neurotransmitter systems (notably a relative cholinergic deficiency and dopaminergic excess) and large-scale attention networks. Common causes (the mnemonic below is useful): Kaplan & Sadock

  • Infection (urinary tract infection, pneumonia, sepsis) — a very common trigger in the elderly.
  • Drugs and toxins — anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, sedatives, corticosteroids, and polypharmacy; alcohol/sedative withdrawal.
  • Metabolic and electrolyte disturbance — hypo/hyperglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypercalcaemia, hepatic or renal failure, dehydration.
  • Hypoxia, cardiac events, and constipation / urinary retention and uncontrolled pain in frail patients.
  • Intracranial causes — stroke, head injury, encephalitis, seizures, raised intracranial pressure.
  • Postoperative states, especially after major or emergency surgery in older patients.

Predisposing (vulnerability) factors: older age, pre-existing dementia, sensory impairment (poor vision/hearing), frailty, multiple medical comorbidities, and previous delirium.

3. Epidemiology

  • Very common in the general hospital: point prevalence in medically ill inpatients is commonly cited around 10–30%, rising much higher (frequently over half) in intensive-care and postoperative elderly patients. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Pooled delirium prevalence around 36% at hospital discharge in older inpatients in a systematic review. Other evidence
  • In general-hospital admissions aged 65+, one large cohort found roughly 17% with delirium alone and about 8% with delirium superimposed on dementia. Other evidence
  • Delirium superimposed on dementia is common (reported ranges of roughly 22–89%, pooled near half). Other evidence
  • Delirium is under-recognised (particularly the hypoactive subtype) and is associated with prolonged admission, increased mortality, functional decline, and an increased risk of subsequently developing dementia. Other evidence

4. Clinical Features and Symptoms

ICD-10 requires symptoms, mild or severe, in each of the following areas: (1) impairment of consciousness and attention; (2) global disturbance of cognition; (3) psychomotor disturbance; (4) disturbance of the sleep–wake cycle; (5) emotional disturbances.

Motor subtypes

  • Hyperactive: agitated, restless, hypervigilant, hallucinating — easily noticed.
  • Hypoactive: drowsy, withdrawn, slowed, quiet — frequently missed and often mistaken for depression; carries a worse prognosis.
  • Mixed: fluctuates between hyperactive and hypoactive.

Unique and easily identifiable clues — "recognise delirium"

  • Impaired attention is the hallmark — a bedside screen is asking the patient to recite the months of the year backwards; a delirious patient cannot sustain it.
  • Acute onset over hours to days with a fluctuating course, characteristically worse in the evening and at night (sundowning (worsening confusion as daylight fades)).
  • Perceptual disturbances are typically visual (in contrast to the predominantly auditory hallucinations of primary psychosis).
  • The hypoactive form is the diagnostic trap — a quiet, drowsy patient wrongly labelled as depressed or simply "tired".
  • Special clue: alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal → delirium tremens (severe withdrawal delirium with tremor, sweating, rapid heart rate, and vivid hallucinations) — the one form of delirium treated with benzodiazepines.

5. Diagnostic Criteria (strictly from ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines

For a definite diagnosis, symptoms, mild or severe, should be present in each one of the following areas:

  1. Impairment of consciousness and attention (on a continuum from clouding to coma; reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain, and shift attention).
  2. Global disturbance of cognition (perceptual distortions, illusions and hallucinations — most often visual; impairment of abstract thinking and comprehension, with or without transient delusions, but typically with some degree of incoherence; impairment of immediate recall and of recent memory but with relatively intact remote memory; disorientation for time as well as, in more severe cases, for place and person).
  3. Psychomotor disturbances (hypo- or hyperactivity and unpredictable shifts from one to the other; increased reaction time; increased or decreased flow of speech; enhanced startle reaction).
  4. Disturbance of the sleep–wake cycle (insomnia or, in severe cases, total sleep loss or reversal of the sleep–wake cycle; daytime drowsiness; nocturnal worsening of symptoms; disturbing dreams or nightmares which may continue as hallucinations after awakening).
  5. Emotional disturbances (depression, anxiety or fear, irritability, euphoria, apathy, or wondering perplexity).

The onset is usually rapid, the course diurnally fluctuating, and the total duration of the condition is less than 6 months. Subtypes: F05.0 not superimposed on dementia; F05.1 superimposed on dementia; F05.8 other (e.g. of mixed origin, subacute confusional state); F05.9 unspecified.

ICD-11 Essential (required) features

  1. Disturbance of attention (reduced ability to direct, focus, sustain, and shift attention) and awareness (reduced orientation to the environment).
  2. The disturbance develops over a short period of time (usually hours to a few days), represents a change from the individual's baseline level of functioning, and tends to fluctuate in severity over the course of a day.
  3. The disturbance is accompanied by impairment in other cognitive domains (e.g. memory, language, visuospatial ability, or perception) and may include disturbance of emotion, behaviour, and the sleep–wake cycle.
  4. The symptoms are not better accounted for by another pre-existing, established, or evolving neurocognitive disorder, and do not occur in the context of severely reduced arousal such as coma.
  5. There is evidence that the disturbance is a direct consequence of a medical condition, a substance or medication, or multiple aetiological factors (assign the relevant sub-code). Delirium may be superimposed on dementia (in which case both are coded).

6. Easy Way to Remember

Memory aids

Delirium recognition — "acute, fluctuating, inattentive, in a sick patient." Causes screen — "I WATCH DEATH": Infection, Withdrawal, Acute metabolic, Trauma, CNS pathology, Hypoxia, Deficiencies, Endocrine, Acute vascular, Toxins/drugs, Heavy metals.

Clinical signature: sudden + fluctuating + inattention + altered awareness + an identifiable acute medical trigger = delirium (an organic emergency, not "just confusion").

7. Treatment

Identify and treat the cause (definitive)

The essential treatment is to find and correct the underlying cause — treat infection, correct electrolytes and oxygenation, review and stop offending medications, relieve pain, urinary retention, and constipation.

Non-pharmacological (first-line for the delirium itself)

  • Reorientation (clocks, calendars, familiar faces), good daytime lighting and quiet nights, day–night cues.
  • Ensure sensory aids (glasses, hearing aids); maintain hydration and nutrition; encourage early mobilisation; promote normal sleep.
  • Avoid physical restraints and deliriogenic drugs; involve family; provide a calm, consistent environment.

Pharmacological (reserved) Kaplan & Sadock

  • Medication is only for severe agitation, distress, or danger: a low dose of an antipsychotic (e.g. haloperidol or an atypical agent) for the shortest necessary time.
  • Benzodiazepines are first-line only for alcohol or sedative withdrawal delirium (delirium tremens); in other deliria they can worsen or prolong the condition.
  • Avoid antipsychotics in suspected Lewy body dementia (neuroleptic sensitivity).

Prognosis: often reversible with prompt treatment, but associated with longer hospital stays, higher mortality, functional decline, and increased risk of later dementia. Hypoactive delirium and delirium superimposed on dementia have worse outcomes. Other evidence

F06 · Other Mental Disorders due to Brain Damage/Dysfunction and Physical Disease → ICD-11 6E6x and 6D71

ICD-10 code F06 · .0 hallucinosis, .1 catatonic, .2 delusional, .3 mood (.30–.33), .4 anxiety, .5 dissociative, .6 emotionally labile, .7 mild cognitive
ICD-11 codes Secondary syndromes: 6E61 psychotic, 6E62 mood, 6E63 anxiety, 6E65 dissociative, 6E67 catatonia; plus 6D71 mild neurocognitive disorder
Status Moved / reorganised
Key change "Symptomatic" psychiatric syndromes become "secondary syndromes"; mild cognitive disorder becomes a neurocognitive category

1. Introduction

This category covers presentations in which the prominent feature is a psychiatric symptom cluster (hallucinations, delusions, mood change, anxiety, catatonia, dissociation) that is caused by a brain disease or systemic physical disease, with cognition relatively preserved — that is, the symptoms are secondary to a demonstrable medical condition rather than being a primary psychiatric disorder. ICD-11 reframes these as secondary syndromes (psychiatric syndromes that are the direct physiological consequence of another medical condition) and places them in a dedicated grouping (6E60–6E69), each mirroring its primary counterpart (e.g. secondary psychotic syndrome parallels schizophrenia). F06.7 (mild cognitive disorder) is reclassified as a neurocognitive disorder (6D71).

2. Aetiology

The same broad range of causes as the general aetiological framework (brain tumours, epilepsy, stroke, encephalitis, endocrine disease such as thyroid or cortisol disorders, autoimmune disease, and medications such as corticosteroids and dopaminergic drugs) — but here the medical cause produces a symptom syndrome rather than a global cognitive syndrome. Kaplan & Sadock

3. Epidemiology

Common among medically ill and neurological patients but frequently under-recognised as "secondary" rather than "primary" psychiatric disorders. Mild neurocognitive disorder (6D71) is common in older adults; a proportion progress to dementia, some remain stable, and some reverse.

Master rule — when to suspect a secondary (organic) cause instead of a primary psychiatric disorder

  • Atypical age of onset (e.g. a first episode of psychosis or mania in an older adult).
  • Prominent visual hallucinations (rather than auditory).
  • Symptoms beginning in close temporal relationship to a medical illness, a new medication, or a metabolic derangement.
  • Abnormal physical examination or investigations, fluctuating cognition, or poor response to standard psychiatric treatment.

5. Diagnostic Criteria (strictly from ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10 General guidelines for F06

  1. Evidence of cerebral disease, damage, or dysfunction, or of systemic physical disease known to be associated with one of the listed syndromes.
  2. A temporal relationship (weeks or a few months) between the development of the underlying disease and the onset of the syndrome.
  3. Recovery from the mental disorder following removal or improvement of the underlying presumed cause.
  4. Absence of evidence to suggest an alternative cause of the mental syndrome (such as a strong family history or precipitating stress).

If conditions 1, 2, and 4 are met, a provisional diagnosis is justified; if condition 3 is also met, the diagnosis is certain.

ICD-11 Essential features (secondary syndromes, 6E6x)

  1. The presentation is characterised by symptoms typical of the corresponding primary disorder (e.g. psychotic symptoms for 6E61, mood symptoms for 6E62, anxiety symptoms for 6E63).
  2. There is evidence from history, examination, or laboratory findings that the syndrome is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition or substance/medication not classified under mental and behavioural disorders.
  3. The symptoms are not better accounted for by a primary mental disorder (e.g. a primary psychotic or mood disorder) and do not occur exclusively during delirium.
  4. The symptoms are sufficiently severe to warrant specific clinical attention. The secondary-syndrome code is assigned together with the code for the underlying medical condition (order not meaningful).

Mild neurocognitive disorder (6D71): objectively verified mild decline from a previous level in one or more cognitive domains, that is not severe enough to significantly interfere with independence in everyday activities, and not occurring exclusively during delirium or better explained by another mental disorder.

6. Easy Way to Remember

Memory aid

"Right symptom, wrong story" — a textbook psychiatric picture appearing out of context (wrong age, visual hallucinations, tied to an illness or drug) signals a secondary syndrome. Mild neurocognitive disorder = "slipping, but still independent."

7. Treatment

  • Pharmacological: the priority is to treat the underlying medical cause (e.g. correct thyroid dysfunction, withdraw the offending drug), which often resolves the syndrome; add symptomatic treatment as needed — antidepressants for secondary depression, cautious antipsychotics for secondary psychosis, benzodiazepines (and consider electroconvulsive therapy) for catatonia. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Psychological: psychoeducation, supportive psychotherapy; for mild neurocognitive disorder — cognitive strategies, vascular risk-factor control, and monitoring for progression to dementia.
  • Prognosis: frequently improves or reverses when the cause is treated; mild neurocognitive disorder has a variable trajectory.

F07 · Personality and Behavioural Disorders due to Brain Disease, Damage and Dysfunction → ICD-11 6E68

ICD-10 code F07 · .0 organic personality disorder, .1 postencephalitic syndrome, .2 postconcussional syndrome, .8/.9
ICD-11 code 6E68 Secondary personality change (postencephalitic and postconcussional syndromes have no exact 1:1 equivalent)
Status Moved / renamed / partly no equivalent
Key change Organic personality disorder becomes "secondary personality change"; the two post-injury syndromes lack a direct ICD-11 counterpart

1. Introduction

This category covers a significant alteration of the habitual pattern of premorbid behaviour following brain disease, damage, or dysfunction. The change particularly affects the expression of emotions, needs, and impulses. Cognition may be largely preserved, so the person can appear intellectually intact while being behaviourally transformed. The classic cause is frontal-lobe damage.

2. Aetiology

Frontal-lobe head injury, frontal or other brain tumours, stroke, encephalitis, epilepsy, and other frontal-system diseases. Kaplan & Sadock

3. Epidemiology

Common after significant traumatic brain injury and frontal pathology; a frequent reason families seek help ("he/she is not the same person").

4. Clinical Features and Symptoms

Unique and easily identifiable clues (organic personality disorder, F07.0 / 6E68)

  • A clear change from the person's previous baseline personality following a brain insult — not a lifelong pattern (which would indicate a primary personality disorder).
  • Disinhibition (loss of social restraint — tactless, impulsive, or sexually/socially inappropriate behaviour) and/or apathy (marked loss of drive and motivation).
  • Reduced ability to persevere with goal-directed activity; shallow or labile emotions; impaired judgement.
  • Relatively preserved memory and general cognition with prominent executive dysfunction (impaired planning, self-monitoring, and self-control) — "same intellect, different person".

Postencephalitic syndrome (F07.1): residual, often reversible behavioural change after recovery from viral or bacterial encephalitis — general malaise, apathy, irritability, some cognitive difficulty, altered sleep and appetite, and changes in sexual behaviour and social judgement.

Postconcussional syndrome (F07.2): occurs after head trauma (usually severe enough to cause loss of consciousness) — headache, dizziness, fatigue, irritability, difficulty concentrating and performing mental tasks, memory complaints, insomnia, and reduced tolerance of stress, emotional excitement, or alcohol.

5. Diagnostic Criteria (strictly from ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10 (F07.0 Organic personality disorder)

In addition to an established history or other evidence of brain disease, damage, or dysfunction, a definite diagnosis requires two or more of the following features:

  1. Consistently reduced ability to persevere with goal-directed activities, especially ones involving longer periods and postponed gratification.
  2. Altered emotional behaviour, characterised by emotional lability, shallow and unwarranted cheerfulness (euphoria, inappropriate jocularity), and easy change to irritability or short-lived outbursts of anger and aggression; in some instances apathy may be the more prominent feature.
  3. Expression of needs and impulses without consideration of consequences or social convention (antisocial acts such as stealing, inappropriate sexual advances, voracious eating, or extreme disregard for personal hygiene).
  4. Cognitive disturbances, typically in the form of suspiciousness or paranoid ideation, and/or excessive preoccupation with a single, usually abstract, theme (e.g. religion, "right and wrong").
  5. Marked alteration of the rate and flow of language, with features such as circumstantiality, over-inclusiveness, viscosity, and hypergraphia.
  6. Altered sexual behaviour (hyposexuality or change of sexual preference).

ICD-11 (6E68 Secondary personality change)

  1. A persistent personality disturbance that represents a change from the individual's previous characteristic personality pattern (in individuals for whom the personality was previously established, or an alteration in the developmental trajectory in children).
  2. There is evidence that the personality change is the direct pathophysiological consequence of a medical condition not classified under mental and behavioural disorders.
  3. The disturbance is not better accounted for by another mental disorder and does not occur exclusively during delirium. It is coded together with the underlying medical condition.

Postencephalitic and postconcussional syndromes: there is no exact one-to-one ICD-11 equivalent; the post-concussional picture is primarily addressed within the chapter on diseases of the nervous system, with secondary mental/behavioural syndromes coded in addition where specific psychiatric features are present.

6. Easy Way to Remember

Memory aid

"Same brain-power, different person" — a change from baseline personality after a frontal-lobe insult, with disinhibition or apathy and preserved memory. Contrast: a primary personality disorder is lifelong with no discrete brain insult.

7. Treatment

  • Pharmacological: treat the underlying condition; symptomatic medication (e.g. mood stabilisers or SSRIs) may be used cautiously for aggression, impulsivity, or lability. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Psychological: behavioural management programmes, structured and predictable environments, family psychoeducation and support, and neurorehabilitation (structured retraining and support after brain injury).

F09 · Unspecified Organic or Symptomatic Mental Disorder → ICD-11 6E6Z

ICD-10 code F09
ICD-11 code 6E6Z Secondary mental or behavioural syndrome, unspecified
Status Retained (residual category)
Key change Functionally equivalent placeholder

1. Introduction and use

Used when there is evidence of a mental disorder attributable to a cerebral or systemic condition, but the presentation cannot be assigned to a more specific category within this block. It is a residual placeholder pending clarification.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10 (F09)

A category for an organic or symptomatic mental disorder that meets the general criteria for an aetiological (organic) basis but cannot be allocated to any of the specific categories F00–F07. Includes "organic psychosis not otherwise specified" and "symptomatic psychosis not otherwise specified".

ICD-11 (6E6Z)

A secondary mental or behavioural syndrome is judged present (attributable to a medical condition) but is unspecified.

7. Treatment

Identify and treat the underlying cause; provide symptomatic and supportive care while investigation continues.
