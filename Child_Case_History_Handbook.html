⚑ Clinical disclaimer This handbook is an educational clinical-interviewing reference. Questions should be selected according to the child's age, presenting problem, cultural context, developmental level, and clinical need. Not every question needs to be asked in every case. No diagnosis should be made from a single symptom. Developmental norms are guides, not absolute cut-offs. Sensitive topics (abuse, self-harm, sexuality, substance use) must be approached only in a safe, private, confidential and developmentally suitable manner.

iHow to use this handbook

This is a practical interview companion, not a textbook chapter. It follows the Clinical Case History – Child proforma section-by-section, and for the important domains gives you the five working layers:

NormalPositiveNegative ConcerningUncertainNot applicableInconsistent

Every question card shows:

  • Ask — the exact natural question you can say aloud.
  • Who? — parent / caregiver / child / teacher / multiple informants.
  • Possible answers — realistic replies, tagged so you can see the range (a concerning answer is only one of several possibilities).
  • Follow-up (Ask next) — branching probes for a positive answer.
  • Why it matters — the clinical significance.
  • Documentation — how to convert the reply into professional case-history language.
Reading the tagsThe answer tags (Normal / Concerning / Uncertain etc.) are there to protect you from bias: a positive-sounding reply is not proof of a disorder, and a single concerning reply is a lead to probe — not a conclusion.

Avoid leading questions

Poor (leading / loaded)Better (open, then probe)
"Does your child have ADHD?""Tell me about your child's attention and activity level." → then probe distractibility, sitting still, waiting turn.
"He's autistic, isn't he?""How does he get along with other children? How does he play?"
"You must be very strict at home?""How do you usually respond when he doesn't listen?"
"She's just lazy at studies, right?""How does she manage reading, writing and maths compared with the rest of the class?"

Family interview technique

  • Rapport & normalization — "Many parents notice these things; you've done well to bring him."
  • Non-judgmental language — avoid blame; ask "what happens" not "what did you do wrong".
  • Check multiple sources — parent, other caregiver, teacher, child, and your own observation.
  • Summarize & confirm — "So the main concern is… since about two years — have I understood correctly?"

Child rapport by age

StageApproachOpening examples
PreschoolSimple language, play materials, concrete questions; rely mostly on caregiver."Shall we draw something together?" "Which is your favourite toy?"
School-ageDirect questions with examples; child report + caregiver report."What do you like doing after school?" "Who do you sit with in class?"
AdolescentDirect, respectful; offer privacy for sensitive topics; assess mood, self-harm, peers, substances, safety."What's going well for you right now, and what's hard?"
What NOT to ask
  • Leading ("He gets very angry, doesn't he?")
  • Accusatory ("Why do you let him behave like that?")
  • Judgmental / shaming the parent or child
  • Unnecessarily repetitive questions the informant already answered
  • Developmentally inappropriate abstract questions to a young child
  • Diagnostically loaded ("So he's basically bipolar?")

1Socio-Demographic Data

Recorded first, exactly as the proforma lists it. Ask factually; document neutrally. Age, gender and educational/schooling details are documented as they present in the clinical setting.

Basic identification (name, age, DOB, gender)Parent / records
Ask"Can you tell me the child's full name, date of birth and age? And how is the child registered here — as a boy or a girl?"
Possible answers
  • Rahul, born 12 March 2016, 8 years old, boy.Clear
  • We're not sure of the exact date — around 2017.Uncertain
  • The school records one age, the birth certificate another.Inconsistent
DocumentationMaster R., an 8-year-old boy (DOB 12/03/2016), was brought for assessment. If age is uncertain: "Exact date of birth could not be confirmed by the informant; approximate age 7–8 years."
Schooling, class & medium of instructionParent / child
Ask"Which school does the child attend, in which class, and what is the medium of instruction?"
Possible answers
  • Class 3, Hindi-medium government school.Normal
  • He is not enrolled in any school.N/A
  • He was in Class 2 but stopped last year.Concerning
Why it mattersEducational status, class and medium frame the school and learning history; discontinuation is itself a lead to explore under Schooling History.
DocumentationThe child studies in Class 3 in a Hindi-medium government school.
Socio-economic status, residence, referralParent
Ask"Where does the family live? What does the family do for a living? Who suggested you come here today?"
Possible answers
  • Urban, father is a shopkeeper; referred by the school counsellor.Clear
  • Rural, daily-wage family; came on their own after neighbours' advice.Clear
  • Referred by paediatrician for suspected developmental delay.Referral note
DocumentationThe family belongs to a lower-middle socio-economic background and resides in an urban area. The child was referred by the school for behavioural concerns.
Also record on this pageUHID No., Aadhar No., Mob. No., Date of Admission, Trainee status, "Case worked up by", and "Reliable & Adequacy" — the latter is elaborated under the Informant section below.

2Informant & reliability

The proforma records the informant's name, relationship, duration of acquaintance and whether information is reliable and adequate. Begin history-taking here.

Who is the informant & how well do they know the child?Accompanying adult
Ask"Who has come with the child today? What is your relationship? Do you live with the child, and how much time do you spend with him each day? Is there anyone else who could add to this history?"
Possible answers
  • I'm his mother, I'm with him all day.Primary caregiver
  • I'm his father; his mother knows the early history better.Partial
  • Grandmother — she's raised him since infancy.Primary caregiver
  • Older sister; parents are working, she brings him.Limited on early history
  • I only started living with him two years ago.Limited
Why it mattersReliability of the whole history depends on the informant's contact, memory and relationship. Corroboration (a second caregiver or the teacher) strengthens the account.
Documentation (reliable)Information was provided by the mother, who is the primary caregiver and lives with the child; the information was considered reliable and adequate.
Documentation (limited)Information was provided by the father, who was unable to give details of early development as this was primarily managed by the mother; the account was adequate for the present concerns but limited regarding early history.
Documenting reliability without over-judging State the basis for the judgment, not just the label. Prefer "considered reliable and adequate, as the informant is the primary caregiver with continuous contact" over a bare "reliable". If two informants disagree, record both accounts (see §23) rather than choosing one without evidence.
Primary source: Child Proforma — Informants / Reliable & Adequacy Documentation style: Psychodiagnostic Cases Submission

3Chief Complaints

The proforma provides 6 numbered lines plus "Reason for Consultation". Complaints should be concise, symptom-focused, with duration, in the informant's frame, in chronological order — not converted into diagnostic labels.

Eliciting the complaintsParent (then child)
Ask"What brought you here today? What problems have you noticed in the child? Which one concerns you the most? When did you first notice it, and how long has it been present?"
Possible answers (parent's words)
  • He doesn't sit in one place and keeps running around.Symptom report
  • She hardly speaks and doesn't play with other children.Symptom report
  • His marks have dropped and the teacher complains.Symptom report
  • Honestly, the school told us to come; we don't see much of a problem.Discordant
Convert to concise complaints (with duration)
  • "Doesn't sit in one place / keeps running" → Excessive activity — 2 years
  • "Hardly speaks, doesn't play with others" → Reduced speech and social interaction — since early childhood
  • "Marks dropped, teacher complains" → Decline in academic performance — 1 year
DocumentationChief complaints (as reported by the mother):
1. Excessive activity — 2 years
2. Difficulty sustaining attention — 2 years
3. Not following instructions — 1½ years.
Reason for consultation: brought for assessment of overactivity and inattention on the school's advice.
Keep it as report, not diagnosisWrite "excessive activity", not "ADHD"; write "repetitive hand movements", not "stereotypies of autism". Diagnostic terms belong in the formulation, after the whole history.
Primary source: Child Proforma — Chief Complaints / Reason for Consultation

4History of Present Illness (HOPI)

The proforma structures HOPI as Total Duration · Onset (abrupt/acute/subacute/insidious) · Course (episodic/chronic/chronic-fluctuating) · Progress (static/deteriorating/improved) · Predisposing · Precipitating · Perpetuating factors · Biological functioning · Occupational functioning. Open the narrative with the classic anchor.

Opening line"The index child was apparently well until … when the informant first noticed …"

HOPI question bank (open → probe)

Onset, course & progressionParent
Ask (open)"What was the first change you noticed? How old was the child then? Did it start suddenly or gradually? Since then, has it stayed the same, worsened, or improved?"
Probes
  • "What was happening in the child's life around that time?" (precipitant)
  • "Does it come and go, or is it there all the time?" (course)
  • "What makes it better or worse?" (maintaining factors)
  • "Where does it happen — home, school, or both?" (situational variation)
  • "How is it affecting his studies, friendships and daily routine?" (functional impact)
  • "Have you consulted anyone before? What was tried and did it help?" (previous treatment / response)
  • "Why did you decide to seek help now?" (reason now)
Possible answers
  • Gradual, over about two years, slowly getting worse.Insidious, deteriorating
  • Started after he changed school; comes in episodes.Precipitant + episodic
  • Present since he was a toddler; unchanged.Chronic, static
DocumentationTotal duration ~2 years. Onset insidious; course chronic; progress gradually deteriorating. Symptoms are present across home and school. Precipitating factor: none clearly identified. Perpetuating factors: inconsistent limit-setting and academic pressure. No prior consultation.
Biological & occupational functioning within HOPIParent
Ask"Since the problem began, how has his sleep, appetite, self-care, and bowel/bladder control been? Has school attendance or routine been affected?"
Possible answers
  • Sleep and appetite normal; self-care age-appropriate.Preserved
  • Sleep disturbed, appetite reduced since it started.Affected
  • Started wetting the bed again after being dry.Regression
DocumentationBiological functions: sleep and appetite reported adequate; self-care age-appropriate; bowel and bladder control maintained. Occupational (scholastic) functioning impaired, with frequent teacher complaints and declining marks over the past year.
Symptom-by-symptom probe template (use for each complaint) When? · How often? · What happens immediately before? · During? · Afterward? · Home / school / both? · Better or worse with anything? · How does the child behave afterward?
Primary source: Child Proforma — History of Present Illness Documentation style: Psychodiagnostic Cases Submission (Onset/Course/Progress/Precipitating/Perpetuating)

5Psychiatric symptom probing — domain-wise

Screen each domain with an open question, then use the probes. A positive screen is a lead to explore, never a diagnosis. Use developmentally appropriate wording.

Attention / inattention

AttentionParent + Teacher
Ask"Tell me about his ability to pay attention. Does he finish what he starts? Does he lose or forget things?"
Probesdistractibility · sustaining attention on tasks/play · careless mistakes · seems not to listen when spoken to · task completion · loses belongings · everyday forgetfulness · avoids sustained mental effort.
Possible answers
  • He concentrates well on things he enjoys and finishes homework.Normal
  • Even after telling him 2–3 times, he forgets what to do.Concerning
  • He's fine at home but the teacher says he drifts off in class.Setting-specific
Ask next (if positive)Is it in one setting or both? Since when? Has it always been this way or is it new? Does it improve with one-to-one attention? Is it worse when the task is boring vs interesting?
DocumentationCaregiver reported difficulty following and retaining multi-step instructions across home and school, with frequent forgetfulness and incomplete tasks.

Hyperactivity & impulsivity

Hyperactivity / impulsivityParent + Teacher
Ask"How active is he compared with other children his age? Can he sit through a meal or a class? Does he wait for his turn?"
ProbesHyperactivity: fidgeting · leaving seat · running/climbing when inappropriate · always "on the go" · excessive talking · difficulty playing quietly. Impulsivity: interrupts · answers before question finishes · difficulty waiting · acts without thinking.
Possible answers
  • Active but settles when needed.Normal
  • Can't sit for a meal, climbs on furniture, always running.Concerning
DocumentationReported motor overactivity with difficulty remaining seated, and impulsivity in the form of interrupting and difficulty awaiting turn, present across settings.

Oppositional behaviour & temper

Oppositional / defiant behaviourParent
Ask"How does he respond when asked to do something he doesn't want to? Tell me about his temper."
Probesarguing with adults · defiance / refusing rules · temper outbursts · deliberately annoying others · blaming others · touchy / easily annoyed · resentful · spiteful.
Ask next — "Does he have temper tantrums?" (if YES)How often? How long do they last? What triggers them? What does he do during (hurt self / hurt others / break things)? How do parents respond? Does he calm down on his own? Does it happen at school too? How does he behave afterward?
Clinical meaningFrequency, duration, triggers and whether behaviour is directed/vindictive help distinguish age-typical tantrums from oppositional defiant patterns and from conduct problems.
DocumentationCaregiver reported frequent temper outbursts (3–4/week, lasting 10–20 min), typically triggered by demands, without deliberate harm to others; the child settles with time. No cruelty or destructiveness reported.

Conduct symptoms

ConductParent + Teacher
Ask"Have there been any problems with rules — things like fighting, taking things that aren't his, or staying away from home or school?"
Probes (screen sensitively)aggression to people/animals · cruelty · stealing · lying/conning · destroying property · truancy · running away · bullying · use of weapons · fire-setting · serious rule violations.
Possible answers
  • No such problems.Negative
  • Occasional lies to avoid scolding.Ambiguous / age-typical
  • Repeated stealing, bunking school, hurt a younger child.Concerning
Documentation (if present)Reported repetitive rule-violating behaviour including truancy and taking others' belongings over the past year. (If genuinely assessed and absent: No history suggestive of aggression, cruelty, stealing, or truancy.)

Anxiety

AnxietyParent + Child
Ask (child)"Is there anything that makes you feel scared or worried a lot?"
Ask (parent): "Does he worry more than other children? How does he manage being away from you?"
Probesexcessive worries · separation anxiety · school refusal · specific fears · somatic complaints (tummy ache, headache before school) · repeated reassurance-seeking · panic-like episodes.
Possible answers
  • Normal shyness that settles.Normal
  • Cries and clings every morning before school; complains of stomach ache.Concerning
DocumentationReported separation-related distress with morning somatic complaints and reluctance to attend school over the past three months.

Depression (developmentally worded)

Depression / low moodChild + Parent
Ask (child)"How have you been feeling — happy, okay, or sad most days?" "Are the things you used to enjoy still fun?"
Probessadness or irritability · loss of interest · reduced activity/play · guilt · hopelessness · sleep & appetite change · fatigue · poor concentration · thoughts of death or self-harm (ask directly and calmly — see §21).
Possible answers
  • Usually cheerful.Normal
  • Irritable, stopped playing, says "nobody likes me".Concerning
DocumentationChild reported persistent low mood and irritability with reduced interest in play over the past two months; no active suicidal ideation elicited on direct enquiry.

Obsessive–compulsive symptoms

Obsessions & compulsionsChild + Parent
Ask"Do thoughts ever get stuck in your mind that you can't push away? Do you feel you must do certain things a special way or something bad will happen?"
ProbesObsessions: unwanted thoughts · fears · doubts · contamination · symmetry. Compulsions: washing · checking · counting · repeating · arranging · reassurance-seeking. Ask about time spent, distress, and interference.
DistinguishBedtime rituals / lining up toys ≠ automatically OCD. Developmentally normal routines are flexible and not distressing; clinical compulsions are rigid, time-consuming and cause distress when prevented.
DocumentationReported repetitive hand-washing associated with contamination fears, occupying >1 hour/day and causing distress when interrupted.

Psychotic-spectrum phenomena (age-appropriate)

Perceptual / unusual experiencesChild + Parent
Ask (gently)"Sometimes children hear or see things that others don't — has that ever happened to you?" "Do you ever feel that others are against you?"
Probeshearing voices · seeing things others don't · unusual/fixed beliefs · suspiciousness · bizarre behaviour · self-talk beyond age norm.
Distinguish imagination from psychosisImaginary friends and vivid pretend play in young children ≠ psychosis. Normal imaginative phenomena are under the child's control, ego-syntonic and age-typical; genuine hallucinations/delusions are distressing, not controllable, and often accompanied by functional decline. Interpret cautiously and never from a single item.
DocumentationOn sensitive enquiry, no clear history suggestive of hallucinations or delusional beliefs was elicited; reported "imaginary friend" appeared developmentally typical and ego-syntonic.
Structure: standard child psychiatric symptom review (integrated with Nitin Sethi psychiatric history-taking framework) Terminology anchored to Child Proforma & ICD-10/ICD-11 (repo copies)

6Family History

The proforma asks for a genogram, family history of medical/psychiatric illness, living arrangements, primary caregiver, description of parents, home & neighbourhood, family life & relationships, and child-rearing practices. Ask sensitively and without blame.

Possible answers & how to document each
  • He developed normally. → "Milestones reported to be age-appropriate across domains."Normal
  • He started walking at around 13 months. → "Independent walking attained at ~13 months (within normal range)."Normal
  • He was late in speaking. → "Language milestones reported delayed; first meaningful words after 2½ years."Delay
  • He still needs help with dressing. → "Adaptive self-help skill (dressing) not yet independently attained."Delay
  • I'm not sure about the exact age. → "Informant was unable to recall the exact ages of attainment."Uncertain
Four things to keep separate when documenting
  • Delayed milestone — skill attained, but later than typical.
  • Skill never acquired — the child has not attained it at all.
  • Regression — a skill was clearly acquired and then lost.
  • Loss of a previously acquired skill — the specific event that defines regression.
Regression ≠ developmental delay. Document exactly which one the history supports.

Developmental regression (ask explicitly)

Loss of previously acquired skillsParent
Ask"Did the child ever learn a skill and then lose it — words he used to say, ways he used to play or interact, being toilet-trained, or things he had learnt at school?"
Probeslanguage loss · social loss · motor loss · toileting loss · academic regression · behavioural regression. Establish age, speed, and any illness/seizure around that time.
Why this needs careful assessmentGenuine loss of previously acquired skills is a red flag that warrants prompt medical/neurological evaluation. Document the specific skill, the age it was present, and when it was lost.
DocumentationThe informant reported that the child had acquired ~10 meaningful words by 18 months and subsequently lost expressive speech by ~24 months, with reduced social responsiveness — suggestive of regression requiring further evaluation.

23Negative, unknown & inconsistent history

Writing negative history correctly

RuleRecord a negative finding only when it was actually assessed. Do not auto-generate long lists of negatives.

Example (assessed and absent): "There was no history suggestive of seizures, loss of consciousness, or significant head injury." The Child Proforma provides a dedicated Negative History block for exactly these points, mirroring the style used in the psychodiagnostic case submissions.

Handling unknown information

SituationStandard wording
Informant cannot recall"Informant was unable to recall the exact age."
Details not obtainable"Details were not available."
Nothing relevant elicited"No relevant history could be elicited."
Informant lacks the information"Information was unavailable from the accompanying informant."
"No history" vs "History unknown"No history = the item was assessed and is genuinely absent. History unknown = the item could not be assessed / the informant did not know. These are not interchangeable — never convert "unknown" into "absent".

Handling inconsistent information

Record both accounts and their sources without resolving the contradiction unless you have evidence: "Caregiver A reported X, whereas caregiver B reported Y."

26Differential-diagnosis questioning

Targeted questions that help separate look-alike presentations. Use to guide, not to conclude.

DistinctionDiscriminating questions
ADHD vs anxietyIs inattention pervasive, or only when worried? Are there specific fears/worries driving distraction?
ADHD vs autismAlongside overactivity, is there qualitative social-communication difficulty, restricted interests, or rigidity?
ADHD vs specific learning disorderIs the difficulty global (attention everywhere) or confined to specific academic skills (reading/maths)?
ADHD vs sleep disorderHow is sleep? Could daytime inattention/overactivity follow poor or fragmented sleep?
ADHD vs intellectual disabilityAre difficulties selective to attention, or is there global delay across development and adaptive skills?
Autism vs developmental language disorderIs difficulty limited to language, or also social reciprocity, joint attention, and play?
Autism vs social anxietyDoes the child want to interact but fears judgment (anxiety), or lack social interest/reciprocity (autism)?
Specific learning disorder vs inadequate schoolingHas schooling been regular and adequate? Language of instruction understood?
Depression vs normal adolescent mood variationDuration, pervasiveness, loss of interest, functional impact, and any self-harm thoughts?
Oppositional behaviour vs conduct disorderArgumentative/defiant only, or violation of others' rights (aggression, stealing, truancy)?
OCD vs normal ritualsAre routines flexible & non-distressing, or rigid, time-consuming & distressing when prevented?
Psychosis vs imaginationIs the experience controllable, ego-syntonic & age-typical, or distressing, uncontrollable, with functional decline?
Seizure vs behavioural episodeResponsiveness during the event? Stereotyped? Post-event confusion? Triggers?
Dissociation vs seizureSituational triggers, variability, and preserved responses vs stereotyped ictal features (consider EEG).
Trauma-related vs primary disorderDid symptoms follow a specific adverse event, with re-experiencing/avoidance/hyperarousal?

Global developmental delay · intellectual developmental disorder · autism spectrum disorder · developmental language disorder · ADHD · specific learning disorder · motor disorders · communication disorders. 29Three worked fictional cases

Illustrative only — no real patient data. Each moves from raw interview answers → organized history → MSE → assessment → formulation.

Case 1 — Neurodevelopmental presentation (fictional): "Aarav", 5 years

Raw answers: Mother: "He doesn't talk much, only a few words. He doesn't look at us when we call, plays alone lining up his cars for hours, gets very upset if the routine changes." Milestones: walked at 14 months; said 5–6 words by 18 months then spoke less; no pointing to show things.

Organized history: Chief complaints — reduced speech (since ~2 yr), poor social response (since ~2 yr), repetitive play. HOPI — insidious onset, chronic, static-to-slowly-progressive; concern for early expressive-language regression around 18–24 months. Milestones — motor age-appropriate; language delayed with reported loss; social milestones (joint attention, pointing) not attained. No seizures reported.

MSE: poor eye contact, limited response to name, minimal spontaneous communication, repetitive lining-up play, distress at interruption; affect difficult to engage.

Assessment / formulation: Pervasive difficulties in social communication with restricted/repetitive behaviour and reported early language loss — hypotheses centre on an autism spectrum presentation; differentials include developmental language disorder and global developmental delay. Reported regression warrants neurodevelopmental/neurological evaluation. Provisional (Axis 1) pending formal assessment (e.g. developmental/autism-specific tools). No single feature is diagnostic on its own.

Case 2 — Emotional / behavioural presentation (fictional): "Kabir", 9 years

Raw answers: Father: "He can't sit still, forgets instructions, blurts out answers, and the teacher says he's out of his seat constantly. At home he argues and has tantrums." Teacher's note: inattentive, disruptive, but no cruelty or stealing. Present since Class 1 (~3 yr), across home and school.

Organized history: Chief complaints — overactivity, inattention, impulsivity (~3 yr), plus argumentativeness/temper. HOPI — insidious, chronic, pervasive across settings; sleep/appetite normal; scholastic functioning impaired. No mood or perceptual symptoms. No trauma. FH: paternal uncle "restless as a child".

MSE: fidgety, left seat repeatedly, interrupted, attention poorly sustained on tasks; mood euthymic; no thought/perceptual abnormality.

Assessment / formulation: Pervasive inattention–overactivity–impulsivity with cross-setting impairment suggests a hyperkinetic / ADHD hypothesis; the oppositional/temper features are noted but do not meet a conduct pattern (no rights-violating behaviour). Differentials: anxiety-driven inattention, learning disorder, sleep problems — screened and less likely. Multi-axial: Axis 1 provisional hyperkinetic disorder; Axis 5 note academic pressure. Confirm with rating scales (parent + teacher) and cognitive assessment.

Case 3 — Learning / academic presentation (fictional): "Meera", 10 years

Raw answers: Mother: "She's bright and chatty but just can't read well — mixes up letters, reads very slowly, spells poorly. Maths and everything else is fine." Teacher: reading & spelling well below class; attention and behaviour fine; regular schooling in her first language.

Organized history: Chief complaints — difficulty reading and spelling (since Class 1). HOPI — persistent, specific to literacy; no global delay; milestones age-appropriate; good social/adaptive skills; no inattention across settings; hearing/vision normal; schooling regular and adequate.

MSE: alert, articulate, cooperative; attention adequate; on reading tasks — slow decoding, letter confusions; other cognition grossly intact.

Assessment / formulation: An isolated, persistent difficulty in reading/spelling with otherwise intact development and adequate schooling points to a specific learning disorder (reading) hypothesis. Differentials ruled out: inadequate schooling, sensory impairment, intellectual disability, ADHD. Multi-axial: Axis 2 specific reading disorder; average intellectual level (Axis 3) to be confirmed on formal psychoeducational assessment. Recommend remedial teaching and formal SLD evaluation.
