⚠ Important note on terminology & approach

The primary book uses the abbreviation EX/RP (Exposure and Ritual Prevention). "ERP" (Exposure and Response Prevention) is the same treatment; both terms are used in the field. This guide uses ERP / EX/RP interchangeably.

The Foa 2012 protocol is a behavioural, emotional-processing model that emphasises within- and between-session anxiety reduction (habituation) plus disconfirmation of feared consequences. It deliberately does not use formal cognitive restructuring. The more recent inhibitory-learning / expectancy-violation framework is presented in Module 5 and clearly labelled as a later development that reframes—rather than contradicts—the same exposure practice.

✦ Clinical pearl

Wherever a session plan or hierarchy here is a synthesis rather than the exact book text, it is labelled as such. The overall program length (a nominal 17-session, twice-weekly course, expandable to 17–20) follows the book's own worked structure.

Module 1

Introduction to OCD & ERP

What ERP is, why it works for OCD, and the single diagram that captures the whole treatment.

Slide 2 · Module 1

What is ERP?

ERP = deliberately confronting what OCD fears (Exposure) while not doing the compulsion or avoidance that normally follows (Response/Ritual Prevention).

One sentence

ERP is facing your fear on purpose and choosing not to do the ritual, so your brain learns the feared disaster doesn't happen and the anxiety fades on its own.

Simple explanation

OCD is a trap: the ritual gives relief, so you do it again and again, and the fear grows. ERP breaks the trap by staying with the discomfort until it settles without the ritual — teaching you that you're safe and can cope.

Postgraduate-level

ERP is a manualised CBT intervention in which the patient makes systematic, graded, prolonged contact with fear-eliciting stimuli (external cues, intrusive thoughts, images, sensations, and feared consequences) while abstaining from overt and covert neutralising responses, avoidance, and reassurance — enabling emotional processing, extinction of the conditioned distress, and disconfirmation of catastrophic appraisals.

Core mechanism

  • Exposure re-engages the fear structure so it can be modified by new, corrective information.
  • Response prevention blocks the compulsion/avoidance that would otherwise abort learning and maintain the disorder.
  • Together they let the patient discover: (1) anxiety declines without ritualising, (2) it declines further with repetition, (3) distress is tolerable, and (4) feared consequences do not occur or are far less likely than OCD claims.
Therapist note · Relationship with CBT

ERP is the behavioural engine of CBT for OCD. In the Foa model, belief change happens through exposure used as hypothesis-testing rather than through separate cognitive-restructuring sessions. Cognitive therapy for OCD (e.g., appraisal-focused work) is a related but distinct approach — see Module 5.

Why ERP for OCD specifically

In simple phobia, avoidance reduces distress and the fear stays "contained." In OCD, escape and avoidance fail to reliably reduce distress (the checker still worries once away from the door; the washer still feels contamination spreading), so the person escalates into rituals. ERP directly targets that failed-relief cycle.

Module 2

Understanding OCD & the Maintenance Cycle

The clinical anatomy of OCD and the self-perpetuating loop ERP is designed to break.

Slide 4 · Module 2

The Building Blocks of OCD

OCD is made of obsessions (the alarm) and compulsions (the failed off-switch), plus a wider ecosystem of avoidance, reassurance, and covert rituals.

Obsessions

Recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images, urges, or doubts that cause marked distress. Experienced as ego-dystonic ("not like me"). Content is often the opposite of the person's values.

Compulsions

Repetitive behaviours or mental acts the person feels driven to perform to reduce distress or prevent a feared outcome. Not realistically connected to what they aim to prevent, or clearly excessive.

The emotions

Not only anxiety — also disgust (contamination), guilt and shame (harm, sexual, religious), and an inflated sense of responsibility and threat.

The engine: uncertainty

Much of OCD is intolerance of doubt — "I can't be sure nothing bad will happen." Rituals chase a certainty that never fully arrives.

The wider ecosystem clinicians must map

  • Passive avoidance
  • Reassurance seeking
  • Mental compulsions
  • Covert rituals
  • Neutralising / undoing
  • Safety behaviours
  • Checking (overt & mental)
  • Thought suppression
✦ Clinical pearl

The single most useful question for classifying any behaviour: "What is its function?" If a behaviour or thought reduces anxiety or aims to prevent a feared outcome, it is a compulsion/safety behaviour — even if it looks reasonable (e.g., silently reassuring oneself "I'm a good person"). Function, not form, defines the target.

⚠ On "Pure-O"

"Pure obsessional" is a misleading label. These patients almost always have compulsions — they are just covert: mental review, neutralising, silent reassurance, mental checking, rumination. Treating "Pure-O" means hunting for the hidden ritual, then applying response prevention to it. Nobody is truly "pure obsession only."

⚠ Distressing intrusive content (harm / sexual / paedophilic / aggressive)

Intrusive thoughts about harming others, taboo sexual themes (including harm to children), and aggressive images are common OCD contents and are ego-dystonic — the person is horrified by them and does not want to act. They are not desires, plans, or indicators of risk of offending. Distinguishing OCD intrusions from genuine risk is a core assessment skill (Module 3); when in doubt, seek supervision. These themes respond well to ERP (largely imaginal) once correctly identified.

Module 3

Assessment Before ERP

What to assess, which tools to use, and how functional analysis turns symptoms into ERP targets.

Slide 7 · Module 3

What to Assess Before ERP

Assessment is not a formality — it is the treatment plan. Every item you elicit becomes a hierarchy step, a ritual-prevention rule, or a safety consideration.

OCD symptom map

  • Obsessions: external cues, internal cues (thoughts, images, impulses, doubts), and feared bodily sensations
  • Feared consequences (external, internal, and "harm from long-term anxiety")
  • Compulsions: overt and mental/covert
  • Avoidance: passive & active
  • Reassurance seeking (self & others)
  • Triggers, frequency, duration, distress (SUDS)

Person & context

  • Functional impairment (home, work, social)
  • Insight & overvalued ideation
  • Motivation & readiness
  • Previous treatment & response
  • Current medication
  • Family accommodation
  • Comorbidities (depression, other anxiety, tics)
  • Risk assessment
Therapist note · the Information-Gathering interview

The primary protocol structures this as a general history (~25 min) followed by detailed OCD information-gathering (~40 min) across fixed categories: external cues, internal cues, bodily sensations, feared consequences, passive avoidance, active avoidance, rituals (overt + mental), the avoidance–fear relationship, and onset/course. Record a summary for each; it feeds directly into Sessions 1–2.

⚠ Risk & insight

Assess suicidality and, for harm/sexual themes, differentiate ego-dystonic OCD intrusions from genuine intent (OCD = unwanted, resisted, out-of-character, no urge to enact; risk = congruent, planned, or desired). Note insight: poor insight/overvalued ideation predicts a harder course and may need extra motivational work before ERP.

Functional analysis converts a messy symptom picture into a clear ERP target by exposing the maintaining contingency.

e.g., Touching a public surface.

B · Behaviour

e.g., "I'm contaminated" → hand-washing.

Therapist note

The "C" that maintains OCD is almost always short-term relief. Naming the relief-as-reinforcer out loud helps the patient see why "just this once" checking is not harmless — it feeds the loop.

OCD Case Formulation

Weaving assessment into an explanatory model that points straight at treatment targets.

Slide 10 · Module 4

The 5-P Formulation Frame

Predisposing → Precipitating → Perpetuating → Protective factors → the maintenance cycle → treatment targets.

Predisposing

Genetic/temperamental vulnerability, inflated responsibility beliefs, early modelling of anxiety/checking, perfectionism.

Precipitating

The stressor or life event around onset (a first intrusive thought that "stuck," a loss, becoming a parent, illness scare).

Perpetuating

The heart of the formulation. Compulsions, avoidance, reassurance, thought suppression, family accommodation, "not-just-right" experiences — each producing short-term relief that maintains the disorder.

Protective

Good insight, motivation, supportive relationships, prior treatment gains, values worth recovering for.

Maintenance cycle → targets

Feed the perpetuating factors into the OCD loop (Module 2), then read off the ERP targets: which cues to expose, which rituals to prevent.

Slide 11 · Module 4

Worked Formulation — "Priya" (original fictional case)

Case snapshot

Priya, 27, teacher. Contamination OCD focused on illness after her father's hospitalisation. Washes 2–3 hrs/day; avoids public transport and shared kitchens; asks her partner "are my hands clean?" repeatedly.

Predisposing

Anxious temperament; mother modelled cleaning rituals; high conscientiousness/perfectionism.

Precipitating

Father's serious infection 8 months ago; a news story about "superbugs."

Perpetuating

Washing → relief; avoidance of transport/kitchens; reassurance from partner; googling symptoms; wearing gloves (safety behaviour).

Protective

Wants to travel again; strong relationship; good insight ("I know it's excessive").

Treatment targets read off the formulation

  • Expose: public transport rails, shared kitchen surfaces, "dirty" hands before eating.
  • Prevent: washing beyond one normal wash, glove use, googling, reassurance from partner.
  • Family: coach partner to stop answering "are my hands clean?" (see Module 13).
Patient perspective

"Seeing it drawn out, I realised the washing wasn't keeping me safe — it was keeping the fear fed."

Module 5

ERP Theory

The behavioural model, emotional processing, and the modern inhibitory-learning reframe.

Slide 12 · Module 5

The Behavioural Foundations

OCD is maintained by negative reinforcement; ERP dismantles it by re-activating the fear and blocking the reinforcing response.

  • Two-factor learning: fear is acquired (classical conditioning) and maintained by avoidance/rituals that are rewarded by relief (operant conditioning).
  • Exposure reverses avoidance and re-engages the fear so it can change.
  • Response prevention removes the relief that reinforces the ritual.
  • Safety behaviours (partial rituals, gloves, "clean hand/dirty hand") are subtle avoidance and must be dropped, or they keep the feared belief alive.
  • Uncertainty tolerance is the deeper skill — OCD demands certainty; recovery is learning to live with doubt.
Slide 13 · Module 5

Emotional Processing Theory (the primary model)

Foa's protocol is built on emotional processing theory, combining conditioning and information-processing ideas. Exposure activates the "fear structure," and corrective information modifies it.

What must happen

  • The fear must be activated (emotional engagement — a good match to real fears).
  • New information must be available that is incompatible with the fear ("anxiety fell; nothing bad happened").
  • Rituals/avoidance must be blocked so that new information is not explained away.

Processing (the 4 lessons)

  • Anxiety declines within an exposure, even without ritualising.
  • Anxiety declines across repeated exposures and stays lower.
  • Distress is manageable without rituals.
  • Feared consequences are disconfirmed or shown to be low-probability.
Therapist note · exposure as hypothesis-testing

The primary program uses no formal cognitive restructuring. Instead, each exposure is framed as a test of a specific prediction ("If I touch the rail and don't wash, I'll be 100% sure to get sick by tomorrow"). Track SUDS and check the prediction afterwards. Belief change happens through experience, not debate.

Slide 14 · Module 5

Habituation vs Inhibitory Learning

Two lenses on the same practice. The primary book (2012) leans on habituation; contemporary practice adds inhibitory-learning ideas. They are complementary — not contradictory.

Habituation model (traditional)

  • Goal: stay in the exposure until anxiety drops (within- and between-session).
  • Falling SUDS is the sign of success.
  • Emphasis on long, repeated exposures until distress subsides.
  • Strongly represented in the Foa 2012 protocol's language.

Inhibitory-learning model (modern)

  • Goal: expectancy violation — make the gap between "what I feared" and "what happened" as large and surprising as possible.
  • Anxiety reduction is welcome but not required; new learning can occur while still anxious.
  • Techniques: varied contexts, combining fears, removing safety behaviours, occasional reinforced/deepened exposures.
  • A later development that reframes the same ERP toolkit.
⚠ "ERP is NOT simply waiting until anxiety disappears"

This is the crucial correction. Even in the habituation tradition, the primary protocol explicitly tells therapists what to say when anxiety does not decrease: continue the item, the point is the learning. From the inhibitory-learning view:

  • Why distress may remain — anxiety and learning are separable; you can learn "I coped and nothing happened" while still feeling anxious.
  • Why exposure can still succeed — the memory of "I did it, I survived, my prediction was wrong" is retained regardless of the SUDS curve.
  • Why learning > immediate anxiety reduction — durable, generalisable recovery comes from disconfirmed expectancies, not a single calm session.
  • Why compulsions interfere with learning — any ritual/safety behaviour lets OCD credit the ritual ("I only survived because I washed"), blocking the new learning.
Where the models differ, this guide follows the primary Foa protocol for procedure and flags inhibitory-learning language as an optional, evidence-based reframe. The cognitive model (Clark; Salkovskis) — which targets appraisals directly — is a separate tradition; core ERP does not require it.

Module 6

Psychoeducation & Motivational Preparation

How to explain OCD and ERP — in therapist language and in warm, plain patient language — and how to build the motivation ERP demands.

Slide 15 · Module 6

Explaining OCD & ERP — Paired Scripts

For each concept: a precise therapist framing and a plain-language patient version. Deliver the patient version conversationally, using the patient's own symptoms as examples.

Therapist · What OCD is

A disorder of intrusive obsessions plus compulsive neutralising, maintained by negatively-reinforced escape/avoidance.

Patient · What OCD is

"OCD sends you unwanted thoughts that feel urgent and scary, and then pushes you to do something to feel safe. The 'doing something' is what keeps it going."

Therapist · Why intrusions occur

Intrusive thoughts are universal; OCD is the meaning and response, not the presence of the thought.

Patient · Why intrusions occur

"Everyone has weird, unwanted thoughts. In OCD your alarm system treats them as important warnings — so you notice them more and fear them more."

Therapist · Thought ≠ action

Thought–action fusion is a core appraisal error; having a thought neither causes nor equals the act.

Patient · Thought ≠ action

"Thinking about something does not make it happen and does not mean you want it. A thought is just a thought."

Therapist · Suppression backfires

Deliberate suppression increases intrusion frequency (rebound); we do not fight the obsession.

Patient · Suppression backfires

"Try not to think of a pink elephant… The more you push a thought away, the more it pushes back."

Therapist · Compulsions maintain OCD

Rituals produce short-term relief that negatively reinforces them and prevents disconfirmation.

Patient · Compulsions maintain OCD

"Rituals work for a minute, then the fear comes back stronger — like paying a blackmailer. ERP stops paying."

Therapist · Avoidance & reassurance

Avoidance prevents new learning; reassurance is an interpersonal compulsion that maintains doubt.

Patient · Avoidance & reassurance

"Avoiding keeps the fear alive because you never find out you'd be okay. Reassurance feels kind but it feeds the doubt."

Therapist · What ERP involves / expectations

Graded, prolonged exposure + strict ritual prevention + between-session practice; effortful, collaborative, time-limited.

Patient · What ERP involves / expectations

"We'll face fears step by step, starting manageable, and practise not doing the ritual. It's hard at first and gets easier fast. Practice between sessions is where most change happens."

⚠ The 4 "thinking mistakes" to name gently

(1) "Anxiety will last forever / I'll have a breakdown if I don't ritualise." (2) "Thinking it is the same as doing/wanting it." (3) "If I don't prevent harm, I've caused it." (4) "If I don't ritualise, the feared thing will happen." ERP tests each one against reality.

Slide 16 · Module 6

Motivational Preparation

ERP asks people to walk toward what terrifies them. Motivation, ambivalence, and a clear "why" must be worked before and revisited throughout treatment.

Address directly

  • Ambivalence & fear of exposure ("I can't do that")
  • Realistic expectations (discomfort is the treatment working)
  • Cost–benefit of OCD vs recovery
  • Values: what OCD is stealing
  • Concrete treatment goals
  • Commitment to full ritual prevention (no "lines in the sand")

Motivation strategies (from the protocol)

  • Frame therapy as "you & me vs OCD — a shared battle."
  • Discourage drawing "lines in the sand" (partial commitment predicts relapse).
  • Use analogies: leaving weeds in the garden / leaving cancer cells behind → it grows back.
  • The "hip-pocket patient": a de-identified example of someone whose partial effort led to relapse.
  • Reconnect to a life without OCD (work, relationships, hobbies, faith).
✦ Clinical pearl

"I will encourage you, but I will never force you — you do the exposures of your own free will." Volitional participation reduces arguing and increases follow-through. The therapist is a coach, not an enforcer.

Five original motivational worksheets — Why I Want to Recover, What OCD Is Costing Me, What Recovery Would Give Me, My Treatment Goals, My Reasons for Doing ERP — are provided in the Patient ERP Workbook (Worksheets 1 & 20 and the motivational set).

Module 7

Building the Exposure Hierarchy

The central planning tool of ERP — generating, rating, and sequencing exposures with SUDS.

Slide 17 · Module 7

What a Hierarchy Is & How to Build One

A hierarchy is a personalised, SUDS-ranked ladder of feared situations, thoughts, and cues — the map for the whole exposure phase.

1 · Generate triggers

From the info-gathering interview and self-monitoring: external cues, internal cues (thoughts/images), feared consequences, bodily sensations.

2 · Identify avoided situations

Everything the person steers clear of — including subtle avoidance (touching the "least-used" side of a door).

3 · Identify rituals & safety behaviours

Overt and mental rituals, plus partial "mini-rituals" (hand cream instead of washing, using a sleeve).

4 · Estimate distress with SUDS

Rate each item 0–100 (see below). Get the patient's own examples for the anchors.

5 · Rank & select achievable steps

Order ascending by SUDS. Ensure each step is a meaningful but tolerable increment.

6 · Revise as you go

SUDS estimates are predictions; update them from real exposure data. Add newly discovered cues; drop items that no longer evoke distress for several days.

The SUDS scale (0–100) — per the primary protocol

0102030405060708090100
  • 0 = no discomfort at all (anchor to a real calm situation the patient names).
  • 50 = distressed but still manageable (patient's own example).
  • 100 = the most distressed they have ever been (patient's own example).
The primary source uses a 0–100 SUDS scale with 0 / 50 / 100 anchors. Some clinicians use 0–10; this guide follows the source's 0–100 throughout.
Therapist note · sequencing rules

Present items ascending, beginning near the midpoint of the patient's range (if the top is 100, start around 50 — big, meaningful increments, not tiny desensitisation steps). Aim to reach the most disturbing item around the mid-point of treatment (~Session 8). Do the item first in-session, then assign it as homework. Keep the plan flexible — rigid adherence contradicts the very lesson of tolerating uncertainty.

Response (Ritual) Prevention

The other half of ERP — blocking every overt and covert ritual so new learning can occur.

Slide 19 · Module 8

Response Prevention in Depth

Ritual prevention means refraining from all compulsive behaviour — overt and covert — and from the subtle substitutes OCD invents when the obvious ritual is blocked.

Overt rituals to prevent

  • Washing / cleaning
  • Checking / retracing
  • Repeating actions
  • Ordering / arranging
  • Counting
  • Confessing

Covert / mental rituals to prevent

  • Mental checking
  • Mental reviewing
  • Neutralising thoughts
  • Self-reassurance
  • Silent "good" words/images
  • Ritual prayer
  • Rumination-as-solving

Interpersonal / info-seeking

  • Asking others for reassurance
  • Internet searching / googling symptoms
  • Confessing to obtain absolution

Subtle substitutes ("mini-rituals")

  • Hand cream / wipes instead of washing
  • Blowing/brushing "germs" off
  • Using a sleeve or "clean hand"
  • Brief avoidance detours
⚠ Detecting subtle rituals — the therapist's job

Ask directly and repeatedly: "Now that you've stopped the big ritual, are you doing any little thing to relieve the anxiety instead?" If a behaviour or thought functions to reduce distress or prevent the feared outcome, it is a ritual — regardless of how sensible it looks. Add it to the ritual-prevention rules.

Therapist note · mental rituals & the obsession

Two rules the patient must hold together: (1) Do not fight or suppress the obsession — that strengthens it; if anything, deliberately bring it on as exposure. (2) Do not perform the mental ritual that follows it. Guide: obsessive thought → notice, let it be; mental ritual → don't do it, get busy with something else. "Getting busy" is not distraction-to-avoid; it is passing time while allowing the thought to be present.

✦ Clinical pearl · undoing a slip

If a patient rituals (often automatically, out of habit), have them "undo / spoil" it and re-expose immediately — e.g., re-contaminate after washing; walk through the door once more after an even-numbered ritual. Each undo is a step toward beating OCD.

⚠ Emotional engagement

The exposure must evoke the same distress as the real fear. If the patient reframes a cancer ward as "just a hospital" to feel calmer, the exercise fails. Ask them to lean into the feared meaning during exposure, not away from it.

Slide 21 · Module 9

Therapist Conduct — Before, During, After

The therapist is a calm, warm coach who models approach, protects the exposure from subtle sabotage, and helps the patient process the learning.

Before

  • Restate the rationale & the specific prediction being tested
  • Describe exactly what will happen
  • Confirm the ritual-prevention rules for this exposure
  • Get the pre-SUDS

During

  • Model it first (sit on the floor, touch it yourself)
  • Ask SUDS every 5 min ("What's your level?")
  • Keep contact continuous; re-contaminate/re-engage every few minutes
  • Coach toward the feared meaning; block covert rituals & safety behaviours
  • Warm encouragement, never reassurance

After (processing)

  • Review the SUDS curve together
  • Check the prediction: did the feared thing happen?
  • Note ritual-prevention achieved & the lesson learned
  • Set matching homework
Therapist note · language that helps vs harms

Helpful: "You're finding out anxiety doesn't last forever." "As predicted, the urge is dropping." "Notice you're less anxious than at the start." Avoid: anything that functions as reassurance ("You're definitely safe," "It's definitely clean") — that becomes a ritual and undoes the work. If anxiety doesn't fall: "This happens sometimes; we'll keep working this item — the point is what you're learning, not today's number."

⚠ Handling requests to stop & emotional overload

If the patient balks: empathise, explore the hesitation, and encourage proceeding with the agreed plan (offer a smaller interim step if genuinely too much). Exception — emotional overload: if the patient is overwhelmed by feelings unrelated to the exposure (crying, shaking, a life crisis), pause the exposure, address the distress, and resume later or next session — an overwhelmed patient cannot process the exposure.

Session 1 · Chapter 4

Treatment Planning, Part I

⏱ 120 minGoal: engage, assess, explain, motivateExposure: none yet

Primary goal

Build rapport, gather a full history and OCD symptom picture, deliver psychoeducation and the treatment rationale, and start self-monitoring.

Objectives

  • General history (~25 min)
  • OCD information-gathering (~40 min)
  • Explain OCD (~10 min)
  • Rationale for in vivo & imaginal exposure (~20 min)
  • Describe the program (~5 min)
  • Introduce self-monitoring (~10 min)
  • Schedule phone contact; assign homework

Therapist agenda

  1. General history — age, relationships, living situation, work, medical/educational/employment/financial history, key relationships (Information-Gathering Form).
  2. OCD information-gathering — external cues; internal cues (thoughts/images/impulses/doubts); feared bodily sensations; feared consequences (external, internal, harm-from-long-term-anxiety); passive & active avoidance; rituals (overt and mental); the avoidance–fear relationship; onset & course. Have the Y-BOCS checklist available.
  3. Explain OCD — using the patient's own examples, show the obsession→distress→ritual→short-relief→maintenance link.
  4. Rationale — how in vivo exposure + ritual prevention works; introduce imaginal exposure for consequences that can't be tested in reality.
  5. Describe treatment — twice-weekly, exposure begins Session 3, weekly phone calls, effortful but time-limited.
  6. Self-monitoring — teach the daily form; practise filling it with an imagined day.
Therapist script · explaining OCD (original paraphrase)

Therapist: "You have a set of symptoms we call obsessions — thoughts, images, or urges that arrive against your will and bring intense distress. To ease that distress you avoid things, or you do certain actions or thoughts we call rituals. The trouble is, the relief is temporary; the distress returns, so you ritualise more, and more of your life gets swallowed up. This treatment doesn't depend on knowing why you got OCD — it works by changing how you respond to the obsessions now."

Assessment of current OCD

This whole session is the baseline assessment. Summarise every category on the Information-Gathering Form; you'll convert it into SUDS-rated hierarchy items in Session 2.

Expected difficulties & troubleshooting

Shame about symptom content (esp. harm/sexual) — normalise: "these are common OCD themes, not your wishes." Low insight — spend more time on the rationale before pushing toward exposure. Overwhelm — reassure that exposure is gradual and volitional.

Homework

Complete a Self-Monitoring Form daily (time, trigger, SUDS 0–100, ritual, minutes on ritual); read the "OCD: Some Facts" handout; note the phone-call time. Worksheet: "Understanding My OCD" (Worksheet 1).

Therapist documentation

Record history, symptom map, provisional diagnosis, Y-BOCS baseline, insight, motivation, risk, and the plan to build the hierarchy next session.

Phone Contact 1: troubleshoot self-monitoring; confirm reading. Next session: build SUDS hierarchy & treatment plan.

Session 2 · Chapter 5

Treatment Planning, Part II

⏱ 120 minGoal: SUDS, hierarchy, treatment contractExposure: none (planning)

Primary goal

Turn the symptom map into a SUDS-rated hierarchy and an agreed treatment plan, sealed with a treatment contract.

Objectives

  • Review self-monitoring (~5 min)
  • Review rationale (~10 min)
  • Collect detailed symptom info (~50 min)
  • Introduce SUDS & set anchors
  • Build the hierarchy
  • Generate the treatment plan (~30 min)
  • Treatment contract (~15 min)

In-session interventions

  • Introduce SUDS (0–100) and establish the patient's own 0 / 50 / 100 anchors.
  • Elicit & rate items across categories: external feared situations, fear-evoking thoughts, feared bodily sensations, feared consequences, harm-from-long-term-anxiety, passive avoidance, and detailed ritual descriptions.
  • Build the hierarchy (Hierarchy Form): rank ascending; plan to start near the midpoint and reach the top item ~Session 8; map items to sessions (flexibly).
Therapist script · introducing SUDS (original paraphrase)

Therapist: "We'll rate distress from 0 to 100. Describe a moment that was totally calm — that's your 0. Now the most distressed you've ever been — that's 100. And something distressing but still manageable — that's 50. Using that scale, where are you right now?"

Treatment contract

Agree explicitly: patient does planned exposures (in-session & homework), refrains from rituals, contacts therapist/support person before giving in to an urge, and undoes any accidental ritual by re-exposing. Therapist coaches, never forces, and starts with the easiest items. Do not proceed to exposure until the patient agrees to the plan.

Troubleshooting

Vague or "everything is 100" ratings — anchor to concrete moments and re-rate. "Lines in the sand" ("I'll do X but never Y") — address now with motivation strategies (Module 6).

Homework

Continue self-monitoring; read the "Understanding EX/RP" handout. Worksheet: "My OCD Cycle + Exposure Plan" (Worksheet 2), plus Triggers/Compulsions/Avoidance/Reassurance sheets (Worksheets 3–6).

Phone Contact 2: troubleshoot monitoring; confirm reading; preview that exposure begins next session (bring any needed materials). Next session: first in vivo exposure.

Session 3 · Chapter 6

Exposure & Ritual Prevention — Introducing In Vivo Exposure

⏱ 120 minGoal: first structured in vivo exposureExposure: 45–60 min in vivo

Primary goal

Conduct the first supervised in vivo exposure to a low/mid hierarchy item, establish ritual-prevention rules, and set self-exposure homework.

Objectives

  • Review self-monitoring (~5 min)
  • Conduct in vivo exposure (~80 min)
  • Instruct on self-exposure (~15 min)
  • Give ritual-prevention rules (~5 min)
  • Assign homework; schedule call

Conducting the exposure

  • Re-summarise the rationale; state exactly what the patient will do.
  • Model it — participate (e.g., sit on the floor, touch it yourself) to build trust.
  • Maintain continuous contact; for contamination, touch face/hair/clothes every few minutes so "no part stays uncontaminated."
  • Ask SUDS every 5 minutes; record on the Therapist Exposure Recording Form.
  • Coach toward the feared meaning; block covert rituals.
  • Process: review the SUDS trend and what it means; note ritual-prevention achieved, lesson learned, difficulties.
Therapist script · washing-type exposure (original paraphrase)

Therapist: "Today you'll touch this with your whole hand, then bring it to your face, hair, and clothes so no part of you stays 'clean,' and we'll sit with it for the session, re-touching every few minutes. Your distress will rise — and then it will fall, without washing. Let yourself think about the illness you're afraid of. I'm sure you can do this, and it gets easier each time. Here it is — go ahead and touch it."

Ritual-prevention rules & mental rituals

Give the Patient Rules for Ritual Prevention handout (see Workbook). Explain mental rituals: self-reassurance that reduces anxiety is a ritual and must stop; but don't suppress the obsession — if anything, bring it on. "Get busy" with something else while allowing the thought to be present.

Troubleshooting

Anxiety not dropping — normalise, continue the item, extend duration at home. Balking — empathise, offer an interim step, refer to the contract. Covert ritual spotted — name it, add to rules, re-expose.

Homework

Repeat the same exposure daily (~2 hrs), recording SUDS every 10 min on the Exposure Homework Recording Form; follow ritual-prevention rules; continue self-monitoring. Worksheets: Exposure Hierarchy (7), Exposure Planning (8), ERP Practice Record (9), SUDS Monitoring (10), Response Prevention Record (11).

Phone Contact 3: did they expose long enough? did distress drop? any rituals (if so, re-expose)? Next session: introduce imaginal exposure.

Session 4 · Chapter 7

Exposure & Ritual Prevention — Introducing Imaginal Exposure

⏱ 90–120 minGoal: add imaginal exposureExposure: imaginal (~45) + in vivo (~45)

Primary goal

Introduce imaginal exposure for feared consequences that can't/shouldn't be created in reality, then continue in vivo work.

Objectives

  • Review monitoring & homework (~5 min)
  • Introduce & conduct imaginal exposure (~45 min)
  • Continue in vivo exposure (~45 min)
  • Self-exposure instructions (~5 min)
  • Homework; schedule call

Imaginal exposure — how

  • Re-state the imaginal rationale: repeatedly imagining the feared scenario reduces its power, proves "thinking ≠ doing/causing," and builds acceptance of distress.
  • Build the script collaboratively (Imaginal Exposure Script Worksheet): vivid, present-tense, first-person, including the full feared consequence.
  • Patient closes eyes and imagines it "as if happening now"; rate SUDS every 5 min.
  • Audio-record the script; homework = listen twice daily, eyes closed, recording Pre / Peak / Post SUDS.
When imaginal is indicated

Immoral/illegal/impossible-to-create consequences; future or unverifiable harm ("cancer in 30 years"); harm, sexual, and religious-afterlife themes; and a near-must for mental ritualisers / "pure obsessionals." Not needed when in vivo already taps the fear fully.

Troubleshooting

Patient "waters down" the script to feel calmer — coach them to include the worst feared meaning. Difficulty visualising — add sensory detail, use the recording. Guilt at "wishing harm" — clarify: imagining is exposure, not intent.

Homework

Listen to the imaginal recording 2×/day (Pre/Peak/Post SUDS); continue the assigned in vivo self-exposure; keep monitoring. Worksheets: Learning From Exposure (15), Between-Session Practice (16).

Phone Contact: confirm imaginal listening + in vivo practice; ritual prevention; encourage. Next: intermediate sessions — ascend the hierarchy.

Sessions 5–7 · Chapter 8 (Intermediate)

Ascending the Hierarchy

⏱ 90–120 min eachGoal: climb toward harder itemsFormat follows Sessions 3–4
Sessions 5–7 are a clinically organised synthesis of the book's Intermediate Sessions chapter (which covers ~10–12 sessions in one chapter, instructing "same format as Sessions 3–4, increasingly difficult, peak ~Session 8"). Individual session content will vary by patient.

Primary goal

Move steadily up the hierarchy — each session repeats the format of Sessions 3–4 with a somewhat harder item, consolidating gains and widening ritual prevention.

Every intermediate session

  • Review self-monitoring & homework at the start
  • Conduct in vivo (+ imaginal as needed) with processing
  • Assign matching homework at the end; schedule the call

What builds across 5–7

  • Increasing exposure difficulty — introduce the next item(s) once the current one drops.
  • Reducing safety behaviours — hunt for and drop mini-rituals and subtle avoidance.
  • Tightening response prevention — including covert rituals & reassurance.
  • Independent practice — patient begins designing/leading exposures.
  • Multiple contexts — vary place, time, and object to promote generalisation.
Therapist script · processing a good exposure (original)

Therapist: "Your SUDS went 80 → 55 → 40 without any washing. What does that tell you about your prediction that it would 'never ease'?" Patient: "That it did ease… and nothing happened." Therapist: "Exactly — and it'll ease faster next time."

Homework & worksheets

Daily self-exposure to the session item(s); Mental Compulsion Tracker (12) and Safety Behaviour Tracker (13) where relevant; Uncertainty Practice (14).

Session 8 · Chapter 8 (peak)

Confronting the Top of the Hierarchy

⏱ 90–120 minGoal: the most-feared itemMilestone session

Primary goal

Around the mid-point of treatment, confront the highest (≈100 SUDS) item — the exposure the whole hierarchy has been preparing for.

Why now

By Session 8 the patient has repeatedly learned "anxiety falls, feared things don't happen." Those disconfirmations weaken the top item's power before they even face it.

Therapist note

Prepare thoroughly, keep the ritual-prevention rules strict, and process intensively afterwards. This is often the turning point — patients frequently discover the "worst" item is more manageable than they feared, precisely because of the earlier steps.

Troubleshooting

If the top item is genuinely too much today, use an interim step toward it (flexibility + persistence), then return. Watch for emotional overload vs ordinary exposure distress.

Homework

Practise the peak item independently; continue imaginal if used. Learning From Exposure (15).

Sessions 9–15 · Chapter 8 (Intermediate)

Repeat, Vary, Generalise

⏱ 90–120 min eachGoal: consolidate & generaliseHome visits as needed
Per the primary protocol: "Sessions 9 through 15 are repetitions of Session 8 with variations, focusing on the items that provoke the most discomfort." The breakdown below is a clinically organised synthesis.

Primary goal

Repeat the hardest exposures under varied conditions, introduce cues not on the original hierarchy, and generalise to real life.

Focus areas

  • Multiple contexts (home, work, shops, public buildings, hospital)
  • Residual avoidance & remaining covert compulsions
  • Reassurance-seeking (self, others, internet)
  • Uncertainty tolerance as the explicit goal
  • Independent ERP practice (patient designs exposures)

Home visits

Any time from Session 3 on. The therapist observes functioning at home, finds hidden "safe zones" and subtle avoidance, and coaches exposures where "the OCD actually lives." Especially useful when office gains don't transfer home.

Progress review (do periodically)

Ask what's changed in behaviour, tolerance of thoughts, and functioning. If progress stalls, check homework completion and ritual-prevention adherence, and problem-solve obstacles — don't just keep pushing.

✦ Clinical pearl · relapse prevention starts early

From the intermediate phase onward, tell patients intrusive thoughts will still occur even after successful treatment — that's normal, not relapse. Teach them to set up their own exposures so they can be "their own therapist." Progress Review (18) worksheet supports this.

Homework

Patient-designed exposures in real-world contexts; keep dropping safety behaviours; Between-Session Practice (16), Difficult Exposure Troubleshooting (17).

Consolidation, Return to Normal, & Termination

⏱ 90–120 minGoal: relapse prevention & endingHome visit(s) as needed

Primary goal

Consolidate gains, guide the return to normal (non-ritualistic) routines, cement relapse prevention, and end therapy well.

Objectives

  • Progress review (~45 min): re-rate the hierarchy
  • Return to routine behaviour (~15 min): Guidelines for Normal Behavior
  • Relapse prevention detail (~10 min)
  • Follow-up phone calls scheduled
  • Conclude therapy

Return to routine behaviour

Ritual-prevention rules were deliberately strict during treatment. Now relax them to normal levels — e.g., ordinary hand-washing, a single lock-check — using the Guidelines for Normal Behavior (individualise if the patient's symptoms aren't covered).

Relapse prevention — the toolkit

  • Re-rate the hierarchy so the patient sees how far items have dropped.
  • List the strategies that worked: don't avoid; exposure is always easier than feared; give up the need for 100% certainty.
  • Plan for leftover issues and future triggers.
  • OCD "quiz" / role-reversal: patient plays therapist and coaches you on what to do if a symptom returns.
  • Have a plan for a lapse — including calling for a booster if needed.
Therapist script · role-reversal (original)

Therapist: "Let's switch — I'm the patient. I just had a scary intrusive thought and I want to check. What should I do?" Patient(-as-therapist): "Don't check. Notice the thought, let it be there, and don't run away — set up an exposure instead."

Homework & worksheets

Keep practising in vivo + imaginal ERP independently. Worksheets: Relapse Prevention (19) and "My Long-Term ERP Plan / My ERP Maintenance Plan" (20).

Termination

Bid farewell warmly, frame the patient as their own therapist now, and schedule a check-up as needed.

✦ Clinical pearl

"Leaning into anxiety rather than running from it" is the single best predictor of a good long-term outcome. The follow-up calls exist to keep that habit alive.

Case 1 · Module 10

Contamination OCD — "Nisha"

Presentation

Nisha, 24, student. Fear of "germs" causing serious illness. Washes hands until raw; avoids public transport, cafeterias, and shared bathrooms; Y-BOCS in the moderate–severe range.

Assessment & functional analysis

A: touch public surface → B: "I'm infected" → wash/sanitise → C: relief. Feared consequence: contracting a disease and infecting family.

Formulation

Perfectionistic temperament; onset after a flatmate's illness; maintained by washing, avoidance, sanitiser, and reassurance from her mother.

Compulsions: washing, sanitiser, "clean/dirty hand." Avoidance: transport, shared spaces. Hierarchy: doorknob (50) → handrail + eat snack (65) → toilet seat (85) → contaminate whole flat (100).

ERP plan & progression: in vivo from Session 3; peak (flat contamination) ~Session 8; homework ~2 hrs/day. Difficulties: secret hand-cream substitute discovered in Session 6 → added to RP rules. Troubleshooting: re-contaminate after any slip. Outcome: washing to normal levels; travelling again; residual mild doubt she now tolerates.

Case 2 · Module 10

Checking OCD — "Arjun"

Presentation

Arjun, 38, accountant. Repeatedly checks locks, stove, and emails; retraces his commute fearing he caused an accident; late to work daily.

Functional analysis: leaves home → "did I lock it? burglary/my fault" → check ×5 → relief. Formulation: inflated responsibility; onset after a neighbour's break-in. Compulsions: checking, retracing, re-reading, phone photos of the lock. Avoidance: being last to leave.

Hierarchy: switch once + leave (50) → lock once, walk away (65) → send emails unchecked (75) → drive without retracing (100). ERP: mostly in vivo; imaginal for the hit-and-run fear. Difficulty: mental checking replaced overt checking → targeted as covert ritual. Outcome: single normal check; commute time halved.

Case 3 · Module 10

Harm OCD — "Meera"

Presentation

Meera, 31, new mother. Intrusive images of harming her baby; horrified and ashamed; hides knives; avoids bathing the baby alone.

⚠ Risk clarification

Ego-dystonic, resisted, out-of-character images with no desire or urge to act = OCD, not intent. Documented careful risk assessment; supervision sought given the theme.

Compulsions: avoidance of knives & solo caregiving, mental reviewing "I'd never," reassurance from husband, confessing thoughts. Hierarchy (largely imaginal): word "harm" (50) → hold knife near baby (75) → imaginal worst-case script (90). ERP: imaginal script listened to 2×/day + in vivo knife exposure; RP on mental review, reassurance, confession. Outcome: images became "background noise"; resumed full parenting.

Case 4 · Module 10

Religious / Moral Scrupulosity — "Yusuf"

Presentation

Yusuf, 29. Fears he prays "incorrectly" and will be punished; repeats prayers for hours; avoids worship for fear of blasphemous thoughts.

Compulsions: repeating prayers "until right," seeking reassurance from a religious leader, mental cancelling of thoughts. Hierarchy: attend worship, pray once (55) → allow a "blasphemous" thought (75) → imaginal uncertainty about salvation (95). ERP: in vivo (pray once, normally) + imaginal (tolerating doubt about the afterlife); collaborated with a supportive faith figure to define "normal" practice. Outcome: worship restored as meaningful, not compulsive.

Therapist note

For scrupulosity, distinguish devout practice from compulsion by function. Where the patient won't/can't push doctrinal boundaries, imaginal exposure to uncertainty (esp. after-death consequences) is the key tool.

Relationship OCD — "Kavya"

Presentation

Kavya, 27. Relentless doubt "Do I really love my partner? Is this the right relationship?" Checks her feelings, compares to others, seeks reassurance.

Compulsions: feeling-checking, mental comparison, googling "signs of true love," reassurance from friends. Hierarchy: notice a doubt without checking (55) → go on a date and sit with uncertainty (70) → write "I may never be 100% sure" and leave it (85). ERP: uncertainty exposure + RP on feeling-checks/comparisons. Outcome: commitment despite doubt; doubts lost their grip.

Case 6 · Module 10

Presentation

Rohit, 22. Unwanted taboo sexual images; checks his body for arousal to "prove" his identity; avoids certain people and places; deep shame.

⚠ Clarify

Compulsions: arousal-checking, avoidance, mental reassurance, thought suppression. Hierarchy: approach a trigger (60) → script the intrusive thought (80) → sit in a feared context without checking (90). ERP: imaginal scripting + in vivo approach; RP on checking & reassurance. Outcome: shame reduced; thoughts defused.

Case 7 · Module 10

Mental Compulsions / "Pure-O" — "Dev"

Presentation

Dev, 34. Endless rumination trying to "solve" unanswerable doubts; mentally reviews conversations for hours; few visible rituals — a "Pure-O" presentation.

Hidden compulsions: mental review, rumination-as-solving, silent reassurance, mental checking. Hierarchy: notice a doubt, don't review (60) → write the doubt, leave unresolved (75) → deliberately evoke the worst uncertainty (90). ERP: heavily imaginal + strict covert RP ("get busy" while allowing the thought). Outcome: rumination time collapsed once the hidden ritual was blocked.

✦ Clinical pearl

"Pure-O" is never pure — find the mental ritual, then prevent it. That is the whole treatment.

Compulsions: body-checking, googling, reassurance from doctors/family. Presentation

Therapist note

For "just-right" OCD, the target sensation is discomfort, not classic anxiety — the learning is "this uncomfortable feeling fades without fixing anything."

Applied Skills

Therapist–Patient Dialogues

Seventeen original micro-dialogues modelling ERP-consistent responses to common in-session moments.

Dialogues 1–9

Introducing & Protecting the Work

1 · Introducing ERP

T: "We're going to face the fears on purpose, in steps you agree to, and practise not doing the ritual — so your brain gets to learn the truth OCD keeps hiding from you." P: "That sounds terrifying." T: "It's uncomfortable, not dangerous — and I'll coach you the whole way, starting easy."

2 · Explaining compulsions

T: "A compulsion is anything you do — out loud or in your head — to feel less anxious or to stop a feared outcome. It works for a moment, then makes OCD stronger."

3 · Explaining response prevention

T: "Response prevention means we don't do the ritual — even the small, sneaky versions — so the anxiety can settle by itself and you find out you didn't need the ritual at all."

4 · Patient asks for reassurance

P: "Are you sure this is clean?" T: "That sounds like OCD asking. If I answer, you'll feel better for a minute and the doubt will come back stronger. I care about you too much to feed it — let's stay with the uncertainty together."

5 · Patient wants to stop the exposure

P: "I can't, I need to stop." T: "I hear how hard this is. This is exactly the step we agreed on, and I'm right here. Can you give it two more minutes and tell me your number?"

6 · Very high distress

T: "Your SUDS is 95 — that's OCD at full volume. Nothing has gone wrong; this is the fear showing up so we can teach it. Breathe, keep contact, stay with me. What's your number now?"

7 · Patient performs a covert ritual

T: "I noticed you went quiet and looked away — were you reassuring yourself in your head? That's a mental ritual. Let's let the thought sit there without answering it."

8 · Patient says "What if…?"

P: "But what if it really happens?" T: "Great — that's the doubt. The ERP answer isn't to solve it, it's to say 'maybe, I can't be sure,' and carry on. Want to say that out loud with me?"

9 · "Do you think I'm safe?"

P: "Just tell me I'm safe." T: "If I promise certainty, I become part of the ritual. What I can promise is that I'll stand with you while you practise living without that guarantee."

Dialogues 10–17

Obstacles & Difficult Content

10 · Homework not done

T: "No judgement — let's understand what got in the way. Was it too big a step, or the fear itself? We'll shrink it to something you'll actually do, because between-session practice is where most change happens."

11 · Family provides reassurance

T (to family): "When she asks 'are my hands clean?', that's OCD, not her. The kind thing is to say, 'We agreed with your therapist I won't answer that,' then redirect — 'shall we take a walk?'"

12 · Patient wants certainty

T: "OCD promises certainty and never delivers — it just keeps sending the bill. Recovery is trading the search for certainty for a life you can actually live."

13 · Believes the exposure is dangerous

T: "Let's separate real danger from OCD danger. Would most people do this without harm? If yes, it's therapeutic discomfort. If it were truly dangerous, I'd never ask you to do it."

14 · Disgust rather than anxiety

T: "Disgust behaves like anxiety here — it fades if we don't wash it away. Stay in contact and let's watch the disgust drop, same as fear does."

15 · Guilt

P: "I feel so guilty even doing this." T: "OCD weaponises your conscience. Feeling guilty isn't evidence you did wrong — let's let the guilt be present without confessing or undoing it."

16 · Intrusive sexual thoughts

T: "These thoughts horrify you precisely because they clash with who you are — that's OCD, not desire. We'll practise letting the thought be there and not checking or reassuring."

17 · Aggressive intrusive thoughts

T: "An image of harm is not an intention. The more you fight it, the louder it gets. Let's deliberately invite the thought in an imaginal script and prove that thinking it changes nothing about what you'd ever do."

Slide · Module 13

Recognising & Reversing Accommodation

Family accommodation = anything relatives do to reduce the patient's distress that ends up maintaining OCD.

Accommodation cycle

Patient distressed / asks
↓
Family reassures / does the ritual / avoids the trigger
↓
Patient's distress drops briefly
↓
OCD (and the demand) strengthens

Common accommodations

  • Giving reassurance
  • Participating in rituals (checking on the patient's behalf)
  • Providing items or excessive cleaning/checking
  • Changing family routines around the OCD
  • Avoiding triggers for the patient

How family can support ERP without becoming part of OCD

  • Learn the model (invite to an early session): "OCD, not the person, is the enemy."
  • Reduce accommodation gradually and collaboratively — an abrupt withdrawal can backfire.
  • Respond to reassurance requests with a pre-agreed line + redirection ("we agreed I won't answer that — shall we go for a walk?").
  • Encourage, don't force; praise the patient's efforts; expect a gradual pace.
  • Frame it as "we're fighting OCD together, not each other."

Part XX

Medication & ERP

A clinically responsible overview only. Medication decisions belong to the treating psychiatrist / qualified prescriber.

First-line pharmacotherapy

  • SSRIs — the standard medication class for OCD (often higher doses / longer trials than for depression).
  • Clomipramine — a tricyclic with strong OCD evidence; more side-effects, so usually not first choice.

Combined & adherence

  • ERP can be delivered alone, or added to medication — adding ERP can further reduce symptoms in partial medication responders.
  • Discuss adherence; coordinate with the prescriber; note meds in your formulation.
⚠ Scope

This guide gives no individualised prescribing advice, doses, or titration. Refer medication questions to the treating psychiatrist or medical professional.

Part XXI

ERP & Other CBT Techniques

Core ERP is exposure + ritual prevention + processing. Everything else is optional/adjunctive — useful in some cases, never a substitute for exposure.

Core ERP (the engine)

  • In vivo exposure
  • Imaginal exposure
  • Ritual prevention
  • Processing (exposure as hypothesis-testing)
  • Home visits

Optional / adjunctive

  • Cognitive restructuring — a distinct tradition (Salkovskis, Clark) targeting appraisals; the primary protocol omits it
  • Behavioural experiments — overlap heavily with exposure-as-hypothesis-testing
  • Mindfulness / acceptance / defusion — can support "allowing" thoughts, but must not become avoidance
  • Metacognitive strategies
  • Motivational interviewing — for ambivalence/readiness
  • Values work — fuels motivation
⚠ Watch for

Mindfulness/acceptance/relaxation can be quietly recruited as compulsions ("I'll breathe until the anxiety goes"). Keep the function honest: adjuncts support exposure; they don't replace it or neutralise it.

Part XXIII

What ERP Is NOT

Correcting misconceptions protects patients and improves adherence.

ERP is NOT…
  • Forced exposure — it's collaborative & volitional
  • Flooding by default — it's graded, with big meaningful steps
  • Making the patient suffer for its own sake
  • Eliminating every intrusive thought
  • Proving thoughts are false
…and it is NOT
  • Giving reassurance
  • Waiting for anxiety to reach zero
  • Guaranteeing certainty
  • A punishment
  • A rigid, one-size-fits-all protocol
✦ Clinical pearl

ERP is therapeutic discomfort in the service of the patient's own goals — chosen, coached, and time-limited.

Part XXIV

Ethics & Safety

Core principles

  • Informed consent to the rationale, demands, and discomfort of ERP
  • Patient autonomy — encourage, never force
  • Collaborative, agreed exposure planning
  • Ongoing risk assessment
  • Clinical judgement & therapist competence (supervision when out of scope)
  • Boundaries, confidentiality, documentation

Discomfort vs danger

The defining safety question: would ordinary people do this without real harm?

Therapeutic discomfort

Touching a public rail, leaving a light on, holding a knife normally in the kitchen.

Actual danger

Anything genuinely unsafe, illegal, or unhealthy. Never design exposures that create real risk.

⚠ Contraindications / cautions

Do not use ERP as sole treatment amid acute suicidality, unmanaged psychosis, or where a "feared" act is genuinely intended. Reassess and refer.

Part XXVII

Staying Well

Key ideas

  • Lapse ≠ relapse. Everyone has intrusive thoughts; a symptom returning is not failure.
  • Symptoms naturally fluctuate, often around stress.
  • The skill is knowing what to do: notice the thought, don't neutralise, don't avoid, set up an exposure.

Early warning signs

  • Rituals creeping back (overt or mental)
  • New avoidance
  • Reassurance-seeking returning
  • Time lost to obsessions rising

Maintenance plan

  • Keep leaning into anxiety; refuse all rituals & avoidance.
  • Run booster self-exposures when a theme flares.
  • Remember the top strategies: don't avoid; exposure is easier than feared; give up the need for 100% certainty.
  • Have a written action plan and a booster/therapist-contact option.

Quick Reference

The Cheat Sheets

ERP in 5 minutes

Face the fear on purpose (exposure), don't do the ritual (response prevention), stay long enough to learn (processing). Result: anxiety falls, feared things don't happen, you can live with doubt.

OCD cycle in 5 minutes

Trigger → obsession → distress → compulsion/avoidance → short relief → reinforcement → stronger OCD. ERP re-routes the same trigger toward new learning.

ERP in 10 steps

  1. Assess
  2. Formulate
  3. Psychoeducate
  4. Build hierarchy (SUDS 0–100)
  5. Set ritual-prevention rules
  6. Predict the feared outcome
  7. Expose (mid-item first)
  8. Prevent response (incl. covert)
  9. Process the learning
  10. Homework + generalise

How to build a hierarchy

List triggers/avoidance/rituals → rate SUDS 0–100 → rank ascending → start near midpoint → reach top ~Session 8 → revise from real data.

How to identify a compulsion

Ask: does it reduce anxiety or aim to prevent a feared outcome? If yes → compulsion (target it), however sensible it looks.

How to identify a mental compulsion

Look for silent review, neutralising, counting, "good" words, self-reassurance, rumination-as-solving. Same test: function = anxiety reduction.

How to respond to reassurance-seeking

Name it as a ritual; decline warmly ("if I answer, I feed OCD"); put the "blame" on the treatment agreement; redirect.

How to conduct an exposure

Rationale + prediction → describe it → model it → continuous contact → SUDS q5min → block rituals → process the result → matching homework.

How to conduct response prevention

Prevent overt + covert + subtle substitutes. If a slip happens, undo/spoil it and re-expose immediately.

How to handle difficult exposures

Empathise; interim step if truly too big; never force; distinguish emotional overload (pause) from ordinary distress (continue).

How to plan homework

Mirror the in-session exposure; ~2 hrs/day; record SUDS every 10 min; same ritual-prevention rules; troubleshoot on the phone.

How to plan relapse prevention

Lapse ≠ relapse; know the warning signs; keep leaning in; run boosters; written action plan; therapist-contact option.
