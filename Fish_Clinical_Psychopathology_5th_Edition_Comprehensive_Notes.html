Chapter 1

Classification of Psychiatric Disorders

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 1 (pp. 1–10)

This opening chapter explains how psychiatry groups disorders and why the system is still imperfect. Fish begins with a frank admission: a truly definitive classification of any disease must be based on aetiology (the underlying cause), but because we rarely know the causes of mental illnesses, psychiatry adopts a pragmatic, syndrome-based approach instead.

Syndrome, Symptom and Disease

Definition — Syndrome A syndrome is a constellation of symptoms that is unique as a group. Individual symptoms may appear in other syndromes; it is the particular combination that makes a syndrome specific.

Simple explanation: a single symptom is one puzzle piece; a syndrome is a recognisable picture formed when several specific pieces regularly appear together. A disease is reached when that picture is shown to have one essential cause (often a confirmed physical basis).

Worked example from Fish — symptom → syndrome → disease Korsakoff's syndrome illustrates the whole progression. First Korsakoff noticed single symptoms (confabulation, impressionability) in people with alcohol dependence. Then a cluster was recognised (disorientation for time/place, euphoria, poor registration, confabulation, "tram-line" thinking). Finally, the discovery of consistent damage to the mammillary bodies confirmed it as a true disease with a neuropathological basis.

Primary vs Secondary Symptoms

Definitions Primary symptom = the immediate, direct result of the disease process (also used for a symptom not derived from any other psychological event).
Secondary symptom = a psychological elaboration of, or reaction to, a primary symptom.

Example: in mania the elevated mood is primary; the cheerfulness, over-activity, pressure of speech and flight of ideas can all be understood as arising from that elevated mood, so they are meaningfully coherent with it. This capacity to empathise with how symptoms arise underlies the primary/secondary distinction.

Early Distinctions: Organic vs Functional

The first major split was between disorders arising from brain disease (organic) and those with no obvious such basis (functional). Fish stresses these terms are now literally absurd — schizophrenia and manic-depression were called "functional", yet genetics and neuropathology show at least some organic basis — but they survive by tradition. DSM-IV renamed "organic mental syndromes" as "delirium, dementia, amnestic and other cognitive disorders."

Functional Syndromes, Neurosis & Psychosis

"Functional" means no readily apparent coarse brain disease. These were traditionally split into neuroses and psychoses.

Why this is an oversimplification (viva point) Many people with neurotic conditions have no insight and deny their illness; some people with schizophrenia willingly seek help. Personality can be badly changed by non-psychotic illness (e.g., depression) yet stay intact in some psychoses (e.g., persistent delusional disorder). Jaspers viewed neurosis as an abnormal response transformed by a defence mechanism (e.g., dissociation turning emotion into physical symptoms in conversion disorder). Schneider saw neuroses and personality disorders as quantitative variations of normal — but this breaks down for OCD, whose symptoms differ qualitatively from normal.

Terminology drift: "neurotic" and "psychotic" came to mean mild vs severe, or ego-syntonic (compatible with the self-concept, not distressing) vs ego-dystonic (distressing, incompatible with the self-concept). Because of the confusion, DSM-IV removed "neurosis" entirely (continued in DSM-5); ICD-11 replaced the old "neurotic, stress-related and somatoform" group with "anxiety and fear-related disorders", "disorders of bodily distress" and "disorders specifically associated with stress".

Personality Disorders & Psychogenic Reactions

Historically the English-speaking world separated neuroses from personality disorders; the German tradition (Schneider) saw neuroses as reactions of abnormal personalities to mild/moderate stress (or of normal personalities to severe stress). DSM-IV reflected the split using separate axes; the DSM-5 abolished the multiaxial system entirely. Psychogenic reactions were reversible, prolonged psychological responses to trauma shaped by personality and culture. The terms "psychogenic" and "neurotic" have been eliminated from recent DSM/ICD. Scandinavian "psychogenic psychoses" survive as acute and transient psychotic disorders (ICD-11) / brief psychotic disorder (DSM-5).

Key pointBoth DSM-5 and ICD-11 remain syndrome-based (built on co-occurring symptoms), not on aetiology, psychobiology or prognosis. As DSM-5 taskforce chair Kupfer admitted, no laboratory marker specifically identifies any DSM syndrome, comorbidity is very high, and treatment lacks specificity — so the hoped-for aetiology-based classification was never achieved. L1Define a syndrome and distinguish it from a disease.
A syndrome is a unique grouping of symptoms that cluster together; the combination is what makes it specific. A disease is reached when that syndrome is shown to have one essential (usually demonstrable physical) cause. Example: Korsakoff's progressed from symptom → syndrome → disease once mammillary-body damage was identified.
L2Why are the terms "organic" and "functional" considered outdated?
Because "functional" disorders (schizophrenia, manic-depression) increasingly show genetic and neuropathological bases, so the clean organic/functional dichotomy collapses. Fish calls their literal meaning "absurd" but notes they persist by tradition; DSM-IV renamed "organic mental syndromes" accordingly.
L3A researcher wants a homogeneous patient sample for a drug trial. Which classification and why?
DSM, because its operational, precisely defined criteria yield more homogeneous populations than ICD's descriptive, judgement-based approach. (Note the irony Fish mentions: US Medicare billing is mandated to use ICD codes, not DSM.)
L4Both DSM-5 and ICD-11 claim to be "atheoretical". Critically evaluate.
They are atheoretical about aetiology (deliberately dropping "psychogenic", "neurotic", "functional", "psychosomatic") except for stress-related and substance categories. However, being syndrome-based means they rest on an implicit assumption that co-occurring symptoms map onto real entities — an assumption undermined by absent biomarkers, pervasive comorbidity, diagnostic instability and non-specific treatment response (Kupfer). So "atheoretical" is better read as "aetiology-agnostic by necessity", not a positive scientific achievement.

Chapter 2

What Is Psychopathology? Controversies in Classifying Psychiatric Disorder

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 2 (pp. 11–23)

This chapter is the conceptual backbone of the whole book. It answers "what are we actually studying?" and tackles the honest controversies about whether psychiatric disorders are "real".

Jaspers and the Birth of Descriptive Psychopathology

Karl Jaspers — psychiatrist, theologian and philosopher — is called the father of psychopathology. His General Psychopathology argued that mental illness should be assessed by the form of the abnormal phenomena present (hallucination, delusion, thought disorder) rather than their content.

Definition — Form vs Content Form = the structural type of the experience (e.g., whether a delusion is primary/secondary, systematised/non-systematised).
Content = what the experience is about (e.g., persecutory, guilt-laden, nihilistic).

Simple explanation: two patients can both have a delusion (same form) but one believes the CIA is watching and the other believes they are damned (different content). Jaspers said the diagnostically useful fact is the presence of a delusion at all, not its storyline. The psychoanalytic school took the opposite view — that content is a clue to underlying trauma.

Two Broad Approaches: Philosophical and Clinical

Philosophical: is mental illness "real"? (after Kendler, 2016)

Three metaphysical stances on psychiatric disorder
StanceCore claimNote
RealismDisorders are real entities (like DNA or a tumour) awaiting discovery of their genes/pathwaysThe view of most psychiatrists and of biological psychiatry; Kendler cautiously leans here
ConstructivismDisorders have no biological reality; they are social constructs like fashions or music genresThe anti-psychiatry view; some non-antipsychiatry authors worry certain diagnoses arose from the social/political climate (e.g., debates over PTSD, recovered memory)
Pragmatism / utilityClassification is based on what works and is useful, not on what is "real" (Jablensky); a middle groundKendler calls it "unambitious" and raises ethical concerns; Wakefield fears it weakens the scientific basis of diagnosis
Supplementary — helpful terms from this chapter Pessimistic induction (Kuhn): each scientific generation believes it has reached the truth, yet is likely as mistaken as the last — illustrated in psychiatry by discarded "disorders" such as masturbatory insanity, monomania, paraphrenia, hysteria and (the historical declassification of) homosexuality. Coherence theory of truth: truth as a set of propositions that "hang together" (Spinoza, Kant, Hegel) — the hoped-for destination as more evidence accumulates.

Clinical: validity and reliability

Definitions Validity = the classification genuinely (a) distinguishes normal from abnormal states (in both non-psychotic and psychotic forms) and (b) distinguishes one disorder from another.
Reliability (replicability) = different clinicians reach the same diagnosis for the same patient.

Both are basic tenets of diagnosis throughout medicine. The problem: psychiatry has no independent biological markers, so clinicians depend on the patient's description of symptoms.

The "Clinical Significance" Criterion and False Positives

Definition — False positive diagnosis Diagnosing a disorder when none truly exists — e.g., labelling a normal grief response as a depressive episode on symptoms alone. Over-diagnosis inflates prevalence.

DSM-III/IV introduced the requirement that a disturbance cause "clinically significant distress or impairment" to reduce false positives — but never defined "clinically significant". Frances warned this hides "the seeds of tautology": symptoms count as a disorder because a doctor judges them to, and the doctor judges them to simply because they came to attention (a vicious circle). Large epidemiological surveys using lay interviewers compound this, producing false positives and unstable prevalence figures.

Supplementary — Wakefield's "zones of rarity" / explanatory discontinuity A valid disorder should show a gap ("zone of rarity") separating cases from non-cases — ideally not only in symptoms but also across aetiology, functioning, course, treatment response and prognosis. Where symptoms fail to delineate, discontinuity in these other domains (explanatory discontinuity) may still mark out a valid disorder. Fish notes most DSM-5/ICD-10 disorders do not fully meet these requirements.
Mnemonic — the chapter's spine "Jaspers Finds Causes Rarely Valid" → Jaspers (father) · Form over content · Constructivism/realism/pragmatism · Reliability · Validity + clinical significance.
L1Who is regarded as the father of psychopathology, and what was his central contribution?
Karl Jaspers. His central contribution was delineating the diagnostic features of mental illness by form rather than content, laying the foundation of descriptive psychopathology (in General Psychopathology).
L2Distinguish descriptive from explanatory psychopathology.
Descriptive psychopathology observes and describes the patient's subjective experience through form and content, deliberately avoiding explanation. Explanatory psychopathology (cognitive, behavioural, psychodynamic) attempts to explain why phenomena occur. Fish's book is descriptive.
L3How does the "clinical significance" criterion try to improve validity, and why is it criticised?
It requires that symptoms cause significant distress or impairment, aiming to screen out normal reactions and reduce false positives. It is criticised because "clinically significant" was never operationally defined, making it circular (Frances's "seeds of tautology") and unreliable, especially when applied by lay interviewers in epidemiology.
L4"Psychiatric disorders are social constructs." Defend and then rebut.
Defend (constructivism): few disorders have the demonstrable pathology of cancer or Huntington's despite decades of research; some categories appeared in response to social/political climate, and history shows discarded "disorders" (pessimistic induction). Rebut (realism/multifactorial): absence of a single marker does not prove unreality — like coronary artery disease, disorders may be defined by a coherent cluster of multifactorial properties (Kendler et al.), moving toward the coherence theory of truth; treating them as mere constructs risks the ethical harm of implying patients are "not really sick".
Cross-referencesForm/content recurs throughout: delusions (Ch 4), hallucinations (Ch 3). Normal vs abnormal emotion is developed in Chapter 6.

Chapter 3 · High-yield

Disorders of Perception

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 3 (pp. 24–41)

Perception is how the mind turns sensory stimuli into meaningful experience. Fish divides perceptual abnormalities into two great families:

  1. Sensory distortions — a real stimulus is perceived in a changed way (changed intensity, quality, spatial form, or time).
  2. Sensory deceptions — the perception does not match the stimulus. These are illusions (misinterpretation of a real external object) and hallucinations (perception without an adequate external object).

1 · Sensory Distortions

DefinitionSensory distortions are changes in perception resulting from a change in the intensity, quality or spatial form of a real stimulus.

Changes in intensity — hyper- / hypo-aesthesia

  • Hyperaesthesia (increased intensity of sensation) from intense emotion or a lowered physiological threshold — e.g., roof tiles seen as flaming red; a door closing heard like thunder. Hyperacusis (heightened sensitivity to noise) occurs in anxiety, depression, alcohol hangover and migraine. Very bright, intense colours occur in hypomania, epileptic aura, LSD use, and even in intense normal states (religious fervour, being in love).
  • Hypoacusis / hypoaesthesia (reduced sensation) in delirium (raised threshold + attentional defect → speak louder and slower to delirious patients), depression and attention-deficit disorder. In depression everything may look black or all food taste the same.

Changes in quality

Mainly visual, often drug-induced: xanthopsia (yellow), chloropsia (green), erythropsia (red). The metallic taste of lithium is a true change in gustation, not a hallucination. In derealisation everything looks unreal and strange; in mania objects can look perfect and beautiful.

Changes in spatial form — dysmegalopsia

Causes: retinal disease, disorders of accommodation/convergence, but most commonly temporal and parietal lobe lesions; rarely schizophrenia.

Distortions of the experience of time

Personal (not physical) time is affected by illness. In severe/psychotic depression time drags or seems to stand still; in mania it races. Some patients with schizophrenia feel time moves in fits and starts (sometimes with a delusional elaboration that clocks are interfered with) and show age disorientation. Acute organic states cause temporal disorientation.

2 · Sensory Deceptions — Illusions

Definition — IllusionA illusion is a misinterpretation of a real external stimulus: stimuli from a perceived object are combined with a mental image to produce a false perception.

Simple explanation: something is really there, but you read it wrongly — the shadow on a dark road becomes an attacker. Illusions are not in themselves pathological; they occur in healthy people, especially with inattention, strong emotion, or a raised perceptual threshold (as in delirium).

Three types of illusion (after Sims)

Viva trap — pareidolia is special Pareidolia differs from ordinary illusions because it needs no effort and no particular mood/mind-set — it is driven by vivid imagery. So it cannot be explained by affect or set, which is exactly why examiners ask about it.

3 · Hallucinations

Definitions — Hallucination Classic: "a perception without an object." Because that does not cover functional hallucinations (see below) and must exclude dreams, Jaspers defined it as "a false perception which is not a sensory distortion or a misinterpretation, but which occurs at the same time as real perceptions." SCAN simply calls them "false perceptions".

Simple explanation: the experience feels like a true perception coming from outside ("without"), but it actually comes from within. This "from within but experienced as from without" quality is what separates a hallucination from a vivid mental image (which also comes from within but is recognised as such).

Pseudo-hallucinations — the classic controversy

Definition — Pseudo-hallucination (Jaspers) A type of mental image that, although clear and vivid, lacks the substantiality of a true perception. It is experienced in full consciousness, is known not to be a real perception, is located in subjective (inner) space (e.g., "inside the head"), yet, like true hallucinations, is involuntary.
Two competing definitions (examiners love this) Jaspers: defined by inner vs outer space — pseudo-hallucinations sit in inner space and are variants of imagery, so lack the diagnostic weight of true hallucinations. Hare: defined by degree of insight — but insight fluctuates on a continuum, which (Fish notes) makes the whole concept of pseudo-hallucination largely redundant. Jaspers said there is no gradual transition between true and pseudo-hallucinations; Fish disagreed, arguing for a continuum (supported by Leff's sensory-deprivation work). SCAN avoids the term entirely but rates insight and inside/outside-the-head location separately.
Clinical significanceThe usual teaching is that pseudo-hallucinations do not necessarily indicate serious illness, whereas true hallucinations do — but this only holds if you fix a definition. If insight is the criterion and it fluctuates, the distinction loses meaning.

Causes of hallucinations

Intense emotion · psychiatric disorder · suggestion · disorders of a peripheral sense organ · sensory deprivation · CNS disorders.

  • Emotion: very depressed patients with guilt hear fragmentary, disjointed reproachful voices ("rotter", "kill yourself") — unlike the continuous voices of schizophrenia or organic hallucinosis. Continuous persistent voices in "depression" should raise suspicion of schizophrenia or physical disease.
  • Suggestion: normal people can be made to hallucinate (dim-corridor experiments, hypnosis).
  • Peripheral sense-organ disease: Charles Bonnet syndrome — complex visual hallucinations with reduced vision, no psychopathology, clear consciousness, common in the elderly; important as a differential.
  • Sensory deprivation: normal subjects hallucinate after hours of minimal stimulation; may contribute to post-cataract-surgery delirium and paranoid states in the deaf.
  • CNS: diencephalic/cortical lesions; hypnagogic & hypnopompic hallucinations are special organic types.

The sense of "presence"

Hard to classify — neither a true sensory deception nor a delusion. The vivid feeling that someone is present (often behind/beside one) though nothing is seen or heard. Occurs in health, organic states, schizophrenia and dissociation.

Fictional clinical example — distinguishing illusion, functional & reflex hallucination

Illustrative only. A patient reports: "When the extractor fan runs, I hear it and I hear a voice saying my name — two separate sounds." → Functional hallucination (same modality, stimulus still heard, hallucination gone when fan off).
Contrast: "At dusk I thought the coat on the door was a man." → Illusion (real stimulus transformed).
Contrast: "Every time I hear a door bang, I feel an electric shock in my spine." → Reflex hallucination (auditory stimulus → somatic hallucination, different modality).

Clarifying question: "When you hear the voice, is the fan noise still there at the same time, or does the fan noise turn into the voice?" (Still there + separate = functional; turns into = illusion.)

Viva Questions — Perception

L1Define hallucination, illusion and pseudo-hallucination.
Hallucination — a perception without an adequate external object, arising from within but experienced as coming from without, occurring alongside real perceptions (Jaspers). Illusion — a misinterpretation of a real external stimulus. Pseudo-hallucination — a vivid but non-substantial mental image, in inner (subjective) space, recognised as not real, experienced in full consciousness, but involuntary (Jaspers).
L2How do you distinguish a functional hallucination from an illusion?
Both arise in response to an environmental stimulus. In a functional hallucination the stimulus and the hallucination are perceived simultaneously and separately — the stimulus is not changed (e.g., the tap still sounds like a tap while a voice is also heard). In an illusion the stimulus itself is transformed and becomes an integral part of the new false perception.
L2Why is the modality of a hallucination diagnostically important?
Auditory hallucinations in clear consciousness suggest schizophrenia (or affective psychosis); fragmentary reproachful voices suggest depression. Visual hallucinations suggest an organic/acute confusional state and are uncommon in schizophrenia — their presence should prompt a search for organic cause or Charles Bonnet syndrome. Tactile formication suggests cocaine or alcohol states. So modality steers the differential.
L3A patient denies hearing voices but says "everybody here is talking about me in Greek." How do you proceed?
Explore whether this is (a) a delusion without a hallucination ("people talk about me" with no actual auditory experience), or (b) continuous hallucinatory voices attributed to real people (a delusional elaboration of a hallucination). Ask: "Do you actually hear them speaking, or do you just know they are talking about you?" Fish's Greek-patient vignette shows how careful content-analysis revealed continuous hallucinated Greek voices driving "motiveless" attacks — crucial for risk assessment.
L4Critically discuss the clinical utility of the concept of pseudo-hallucination.
Its utility depends entirely on the definition chosen. On Jaspers' spatial definition (inner vs outer space) it marks imagery-like experiences of lesser diagnostic weight. On Hare's insight definition it collapses, because insight varies on a continuum, rendering the category — and its supposed reassurance value — redundant. Fish sided with a continuum between pseudo- and true hallucinations (unlike Jaspers), and SCAN sidesteps the term, rating insight and location separately. Practical conclusion: record the actual phenomenology (location, insight, substantiality) rather than forcing a label.

Chapter 4 · High-yield

Disorders of Thought and Speech

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 4 (pp. 42–62)

Fish groups the disorders of thinking into: disorders of intelligence; disorders of the stream of thought; disorders of the possession of thought (obsessions, compulsions, thought alienation); disorders of the content of thought (delusions, overvalued ideas); and disorders of the form of thought (formal thought disorder). Speech disorders are treated at the end.

Master distinction for the whole chapterThought form = how thoughts are structured and connected (reasoning). Thought content = what is believed (belief). "Disorders of reasoning vs disorders of belief." Keep these apart in every answer.

Disorders of Intelligence

DefinitionIntelligence = the ability to think and act rationally and logically; measured by problem-solving and concept-formation using words, numbers, symbols and patterns. IQ tests are built to a mean of 100, SD 15.

Learning (intellectual) disability bands: borderline 70–90, mild 50–69, moderate 35–49, severe 20–34, profound <20. Dementia = loss of intelligence from brain disease, with disturbance of multiple cortical functions. The cognitive deficits of schizophrenia ("schizophrenic dementia" historically) are not a true dementia — they are part of the psychopathology.

Three legitimate uses of "to think"

  • Undirected fantasy ("autistic"/dereistic) thinking — daydreaming, not goal-bound.
  • Imaginative thinking — does not exceed the rational/possible.
  • Rational/conceptual thinking — aims to solve a problem.

Disorders of the Stream of Thought

Subdivided into tempo (speed) and continuity (flow).

Tempo — Flight of ideas

DefinitionFlight of ideas — thoughts follow each other rapidly with no general direction; successive thoughts are linked by chance connections that can usually still be understood (clang/verbal associations, assonance, alliteration, puns, proverbs). Speech is easily diverted by external stimuli and superficial internal associations.

Simple explanation: like dominoes — each thought shares just one "half" with the next, so you can follow each hop but there is no destination. Typical of mania.

Fish's own example (mania)Illustrative. Asked where she lived, a manic patient replied: "Birmingham, Kingstanding; see the king he's standing, king, king, sing, sing, bird on the wing, wing, wing on the bird, bird, turd, turd." — note the clang/rhyming chain.

Prolixity ("ordered flight of ideas") = a milder hypomanic form: many irrelevances and lively embellishment, but the patient can still return to the goal. Flight of ideas with pressure of speech is usual; without pressure of speech it is claimed in some mixed states.

Tempo — Inhibition / slowing of thinking

Train of thought slowed; fewer ideas/images; experienced as difficulty deciding, poor concentration, loss of clarity, often a strange "fuzziness in the head". Seen in depression and (rarely) manic stupor. May be mistaken for dementia.

Tempo — Circumstantiality

DefinitionCircumstantiality — thinking proceeds slowly with many unnecessary, trivial details, but the goal is eventually reached. The point is never lost; it is reached by a convoluted path.

Associated historically with epilepsy-related personality traits, also learning disability, obsessional traits, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder.

Continuity — Perseveration

DefinitionPerseveration — a mental operation (word, phrase or idea) persists beyond the point at which it is relevant, blocking further progress.

Example: asked the previous PM he says "David Cameron"; asked the current PM he again says "David Cameron. No, I mean David Cameron." Worse with harder tasks. Strongly suggests organic brain disease. Differs from verbal stereotypy (same word regardless of situation, not a volitional problem).

Continuity — Thought blocking

DefinitionThought blocking — a sudden arrest of the train of thought, leaving a "blank"; an entirely new thought may then begin.

In patients retaining insight it can be terrifying. When clearly present it is highly suggestive of schizophrenia — but exhaustion/anxiety can mimic losing one's thread.

Obsessions, Compulsions & Disorders of Possession of Thought

Definition — Obsession (rumination) A thought that persists and dominates despite the person recognising it as purposeless or beyond the point of usefulness. It appears against the will, is recognised as the person's own, is resisted, and is typically repugnant (so the prudish are tormented by sexual thoughts, the religious by blasphemy, the timid by violent images).

Compulsions = obsessional motor acts (e.g., washing driven by a contamination obsession). Because an obsession must be resistible and recognised as one's own, there are obsessional images, ideas, fears, impulses — but no obsessional hallucinations or moods. Contemporary shift: earlier writers stressed sexual obsessions; now fears of doing harm predominate (e.g., a mother fearing she will harm her baby).

Thought alienation

DefinitionThought alienation — the experience that one's thoughts are under external control or that others participate in one's thinking. Unlike obsessions, the thoughts are experienced as foreign and outside one's control.
Forms of thought alienation (now in ICD-11 schizophrenia criteria)
FormExperience
Thought insertionThoughts are put into the mind from outside and recognised as foreign
Thought withdrawal / deprivationThoughts suddenly disappear, withdrawn by a foreign influence (?subjective experience of thought blocking / "omission")
Thought broadcasting / diffusionAs one thinks, everyone else thinks in unison / thoughts escape and are accessible to others (several competing definitions — record verbatim)

Ego-syntonic vs ego-dystonic: as a person with psychosis gains insight, thought alienation tends to feel increasingly ego-dystonic and distressing.

Viva trap — obsession vs thought insertion Obsession: thought is recognised as my own, intrudes against my will, I resist it. Thought insertion: thought is experienced as not mine, placed by an external agent. Possession/ownership is the deciding feature.

Disorders of the Content of Thought — Delusions

Definition — Delusion A false, unshakeable belief that is out of keeping with the person's social and cultural background. Falseness is not its essential quality (a jealous delusion may be true by coincidence); the essence is that it is held with unusual conviction, is unmodifiable by evidence, and is not culturally sanctioned.

Definition — Overvalued ideaA thought that, because of its strong feeling tone, takes precedence over all other ideas and keeps that precedence for a long time. Less fixed than a delusion and with some basis in reality.

Primary delusions (apophany) — Schneider's three forms

FormDescription
Delusional mood (Wahnstimmung)A pervasive sense that "something is going on that concerns me", but the patient doesn't yet know what. Usually resolves into a delusional idea/perception.
Delusional perceptionA new, self-referential meaning attached to a normally perceived object, not derivable from mood/attitude. Schneider's "two-memberedness": link 1 = perceiving the object; link 2 = the new significance. E.g. "the spoon had a crown → I am of royal descent."
Sudden delusional idea (autochthonous delusion)A delusion appears fully formed, out of the blue ("brain-wave"). No two-memberedness — the delusion is contained within a single experience/memory.
Viva trap — delusional perception vs delusional misinterpretation A patient with persecutory delusions hears the stairs creak and "knows" it is a detective. That is delusional misinterpretation (derived understandably from the existing persecutory belief/mood), not a true delusional perception — because in a delusional perception the new meaning cannot be understood from the prevailing affect or attitudes.

Secondary delusions & systematisation

Arise understandably from other morbid experience (depressed mood, hallucinations, stress, personality). "Sensitive" personalities may develop an understandable paranoid psychosis (sensitiver Beziehungswahn, now a delusional disorder). In schizophrenia primary experiences get elaborated into a system ("delusional work"); delusions may be systematised (one basic delusion, logically built up) or non-systematised.

Content of delusions

  • Persecution — including delusions of reference (people talking about/spying on me), poisoning/infection (often "explanatory"), and influence (passivity "explained" by rays, hypnotism, the internet). Note: "paranoid" strictly means "delusional", not "persecutory".
  • Infidelity (morbid jealousy / Othello) — organic & functional; strongly linked to alcohol dependence; dangerous (violence, homicide).
  • Love / erotomania (de Clérambault / "fantasy lover syndrome").
  • Grandiose — variable, from "I am God" to inflated talents; historically GPI (neurosyphilis), now mania.
  • Ill health (hypochondriacal) — characteristic of depression; may involve family; overlaps body dysmorphic disorder.
  • Guilt — in severe depression; may become grandiose ("the most evil person ever").
  • Nihilistic (delusions of negation) — denying existence of body/mind/world; may be linked to delusions of enormity (e.g., refusing to urinate to avoid "flooding the world"). → Cotard syndrome.
  • Poverty — typical of depression (now less common).
The reality of delusionsNot all patients act on delusions; in chronic illness there is often a gap between belief and behaviour (the patient "who is God" happily stays as a voluntary inpatient). But guilt/hypochondriacal delusions (without retardation) and infidelity delusions carry real suicide/homicide risk.

Disorders of the Form of Thought (Formal Thought Disorder)

DefinitionFormal thought disorder (FTD) = disorder of conceptual/abstract thinking, most common in schizophrenia and organic brain disorders — a disturbance of how thoughts connect, not what they contain.
Classic descriptions of FTD

Schneider's three healthy-thinking features — constancy, organisation, continuity — map to three objective thought disorders:

  • Transitory thinking — derailments, substitutions, omissions; grammar/syntax disturbed. (Omission ≠ desultory: in omission the intention is interrupted leaving a gap.)
  • Drivelling thinking — all the constituents of a complex thought are present but muddled together; organisation lost.
  • Desultory thinking — grammatically correct speech, but simple intrusive ideas force their way in.

Aphasic paraphasias/word-dumbness stem from language apparatus damage. Viva Questions — Thought & Speech

L1Define a delusion. Is falseness its essential feature?
A false, unshakeable belief out of keeping with the person's social/cultural background. Falseness is not the essential feature — a jealous delusion may happen to be true; the essence is the abnormal certainty, unmodifiability and cultural incongruity, together with its arising independently of other morbid phenomena (if primary).
L2Differentiate flight of ideas from loosening of associations.
In flight of ideas the links between consecutive thoughts can still be understood (chance/clang/verbal associations), speed is increased, and it is typical of mania. In loosening of associations / derailment the links are not understandable — thought slides onto unrelated subsidiary tracks — and it is typical of schizophrenia and reflects formal thought disorder.
L2Obsession vs delusion vs overvalued idea — key separators?
Obsession: recognised as one's own, resisted, ego-dystonic, insight retained. Delusion: held with full conviction, unshakeable, not resisted, insight absent, culturally incongruent. Overvalued idea: strong feeling tone, dominant and long-lasting, plausible, with some basis in reality, less fixed than a delusion. The patient's attitude (resistance, conviction, ownership) is the discriminator.
L3At interview, how would you demonstrate a delusional perception rather than a delusional misinterpretation?
Elicit the perceived object and the meaning attached, then test whether the new meaning is understandable from the existing mood/beliefs. If the patient already feels persecuted and now "reads" a creaking stair as a spy, that is derivable → misinterpretation. If a completely neutral, normally perceived object suddenly acquires a self-referential meaning that cannot be derived from affect or prior attitude (Schneider's two-memberedness), that is a true delusional perception.
L4Thought broadcasting has several definitions. Why does this matter, and how should you document it?
Fish/Hamilton defined it as "everyone thinks in unison with me"; others define it as thoughts escaping/being accessible, sometimes requiring prior audibility (Gedankenlautwerden). Because the term covers slightly different experiences with different implications, you should record the patient's belief verbatim rather than just the label, so colleagues know whether an auditory component is present and can map it onto ICD-11 criteria accurately.

Disorders of Memory

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 5 (pp. 63–70)

Memory types: sensory (fades in seconds) → short-term / working → long-term. The process of remembering has four parts: registration, retention, retrieval, recall. The hippocampus stores explicit (declarative) memory and transfers short- to long-term; implicit (procedural) memory lives in the limbic system, amygdala and cerebellum.

Memory subtypes

L1Define anterograde and retrograde amnesia.
Retrograde = loss of memory for events before the injury/illness onset; anterograde = inability to form/recall new memories for events after it (failure of encoding).
L3How would you distinguish confabulation from pseudologia fantastica at interview?
Look for organic cognitive impairment and clear consciousness (confabulation) vs an intact sensorium with a dramatic crisis context (pseudologia). Confabulation fills genuine memory gaps and is not corrected; pseudologia is fluent, grandiose, and the person concedes untruth when confronted with incontrovertible evidence. Corroborate with cognitive testing and collateral history.
L4Comment on the forensic significance of false memory and dissociative amnesia.
Memory is reconstructive, not a camera; retrospective falsification, source amnesia and suggestibility can generate confident false memories (even false confessions via memory-distrust syndrome). Dissociative amnesia for offences is hard to separate from malingering. Hence the need for corroboration, caution with "recovered memories" (the false-memory debate), and awareness that statutes of limitation partly reflect memory's fallibility.

Chapter 6

Disorders of Emotion

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 6 (pp. 71–79)

Core vocabulary Feeling = subjective positive/negative reaction to an experience. Emotion = a stirred-up state with physiological change. Mood = a pervasive and sustained emotion that colours one's view of the world (describe intensity, duration, fluctuation, type). Affect = short-lived present emotional responsiveness, inferred by the clinician from body language/face; described as within normal range, constricted, blunt or flat — and may be congruent or incongruent with mood.

Fish organises emotion into five clinical levels: (1) normal emotional reactions, (2) abnormal emotional reactions, (3) abnormal expressions of emotion, (4) morbid expressions of emotion, (5) morbid disorders of emotion.

1 · Normal emotional reactions

Understandable, culturally appropriate responses (grief; distress at a serious diagnosis). Normal grief usually settles over ~6–12 months, with only brief functional incapacity.

2 · Abnormal emotional reactions

Understandable but with prolonged functional impairment (the Yerkes–Dodson curve: performance improves then deteriorates past a stress threshold). Diagnostically = adjustment disorders.

  • Anxiety — "fear for no adequate reason"; may have physical symptoms; sudden combined physical symptoms = panic attack.
  • Anxious foreboding — a sense something terrible will happen without knowing what.
  • Phobia — fear restricted to one object/situation/idea, with avoidance.
  • Reactivity of mood — mood fluctuates with the environment.
  • Demoralisation — isolation, despair, feeling trapped; source seen as external, anhedonia and guilt absent, motivation intact (unlike depression). Studied in cancer.

3 · Abnormal expressions of emotion (patient is aware of them)

TermMeaning
Dissociation of affectLack of emotional response where some is expected (numbness) — unconscious defence against overwhelming stress; underlies the "unresponsive" feeling in depersonalisation/derealisation
La belle indifférenceSerene lack of concern about gross conversion symptoms
DenialUnaware refusal to acknowledge a clear event (distinguish from the conscious avoidance it is often wrongly used for)
ApathyEmotional indifference / lack of motivation (schizophrenia, cannabis amotivational syndrome, deprivation) — not the same as dissociation of affect
PerplexityBewildered, puzzled state (anxiety, mild clouding, emerging schizophrenia)

4 · Morbid expressions of emotion (patient unaware of the morbidity)

  • Blunting / inadequacy of affect ("parathymia", Bleuler) — loss of emotional life, social inappropriateness (schizophrenia).
  • Incongruity of affect — emotion mismatched to context (laughing at bad news).
  • Flattening / constriction — reduced range, but what is shown is in the appropriate direction (unlike incongruity).
  • Lability of affect — rapid abrupt mood changes unrelated to stimuli (mixed states, mania, borderline PD, frontal/cerebrovascular damage).
  • Affective incontinence — total loss of control (cerebral atherosclerosis, MS); "forced laughing/weeping" with a mismatch between expression and subjective feeling.
  • The "smiling depressive" — retains the communicatory smile but the eyes stay tense/unchanged; a dangerous pitfall in suicide-risk assessment.

5 · Morbid disorders of emotion

Self-sustaining pathological states (don't resolve when a stressor is removed). Depressive illness is the commonest: morbid sadness ("a weight/cloud/darkness"), psychomotor retardation, anhedonia (loss of the capacity for interest/pleasure; term coined by Ribot, 1896), loss of emotional resonance (feeling of unreality, inability to feel), diurnal variation, early-morning wakening; may reach depressive stupor. Morbid anxiety can present as agitation. Morbid euphoria/elation in mania/hypomania (unrelated to events, not modified by sad influences; pressure of speech, flight of ideas, disinhibition). Ecstasy = exalted rapturous state (religious experience, post-partum) — distinct from mere elation. Frontal euphoria with silliness/lack of foresight = moria / Witzelsucht.

Viva trap — mania vs hypomania (Fish) The distinction lies in the presence of psychotic symptoms (typically grandiose delusions) and/or "marked" functional impairment in mania — though DSM-5 does not define "marked".
Mnemonic — five levels of emotion"Normal Always Avoids Morbid Madness" → Normal reactions → Abnormal reactions → Abnormal expressions → Morbid expressions → Morbid disorders.
L1Distinguish mood from affect.
Mood is a pervasive, sustained emotion that colours the person's whole outlook (the "climate"); affect is the immediate, observable, short-lived emotional responsiveness inferred from the patient's face and body (the "weather"). Affect may be congruent or incongruent with mood.
L2Differentiate blunting, flattening and incongruity of affect.
Blunting/inadequacy = loss of emotional finesse/social sensitivity. Flattening/constriction = reduced range, but responses still point the right way. Incongruity = emotion in the wrong direction for the context (laughing at tragedy). All are "morbid expressions" because the patient is unaware of them.
L3How does demoralisation differ from a depressive episode, and why does it matter?
Demoralisation: external source, feeling trapped/uncertain about direction, but anhedonia and guilt absent and motivation preserved. Depression: pervasive low mood, anhedonia, guilt, loss of emotional resonance, often diurnal/biological features. It matters because demoralisation (common in cancer) may not respond to antidepressants and calls for meaning-centred/psychological support rather than automatic pharmacotherapy.
L4Why is the "smiling depressive" clinically dangerous?
The retained communicatory smile (lips but not eyes) can beguile the clinician into underestimating depth of depression and suicide risk. Fish advises probing the most sensitive areas and watching for depression to break through; expressions of empathy may unmask it. This is a documentation and risk-assessment hazard.

Chapter 7

Disorders of the Experience of Self

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 7 (pp. 80–85)

Jaspers described four aspects of self-experience (Ichbewusstsein / "self-experience"):

  1. Awareness of the existence and activity of the self.
  2. Awareness of being a unity at any moment in time.
  3. Awareness of continuity of identity over time.
  4. Awareness of being separate from the environment (ego boundaries).

Disturbance of awareness of self-activity

Definition — Depersonalisation The patient feels they are no longer their normal, natural self, often with a feeling of unreality. It is always, to some degree, unpleasant (which separates it from ecstatic states).
Definition — Derealisation The associated feeling that the environment is flat, dull, distant and unreal.

Simple explanation: depersonalisation = "I feel unreal / like a robot / cut off from myself"; derealisation = "the world around me feels unreal / like a stage set / behind glass." Both occur with retained insight and clear consciousness (ICD-11/DSM-5 depersonalisation–derealisation disorder); roughly twice as common in women; reported at least once by 30–70% of young adults.

Viva trap — depersonalisation vs nihilistic delusion Depersonalisation is not a delusion and insight is retained ("I feel as if I don't exist"). A nihilistic delusion is a mood-congruent belief held with conviction ("I am dead / I have no brain", as in Cotard). Severe depression can occasionally present with prominent depersonalisation and be mistaken for schizophrenia because the patient struggles to describe it.

Loss of emotional resonance — in depression the normal flow of feeling toward people/objects is lost ("I cannot feel"), often most painful toward loved ones, intensifying guilt.

Disturbance of immediate awareness of self-unity

Feeling "as if" one is two people (depersonalisation/depression). People with appreciation-needing personalities or learning disability may drop the "as if". Patients with certain delusions (e.g., demoniac possession) or schizophrenia may feel they are two people.

Disturbance of continuity of self

Feeling of not being the same person as before ("I died under my old name and came to life as a new person"; "born again"). Rarely, dissociative identity disorder (multiple personality) — strongly linked to trauma/childhood abuse; differentials include other dissociative disorders, schizophrenia, bipolar, borderline PD, malingering, complex partial epilepsy.

Disturbance of the boundaries of the self (ego boundaries)

Many schizophrenic symptoms represent a breakdown of the self/world boundary:

  • Passivity phenomena ("made"/"fabricated" experiences) — actions, impulses or feelings experienced as made by an external force ("I am a guided missile"). The patient knows the act is not their own.
  • Thought alienation (insertion/withdrawal/broadcasting) — the thinking equivalent (see Ch 4).
  • Belief that one's actions/thoughts have excessive effect on the world ("when I urinate, bad things happen to others").
Viva trap — passivity vs obsession vs "as-if" anxiety In obsession/compulsion, control over the act is lost but ownership is retained (it is still "my" impulse, resisted). In passivity, both control and ownership are lost — the act is "made" by an outside agency. In severe anxiety, a patient may say "as if" they are controlled; the preserved "as if" and intact insight separate this from true passivity.

Theory of mind, consciousness & schizophrenia

Theory of mind / mentalisation = ability to infer others' mental states. Deficits are linked to autism and to paranoid symptoms (not specific to, nor invariable in, schizophrenia). Sass & Parnas argue schizophrenia is fundamentally a self-disorder (increased self-consciousness, diminished self-affection).

Mnemonic — Jaspers' 4 aspects of self"A-U-C-B" = Activity/existence, Unity, Continuity, Boundaries.
Eliciting (Fish's probes)

"Are there times when you feel you are no longer your normal self, or a feeling of unreality about yourself or the world?" · "Do you sometimes feel as if you are living in a dream, or the world is less than real?" · "Are your thoughts, emotions and actions under your control, or does it ever feel as if some are controlled from outside?"

L1Define depersonalisation and derealisation.
Depersonalisation — the unpleasant experience of feeling unreal, detached from or no longer one's natural self, with insight retained. Derealisation — the associated feeling that the external world is unreal, flat and distant.
L2How do passivity phenomena differ from obsessions?
In passivity, ownership of the thought/action/feeling is lost — it is experienced as made by an external agent. In obsession, ownership is retained (it is recognised as one's own intrusive thought) even though control is impaired and it is resisted.
L3A depressed patient says "I don't exist any more." How do you decide if this is depersonalisation or a nihilistic delusion?
Probe the quality of conviction and insight. Depersonalisation: an "as-if" quality with retained insight ("I feel unreal, though I know I'm here"). Nihilistic delusion: a held belief with full conviction, unshakeable, mood-congruent ("I am dead / have no insides"). Clarify with gentle reality-testing and look at the surrounding depressive picture.
L4Evaluate the claim that schizophrenia is fundamentally a disorder of the self.
Sass & Parnas propose a unified "ipseity disturbance" account — hyper-reflexivity (increased self-consciousness) and diminished self-affection — that can generate passivity, thought alienation and boundary loss. It elegantly links disparate first-rank-type symptoms to a single self-boundary disturbance and connects to theory-of-mind research. Limitations: theory-of-mind deficits are neither specific to nor universal in schizophrenia, and the model remains descriptive/phenomenological rather than established at a mechanistic level.

Chapter 8

Motor Disorders

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 8 (pp. 86–102)

Mostly objective motor signs, but Fish opens with a subjective one.

Subjective motor disorder — alienation of motor acts

In obsessions/compulsions, ownership of the act is retained though it appears against the will. In schizophrenia, the act may be experienced as foreign/made by an outside influence — ideas or delusions of passivity, often "explained" by rays, hypnosis, the internet. Severe anxiety can produce "as-if" versions — distinguishing these from true passivity is crucial to avoid misdiagnosis.

Classification of objective motor disorders

Adaptive movements · non-adaptive movements · motor speech disturbances · disorders of posture · abnormal complex behaviour · medication-related movement disorders.

Disorders of adaptive movements

  • Expressive movements: reduced in depression (incl. "smiling depression" mask); stiff masked face in catatonia; "ointment face" in post-encephalitic Parkinsonism; Schnauzkrampf (snout spasm). Exaggerated in mania.
  • Reactive movements: automatic adjustments to new stimuli; lost/obstructed in catatonia and stupor; prompt/excessive in anxiety.
  • Goal-directed movements: psychomotor retardation (depression — like a steady brake) vs obstruction/blocking (Sperrung) (catatonia — like a rod in the spokes: irregular hindrance; the patient may start then stop halfway, or respond only at the last moment). Mannerism = an odd, stilted but still goal-directed movement/posture.
Viva trap — mannerism vs stereotypy Mannerism = an unusual, stilted variation of a goal-directed movement (the goal is still visible). Stereotypy = a repetitive, non-goal-directed movement/posture carried out uniformly. Mannerisms are not diagnostic of any disorder.

L1Define waxy flexibility.
A form of catatonic posturing in which the examiner feels a smooth, plastic ("soft-wax") resistance while moving the patient's limb, after which the imposed, often uncomfortable, posture is maintained.
L2Differentiate mannerism from stereotypy.

L3At the bedside, how would you distinguish catatonic from depressive stupor?
Examine facial expression (deadpan vs depressed), muscle tone (increased/variable + obstruction vs normal), presence of catalepsy/stereotypies/waxy flexibility (catatonic) and incontinence (catatonic). Test emotional reactivity: the depressive stuporose patient becomes more distressed on affect-laden topics, whereas the catatonic patient typically shows no emotional response. Always exclude organic/neurological causes (EEG, imaging, LP) in rapidly developing stupor.
L4Why must movement disorders be examined before starting antipsychotics?
Because spontaneous dyskinesias and neurological soft signs occur in drug-naïve first-episode schizophrenia; a baseline examination (and validated rating scales) separates illness-related from drug-induced movements, informs later causality judgements about tardive dyskinesia, and supports prevention/management — important given evidence that such movement disorders affect quality of life and may be mortality risk factors.

Chapter 9

Disorders of Consciousness

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 9 (pp. 103–107)

DefinitionConsciousness (for descriptive psychopathology) = a state of awareness of the self and the environment. Its intensity varies even in the fully awake person.

Attention first

Active attention (deliberate focus) vs passive attention (stimuli grab attention effortlessly) — reciprocally related. Disturbed active attention = distractibility (fatigue, anxiety, depression, mania, schizophrenia, organic states).

Orientation and "confusion"

Orientation is tested in time → place → person — and consciousness disturbance tends to erode them in that order (person lost last). Disorientation in a recent-onset illness is a prima facie sign of an acute organic state. "Confusion" strictly means disoriented for time and place (though it is loosely used for "muddled/bewildered/perplexed", which also occur in severe anxiety and acute schizophrenia without disorientation).

Key pointConsciousness can change in three basic ways: it may be dream-like, depressed (lowered), or restricted.

1 · Dream-like change of consciousness (delirium)

Lowered consciousness (raised threshold for stimuli); disoriented for time and place but not person; visual hallucinations prominent (small animals, fear/terror); cannot separate mental images from perceptions; disordered dream-like thinking (displacement, condensation, misuse of symbols). Lilliputian hallucinations (with pleasure) may occur. Occupational delirium = carrying out the actions of one's trade. Worse at night; "toxic confusional state" is a milder form. ICD-11 standardises delirium terminology (rapid onset, fluctuating course).

2 · Lowering of consciousness (torpor)

Psychologically benumbed, slowed, apathetic, may perseverate, without hallucinations/illusions/delusions/restlessness. Historically from severe infection; now often post-stroke. May be mistaken for severe dementia but shows remarkable partial recovery.

3 · Restriction of consciousness (twilight state)

Definition — Twilight stateA condition with restriction of morbidly changed consciousness, a break in the continuity of consciousness, and relatively well-ordered behaviour; sudden onset and end, variable duration, sometimes unexpected violent/emotional behaviour. The commonest cause is epilepsy.

Hysterical twilight state = restriction from unconscious motives; the person may "forget" personal identity as a temporary escape. Fugues (wandering + amnesia) are not all hysterical — a depressed person may wander before attempting suicide. ICD-11 places dissociative fugue under dissociative disorders.

Mnemonic — three ways consciousness changes"D-L-R" = Dream-like (delirium) · Lowered (torpor) · Restricted (twilight). Orientation lost in order "T-P-P" = Time → Place → Person.
Eliciting (Fish's probes)

"Are there times you feel especially distractible, as if you cannot focus?" · "Do you find it hard to remember what time/day it is, or where you are, or who the people around you are?" · "Do you sometimes feel as if you are living in a dream, or drifting without focus?"

L1In what order is orientation lost as consciousness is impaired?
Time first, then place, then person (person is retained longest because people supply identifying information).
L2Name and distinguish the three basic changes of consciousness.
Dream-like (delirium): raised threshold, prominent visual hallucinations, fear, image/perception confusion. Lowered (torpor): benumbed, slowed, apathetic, no hallucinations/restlessness. Restricted (twilight state): consciousness narrowed to a few ideas, broken continuity, relatively ordered behaviour, often epileptic.
L3Why can delirium be mistaken for dementia, and how do you avoid the error?
Both show poor grasp and cognitive failure, but delirium has acute onset, fluctuating (often night-worse) course, clouded/dream-like consciousness and perceptual disturbance, whereas dementia is chronic with clear consciousness. Check onset and course, test orientation and attention, look for fluctuation and visual hallucinations, and investigate for organic causes (bloods, imaging, EEG). Torpor and institutionalised chronic schizophrenia are further mimics.
L4Discuss the difficulty of attributing a twilight/fugue state to "hysterical" motives.
Twilight states can be organic (classically epileptic) or psychogenic; fugues may be dissociative, depressive (pre-suicidal wandering) or malingered. It is often impossible to be certain how much motivation is unconscious vs deliberate, and prior head injury/concussion may shape a dissociative presentation. Hence cautious formulation, exclusion of epilepsy/organic causes, collateral history and avoidance of prematurely labelling behaviour "hysterical".

Chapter 10

Personality Disorders

Fish's Clinical Psychopathology, 5th ed., Chapter 10 (pp. 108–121)

Definitions Personality = the totality of a person's emotional and behavioural traits characterising day-to-day living.
Personality disorder = deeply ingrained, maladaptive patterns of behaviour, usually recognisable by adolescence and continuing through adult life.

History: Hippocrates' four humours → four temperaments (choleric, melancholic, sanguine, phlegmatic). Schneider (greatest modern influence) defined the person with personality disorder as one who "suffers or makes society suffer on account of their abnormality."

Dimensions vs categories

Trait psychology places traits on a continuum from absent → severe; only traits crossing a severity threshold (harming self or others) are pathological. Most studies find 4–5 dimensions (e.g., Costa & McCrae's five-factor model: neuroticism, extraversion, openness, agreeableness, conscientiousness). ICD-11 abandons categories for a dimensional severity model.

Assessing personality — the golden rule

Critical assessment principle Assess personality when the person has recovered from the current episode of illness, because mental-state disorder contaminates the picture (a depressed person may describe lifelong worthlessness; hypomanic irritability may look like irascibility). If assessing during illness, rely on informants who know the premorbid, lifelong traits. A trait is only a disorder if it negatively impacts the person and/or others.

Instruments mentioned: MMPI, Eysenck Personality Inventory (affected by current illness via the N scale); screeners (IPDS, SAPAS, PAS-Q); questionnaires (MCMI-IV, PDQ-4, NEO-PR — risk of over-diagnosis/false positives); structured interviews (PAS, DIPD-IV, SIDP, Personality Disorder Examination, SCID-5-PD). Reliability is higher with informants than with subjects alone.

L1Define personality disorder.
A deeply ingrained, maladaptive pattern of behaviour, generally recognisable by adolescence and persisting through adult life, that causes the person to suffer and/or makes others suffer (Schneider).
L2Why should personality be assessed after recovery from illness?
Because a current mental-state disorder contaminates self-report and presentation — depression can mimic lifelong low self-worth, hypomania can mimic irascibility. Lifelong premorbid traits, ideally confirmed by informants, are what define personality; this reduces false-positive personality-disorder diagnoses.
L3How would you separate avoidant PD from social anxiety disorder?
Avoidant PD is more pervasive (fear across many social situations) with pervasive low self-esteem and an excessive need for acceptance as part of a lifelong trait pattern; social anxiety disorder is more circumscribed (e.g., public speaking/eating in front of others) without the global low self-worth. Fish notes considerable overlap, so some view avoidant PD as a mild/severe end of social anxiety.
L4Compare the ICD-11 and DSM-5 approaches to personality disorder and critique them.
DSM-5 retains the 10 categorical diagnoses in 3 clusters despite poor inter-rater reliability (except antisocial) and heavy overlap/comorbidity. ICD-11 abandons categories for a severity-based dimensional model with five trait domains plus a borderline qualifier — more empirically grounded but clinically less familiar and harder to navigate. The anomalous retention of the borderline qualifier (critiqued by Tyrer) and omission of narcissistic PD show the compromises involved. The dimensional move better captures the trait continuum and comorbidity but at some cost to clinical communicability.

L2Distinguish projection from projective identification.

Splitting is the inability to integrate good and bad, so people are seen as all-good or all-bad. In borderline PD it causes shifting idealisation/devaluation of staff, setting team members against one another (e.g., nurses vs doctors) — hence the need for team awareness and consistency.

Common documentation errors
  • Writing a diagnosis instead of a sign ("psychotic") rather than describing the phenomenon.
  • Recording content as if it were form ("heard a voice saying X" without stating modality, 2nd/3rd person, clarity, insight).

  • Labelling resistance "negativism" when it is actually goal-directed refusal.

Long-Answer Questions — Structured Frameworks

Skeletons for essay/long-viva answers. Expand each heading with the chapter detail above.

Q1 · "Discuss the classification and clinical significance of hallucinations."
Framework: (1) Define (Jaspers; SCAN). (2) Distinguish from illusion, mental image, pseudo-hallucination. (3) Classify by modality (auditory/visual/olfactory/gustatory/tactile/visceral) with diagnostic pointers. (4) Special types (functional, reflex, extracampine, autoscopy, hypnagogic/hypnopompic). (5) Hallucinatory syndromes (alcoholic, organic). (6) Causes (emotion, suggestion, sense-organ disease, deprivation, CNS). (7) Clinical significance: modality steers differential; visual → organic; continuous auditory in clear consciousness → schizophrenia; risk from imperative/persecutory voices. (8) Non-clinical hallucinatory-like experiences. (9) Conclude on careful phenomenological assessment over labels.
Q2 · "Compare and contrast delusions, overvalued ideas and obsessions."
Framework: Define each. Tabulate ownership, insight, resistance, fixity, plausibility, cultural congruence. Primary vs secondary delusions (Schneider's three primary experiences). Overvalued idea's feeling tone and reality basis. Obsession's ego-dystonic, resisted quality. Differential pitfalls (OCD with poor insight vs delusion; morbid jealousy as delusion vs overvalued idea). Clinical/forensic significance (risk). Conclude: the attitude of the patient, not content, is decisive.
Q3 · "Describe the motor disorders seen in catatonia."
Framework: Subjective (passivity) vs objective. Adaptive (obstruction/Sperrung, mannerisms). Non-adaptive (stereotypy, perseveration, verbigeration). Induced/compliance spectrum (automatic obedience, echolalia/echopraxia, Mitmachen, Mitgehen, Gegenhalten, negativism, ambitendency, forced grasping). Posture (waxy flexibility, catalepsy, psychological pillow, Schnauzkrampf). Complex behaviour (stupor vs excitement). Ungvari's four syndromal classes. Conclude with examination technique and need to exclude organic causes.
Q4 · "Outline the disorders of memory and their clinical and forensic relevance."
Framework: Memory types/processes. Amnesias (organic: retrograde/anterograde, amnestic state/Korsakoff, Ribot's law; psychogenic: dissociative, katathymic). Paramnesias of recall (retrospective falsification, false memory, screen memory, confabulation, pseudologia, cryptamnesia, delusional memory) and recognition (déjà/jamais vu, false recognition, Capgras). Hyperamnesia/flashbacks. Forensic relevance (false memory, false confession, dissociative amnesia vs malingering, memory's reconstructive nature).
Q5 · "Describe the levels of disordered emotion in Fish, with examples."
Framework: Vocabulary (feeling/emotion/mood/affect). Five levels: normal reactions → abnormal reactions (adjustment; anxiety, phobia, demoralisation; Yerkes–Dodson) → abnormal expressions (dissociation of affect, belle indifférence, denial, apathy, perplexity) → morbid expressions (blunting, incongruity, flattening, lability, affective incontinence, smiling depressive) → morbid disorders (depressive illness with anhedonia/retardation/loss of resonance; morbid anxiety; euphoria/ecstasy; moria). Clinical significance (suicide risk, differentiating normal from pathological).

Clinical Case-Based Viva (Fictional Illustrations)

All vignettes are invented for teaching. Do not diagnose from a single symptom.

Case 1 · The "voice from the tap"
Vignette: A 28-year-old says that only when the bathroom tap runs does he also hear a voice naming him; he hears the water and the voice as separate sounds; when the tap is off, the voice stops.
Phenomenon: Functional hallucination.
Supporting features: stimulus and hallucination perceived together, same modality, stimulus not transformed, hallucination stimulus-dependent.
Rule out: illusion (stimulus would be transformed); reflex hallucination (different modality).
Clarifying question: "When the voice comes, can you still hear the water at the same time, or does the water become the voice?"
Model answer: "This is a functional hallucination, not an illusion, because the real stimulus (running water) is perceived simultaneously with and separately from the hallucination, which is abolished when the stimulus stops."
Case 2 · Multiple phenomena together
Vignette: A 35-year-old describes: (a) thoughts suddenly vanishing mid-sentence "as if someone deletes them"; (b) a certainty that a stranger on TV is in love with her; (c) she dates this belief to "always", though her family say it began 3 weeks ago.
Phenomena: (a) thought withdrawal (thought alienation) — possibly experienced as thought blocking; (b) erotomania (de Clérambault); (c) retrospective delusion (backdating).
Distinguish: (a) thought withdrawal (foreign agency) vs ordinary losing one's thread (fatigue); (b) erotomania vs an overvalued romantic preoccupation (test conviction/fixity); (c) genuine chronicity vs retrospective delusional dating (collateral history).
Clarifying questions: "When the thought disappears, do you feel something/someone takes it?"; "How do you know this person loves you?"; corroborate timeline with family.
Case 3 · Stupor on the ward
Vignette: A mute, immobile patient; the examiner raises the arm to take the pulse and it remains suspended after release; the face is deadpan but the eyes track the examiner; incontinent of urine.
Phenomenon: Catatonic stupor with catalepsy (and likely increased tone/obstruction).
Distinguish from depressive stupor: depressive stupor shows a depressed facies, normal tone, no catalepsy/stereotypy, preserved emotional reactivity, no incontinence.
Clarifying/examination steps: test for waxy flexibility, Mitgehen, Gegenhalten; probe emotional reactivity on affect-laden topics; always exclude organic causes (bloods, imaging, EEG, LP) in rapidly developing stupor.
Case 4 · Confabulation vs lying
Vignette: A 60-year-old with a long alcohol history fills gaps in his recent memory with detailed but untrue "events", maintained in clear consciousness, not corrected when challenged; he is apathetic with poor recent recall.
Phenomenon: Confabulation (suggestive of Korsakoff's amnestic syndrome).
Distinguish: pseudologia fantastica (non-organic, admits lying on proof); ordinary lying (conscious, for gain); delusional memory (held with psychotic conviction).
Clarifying steps: formal memory testing, collateral history, assess for thiamine deficiency; note the clear consciousness + organic context pointing to confabulation.
Case 5 · "The world feels unreal"
Vignette: A 22-year-old with panic attacks says the world looks "flat, like behind glass" and she feels "like a robot watching herself"; she knows this is not actually true and finds it distressing.
Phenomena: Derealisation (world) + depersonalisation (self); insight retained.
Distinguish: nihilistic (Cotard) delusion — would be held with conviction ("I am dead"); schizoid detachment — lifelong, non-distressing.
Clarifying question: "Do you truly believe you are unreal, or does it just feel that way while you know it isn't?" (insight retained → depersonalisation, not delusion).
