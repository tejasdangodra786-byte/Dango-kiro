SECTION 1: INTRODUCTION TO IPDE

1.1 What is the IPDE?

The International Personality Disorder Examination (IPDE) is a semi-structured clinical interview developed to assess personality disorders according to the criteria of the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV). It is the first instrument of its kind designed specifically for reliable cross-cultural assessment of personality disorders worldwide.

Development Process:

  1. Beginning in 1985, several international workshops were convened
  2. Representatives from the international psychiatric community discussed format, wording, and scoring
  3. Translations were undertaken and frequent revisions made based on interviewer experience
  4. A major international field trial was conducted (1988–1990) at 14 clinical facilities
  5. In August 1991, principal investigators met at WHO headquarters in Geneva to finalize revisions
  6. Additional modifications were made for the DSM-III-R to DSM-IV transition

1.3 Purpose and Scope

The IPDE serves the following purposes:

  • Standardized diagnosis of personality disorders according to ICD-10 and DSM-IV criteria
  • Cross-cultural assessment of personality pathology in diverse populations
  • Research applications requiring reliable, replicable case identification
  • Clinical assessment providing both categorical diagnoses and dimensional scores
  • Training tool for clinicians learning personality disorder assessment
The IPDE is NOT designed to: Survey the entire realm of normal personality; Assess neutral, positive, or adaptive traits; Diagnose other mental disorders (Axis I conditions); Replace comprehensive psychiatric evaluation.

2.2 Screening Questionnaire

ICD-10 Screening Questionnaire:

  • Number of Items: 59 True/False statements
  • Purpose: To identify subjects who are unlikely to have a personality disorder, thereby reducing the number requiring the full interview
  • Format: Self-administered paper-and-pencil questionnaire
  • Response Options: TRUE (T) or FALSE (F)
  • Time to Complete: Approximately 10–15 minutes
Instructions to Subject: “The purpose of this questionnaire is to learn what type of person you have been during the past five years. Please do not skip any items. If you are not sure of an answer, select the one – TRUE or FALSE – which is more likely to be correct. There is no time limit, but do not spend too much time thinking about the answer to any single statement.”

Screening Questionnaire Scoring:

  • Items are allocated to specific personality disorders
  • Each disorder has a designated set of screening items
  • Threshold: If 3 or more items from a disorder are endorsed in the keyed direction, the subject has “failed” the screen for that disorder and should be interviewed
  • Clinicians may adopt lower (more sensitive) or higher (more specific) screening standards
Critical Note: The screening questionnaire should NEVER be used to: Make a psychiatric diagnosis; Calculate dimensional scores; Replace the semi-structured interview.

2.3 Semi-Structured Clinical Interview – Six Domains

Domain 1: WORK

Occupational functioning and productivity, perfectionism and task completion, preoccupation with details, rules, organization, avoidance of occupational activities involving interpersonal contact. Items assess Anankastic and Anxious criteria.

Domain 2: SELF

Self-image and identity, goals and aims in life, internal preferences (values, morality, sexual orientation), decision-making capacity, doubt and caution. Items assess Emotionally Unstable-Borderline, Dependent, and Anankastic criteria.

Domain 3: INTERPERSONAL RELATIONSHIPS

Quality and stability of relationships, trust and suspiciousness, social avoidance and anxiety, attachment patterns, assertiveness and compliance. Items assess Paranoid, Anxious, Dependent, Dissocial, Histrionic, and Schizoid criteria.

Domain 4: AFFECTS

Emotional range and expression, mood stability, anxiety and tension, emptiness and abandonment fears, anger and irritability. Items assess Emotionally Unstable (both types), Anxious, Histrionic, and Schizoid criteria.

Domain 5: REALITY TESTING

Conspiratorial thinking, suspicions regarding sexual fidelity, unusual beliefs. Items assess Paranoid criteria.

Domain 6: IMPULSE CONTROL

Impulsive action, self-harm and suicidal behaviour, aggression and violence, irresponsibility, capacity for guilt and remorse, ability to profit from experience. Items assess Emotionally Unstable, Dissocial, Anankastic, and Schizoid criteria (last two rated at interview conclusion).

SECTION 3: ADMINISTRATION PROCEDURE (STEP-BY-STEP)

3.1 Prerequisites Before Administration

  1. Obtain psychiatric history – Ideally through a prior clinical interview (e.g., SCAN, SCID-I) or medical records
  2. Assess current mental state – Rule out severe depression, psychosis, or cognitive impairment
  3. Gather background information – Age, marital status, education, occupation, treatment history
  4. Avoid detailed personality inquiries – Do NOT extensively discuss personality traits with the subject beforehand
  5. Prepare materials – Interview schedule, answer sheet, screening questionnaire, scoring materials

3.2 Step 1: Rapport Building

  • Introduce yourself and explain the purpose of the assessment
  • Provide a comfortable, private setting
  • Establish a collaborative, non-judgmental tone
  • Explain that the interview concerns long-standing patterns of behaviour, not just recent experiences
  • Clarify that there are no “right” or “wrong” answers
  • Inform the subject about approximate duration (60–90 minutes)

3.3 Step 2: Instructions to Client

“The questions I am going to ask concern what you are like most of the time. I’m interested in what has been typical of you throughout your life, and not just recently. If you have changed and your answers might have been different at some time in the past, be sure to let me know.”

3.4 Step 3: Administering the Screening Questionnaire (Optional)

  1. Provide the self-administered questionnaire to the subject
  2. Read the printed instructions aloud if necessary
  3. Ensure the subject understands the True/False format
  4. Allow approximately 10–15 minutes for completion
  5. Remain available to clarify any items if asked
  6. Collect the completed questionnaire

3.5 Step 4: Scoring the Screener

  1. Circle item numbers NOT followed by “F” if they were answered TRUE
  2. Circle remaining item numbers (those followed by “F”) if they were answered FALSE
  3. Count the number of circled items for each disorder
  4. Decision Rule: If 3 or more items from a disorder are circled, the subject has “failed” the screen

3.6 Step 5: Deciding Whether to Proceed to Interview

Screening ResultAction
Failed screen for one or more disordersAdminister full interview (or relevant sections)
Passed all screensGenerally no interview needed; clinical judgment may override
No screening usedAdminister full interview to all subjects

3.7 Step 6: Conducting the Semi-Structured Interview

  1. Begin with Background Information (age, marital status, children, parents, siblings, education, occupation, reason for treatment, psychiatric history)
  2. Deliver the standardized opening instruction
  3. Proceed through the six domains sequentially: Work → Self → Interpersonal Relationships → Affects → Reality Testing → Impulse Control
  4. For each domain: Begin with open-ended introductory questions, follow with specific criterion-targeted questions, probe for examples, score each item
  5. At the conclusion: Rate observational criteria (items 65–67) based on behaviour during the entire interview

3.8 Step 7: Use of Probes and Follow-Up Questions

3.9 Step 8: Handling Resistance and Vague Answers

Vague or general responsesRequest specific examples: “Can you give me a specific situation?”
Only recent examplesRedirect: “Has this been a pattern for many years?”

Critical Rule: If subjects endorse a trait or behaviour but are unable to provide examples, a positive score should NOT be given. If they truly have the trait to a clinically meaningful degree, they should be able to provide anecdotes or examples.

3.11 Step 10: Clinical Judgment Rules

  1. Distinguishing traits from states: Differentiate longstanding personality characteristics from transient Axis I symptoms
  2. Duration requirement: Behaviours must have been present for at least 5 years
  3. Onset requirement: At least one criterion must have been fulfilled before age 25
  4. Past 12 months: Criterion must have manifested during the past year (unless exempt)
  5. Cultural considerations: Evaluate behaviours within the subject’s cultural context
  6. Informant data: Use clinical judgment to determine preferred source when discrepancies exist
  7. Clinical significance: A score of 2 requires convincing clinical documentation, not just endorsement

SECTION 4: SCORING SYSTEM

4.1 Item Scoring Categories

ScoreMeaningDescription
0Absent or NormalBehaviour is denied, rare, insignificant, or not supported by convincing examples
1Exaggerated or AccentuatedTrait present to exaggerated degree OR occasionally present at pathological level, but does NOT fully meet criterion threshold
2Criterion Level (Pathological)Behaviour clearly present, well-documented with examples, meets the full diagnostic criterion
?Inadequate InformationSubject could not or would not provide adequate information to score the item
NANot ApplicableThe item does not apply to this subject (e.g., occupational items for those who never worked)

4.2 Detailed Scoring Guidelines

Score of 2 (Pathological/Criterion Level):

  • The behaviour is frequently present
  • Supported by convincing examples, anecdotes, and descriptions
  • Causes subjective distress or problems in social or occupational functioning
  • Present for at least 5 years
  • Manifested during the past 12 months (unless exempt)
  • At least one criterion must have onset before age 25

Score of 1 (Exaggerated/Accentuated):

  • Behaviour is occasionally rather than frequently present at criterion level
  • Present frequently but less than convincing in documentation
  • Present but clinical significance is uncertain
  • Almost always meets one part of a criterion but not the full criterion

Score of 0 (Absent/Normal):

  • Denied, rare, insignificant, or unconvincing examples
  • Explained by other factors (illness, medications, cultural norms)

4.3 Temporal Scoring Conventions

RequirementDetails

4.4 Diagnostic Algorithms (Handscoring)

ALGORITHM STEPS (Example: F60.0 Paranoid):

Step 1: Count informant-based scores [in brackets]
        → Enter total after "Number of Criteria Based on Informant(s)"

Step 2: Check if ANY positive score (1 or 2) exists in Column 1 (<25)
        → If NO: Enter 0 for Criteria Met & Dimensional Score. Check "Negative."

Step 3: Add ALL scores in Columns 1 and 2
        → Enter sum as "Dimensional Score"

Step 4: Check if any score of 2 exists in Column 1 (<25)
        → If NO score of 2: Enter 0 for Criteria Met. Check "Negative."

Step 5: Count the number of 2s in BOTH Columns
        → Enter as "Number of Criteria Met"

Step 6: Apply diagnostic threshold:
        → Below threshold-1: "Negative"
        → Equals threshold-1: "Probable"
        → Meets or exceeds threshold: "Definite"

4.6 Dimensional Scoring

In addition to categorical diagnoses, the IPDE produces a dimensional score for each disorder – the sum of ALL scores (0, 1, and 2) for all items pertaining to that disorder. Higher dimensional scores indicate greater pathology. These scores are more reliable than categorical diagnoses and useful for tracking treatment progress.

4.7 Summary of Scoring Process

SCORING WORKFLOW:

1. Score each item during/immediately after administration (0, 1, 2, ?, NA)
   |
2. Record on Interview Schedule or Answer Sheet
   |
3. Transcribe to Scoresheet:
   - Informant scores override subject scores
   - Enter in appropriate onset column (<25 or ≥25)
   - Scores of ? treated as 0
   |
4. Apply Diagnostic Algorithm for each disorder:
   - Calculate Dimensional Score (sum of all scores)
   - Determine Number of Criteria Met (count of 2s)
   - Apply threshold rules
   |
5. Assign Diagnosis: DEFINITE / PROBABLE / NEGATIVE
   |
6. Rate Confidence Level (1=High, 2=Moderate, 3=Low)
   |
7. Complete Summary Scoresheet

SECTION 6: QUESTIONS AND ITEMS

6.1 Nature of IPDE Questions

  1. Direct and specific: Target specific diagnostic criteria
  2. Behaviourally anchored: Focus on observable behaviours and verifiable experiences
  3. Open to probing: Initial questions followed by standardized probes
  4. Culturally sensitive: Designed for cross-cultural contexts
  5. Self-report based: Rely primarily on subject’s account
  6. Documentation-requiring: Demand examples and anecdotes for positive scoring

6.4 Abbreviated Interview Options

Option 1: Selective Administration – Omit items not pertaining to disorders of interest. Always include introductory open-ended questions for any section containing relevant items.

Option 2: Screen-Based Abbreviation – Administer screening questionnaire first; interview only for disorders where the screen was “failed.”

Re-examination of 243 subjects after average 6-month interval:

  • Kappa for “any personality disorder” (Definite): 0.62 (DSM-III-R), 0.59 (ICD-10)
  • Compares favourably with temporal stability of Axis I instruments (e.g., SADS-L)

7.3 Strengths of the IPDE

  1. International standardization – only PD interview tested worldwide
  2. Dual classification system (ICD-10 and DSM-IV)
  3. Detailed item-by-item scoring manual
  4. Both categorical and dimensional output
  5. Strong psychometric properties comparable to Axis I instruments
  6. Temporal safeguards (5-year duration, onset requirements)
  7. Documentation requirement reduces false-positive rates
  8. Systematic informant integration
  9. Multiple language translations with back-translation
  10. Screening option for efficient pre-screening

7.4 Limitations of the IPDE

  1. Self-report dependency – individuals may lack insight
  2. Social desirability bias
  3. Time-consuming (60–90+ minutes)
  4. Requires highly trained clinicians
  5. State-trait confusion with severe symptoms
  6. Personality disorder concepts remain predominantly Western
  7. No gold standard for validity assessment
  8. Informant data integration remains complex
  9. Not suitable for severe psychosis, cognitive impairment, age <18
  10. High diagnostic overlap/comorbidity
  11. Threshold arbitrariness in diagnostic cut-points

Ms. Sharma presented as a well-groomed young woman. Affect was notably labile throughout – shifting rapidly between tearfulness, irritability, and brief episodes of warmth. She displayed dramatic descriptions of emotional experiences. Eye contact was variable. No psychotic symptoms observed. Insight appeared partial. Self-report considered moderately reliable, with some tendency toward minimization of interpersonal aggression and exaggeration of victimization.

Affects: Chronic emptiness (“like a black hole inside”), rapid mood shifts several times daily, extreme emotional reactivity to perceived interpersonal slights.

Reality Testing: Some paranoid ideation in relationships (suspecting infidelity), but context-dependent and not delusional.

Impulse Control: Multiple self-harm episodes (15–20 wrist cuts over 8 years), one serious suicide attempt (overdose at age 23), impulsive spending, episodes of property destruction. Reports guilt and remorse after outbursts.

IX. INTERPRETATION

Ms. Sharma’s IPDE profile is consistent with Emotionally Unstable Personality Disorder, Borderline Type (F60.31). Core features include: pervasive identity disturbance with uncertainty about self-image and goals; intense unstable relationships with oscillation between idealization and devaluation; marked affective instability with rapid mood shifts (hours, not weeks); chronic emptiness; recurrent self-harm (8+ years); and frantic efforts to avoid abandonment. Sub-threshold anxious and dependent features reflect persistent tension, fear of rejection, and difficulty making independent decisions partially embedded within the borderline pathology.

X. DIFFERENTIAL DIAGNOSIS

PD vs. Depression: Personality pathology is distinguishable from depressive disorder – self-harm, identity disturbance, and relational instability persist during euthymic periods and preceded first depressive episode.

Borderline vs. Histrionic: Depth of identity disturbance, self-destructive behaviour, and chronic emptiness are more consistent with borderline than histrionic pathology.

Borderline vs. Dissocial: Capacity for guilt, absence of callous disregard, and reactive (not instrumental) aggression rule out dissocial PD.

XI. CONCLUSION

Ms. Sharma meets ICD-10 criteria for Emotionally Unstable Personality Disorder, Borderline Type (F60.31) – Definite diagnosis with high confidence. The disorder has been present for minimum 10 years with onset in adolescence and is the primary driver of recurrent self-harm, relationship instability, and occupational difficulties.

XII. RECOMMENDATIONS

  1. Psychotherapy: Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT), minimum 12–18 months
  2. Focus areas: Emotion regulation, distress tolerance, interpersonal effectiveness, identity consolidation
  3. Risk management: Safety planning, crisis protocols, regular monitoring of self-harm urges
  4. Medication: Continue antidepressant; consider mood stabilizer if affective instability persists
  5. Follow-up: Re-assessment at 6–12 months to track dimensional score changes
  6. Prognosis: Moderate improvement expected within 12–18 months with DBT; complete remission possible with sustained treatment (3–5 years)

9.2 How to Probe Effectively

When the subject endorses a trait but provides no examples:

“You mentioned that you [trait]. Can you give me a specific situation where that happened?”
“What did you actually do in that situation?”
“How did that affect your work/relationships/daily life?”

When the subject denies a trait that may be present:

“Have people ever told you that you’re like that?”
“Why do you think they’ve said that?”
“Has anyone – a family member, friend, or therapist – ever commented on this?”

When distinguishing trait from state:

“Has this been the way you are most of the time, or mainly when you’re feeling depressed/anxious?”
“Were you like this before your current illness started?”
“If you think back 5 years or more, was this already a pattern?”

A.2 ICD-10 General Criteria for Personality Disorder (F60)

For ANY specific personality disorder to be diagnosed, these general criteria must ALSO be met:

  1. Not directly attributable to gross brain damage/disease or another psychiatric disorder
  2. Stable and of long duration, with onset in late childhood or adolescence
  3. Pervasive and clearly maladaptive across a broad range of situations
  4. Leads to considerable personal distress
  5. Usually associated with significant problems in occupational and social performance
  6. Represents extreme or significant deviations from the average individual
