Why this handbook exists

The F00–F98 series worked through ICD-10's chapter block by block, noting where each disorder went in ICD-11. But ICD-11 did not simply rename ICD-10's blocks — it created new groupings and new diagnoses that have no ICD-10 F-block of their own. This handbook collects all of that “left-over” ICD-11 material in one place: the disorders that were newly invented, and the ones that were pulled together into a new grouping from scattered ICD-10 categories.

The organising principle behind every change here is the same one that runs through ICD-11: group disorders by their shared phenomenology (their nature), not by tradition or by age of onset. That is why obsessive-compulsive-type conditions were gathered from all over ICD-10 into one grouping, why catatonia was given its own home, and why stress-response conditions were expanded with two brand-new diagnoses.

Terms in plain language. Grouping = ICD-11's word for a family of related disorders (its version of an ICD-10 “block”); CDDR = the Clinical Descriptions and Diagnostic Requirements, ICD-11's diagnostic manual; insight = the degree to which the person recognises their belief/behaviour is a symptom; egodystonic = experienced as unwanted/intrusive; egosyntonic = experienced as acceptable/part of oneself; obsession = intrusive unwanted thought; compulsion = repetitive act done to relieve distress.

What ICD-11 added, and what it moved

Genuinely NEW diagnoses (no ICD-10 code at all)

The headline “new to ICD-11” list

  • Complex PTSD (6B41) — PTSD plus enduring disturbances of self-organisation, after prolonged/repeated trauma.
  • Prolonged grief disorder (6B42) — persistent, disabling grief beyond the expected period.
  • Gaming disorder (6C51) — a behavioural addiction to digital/video gaming.
  • Compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (6C72) — failure to control intense, repetitive sexual impulses.
  • Hoarding disorder (6B24) — accumulation of possessions and distress at discarding.
  • Body integrity dysphoria (6C21) — a desire for a major physical disability (e.g. amputation) with intense discomfort at being able-bodied.
  • Olfactory reference disorder (6B22) — preoccupation with emitting a foul body odour that others notice.

Restructured groupings (assembled from scattered ICD-10 categories)

  • Catatonia (6A40–6A4Z) — ICD-10 split it between catatonic schizophrenia (F20.2) and organic catatonic disorder (F06.1); ICD-11 makes it a standalone grouping that can apply across many disorders.
  • Obsessive-compulsive & related disorders (6B20–6B2Z) — ICD-10 kept OCD inside the “neurotic” F40–F48 block; ICD-11 makes a dedicated grouping and brings in body dysmorphic disorder, olfactory reference disorder, hypochondriasis, hoarding, trichotillomania and skin-picking (which ICD-10 scattered across somatoform, habit-and-impulse and other blocks).
  • Disorders specifically associated with stress (6B40–6B4Z) — a grouping requiring an identifiable stressor, expanded with complex PTSD and prolonged grief.
  • Bodily distress / bodily experience (6C20–6C21) — replaces the ICD-10 somatoform disorders concept.
  • Impulse control disorders (6C70–6C7Z) — gathers pyromania, kleptomania, intermittent explosive disorder and the new compulsive sexual behaviour disorder.
  • Disorders due to addictive behaviours (6C50–6C5Z) — gambling (moved from ICD-10 impulse disorders) plus the new gaming disorder.
  • Factitious disorders (6D50–6D51) — given explicit codes for imposed on self / on another.

Moved out of the mental-disorders chapter entirely

  • Gender incongruence → a new chapter, Conditions related to sexual health (destigmatised; no longer a mental disorder).
  • Tic disorders → the nervous-system chapter (covered in the F90–F98 handbook).
  • Sleep-wake disorders and sexual dysfunctions → their own chapters (covered in the F50–F59 handbook).

Catatonia (6A40–6A4Z)

Group: Catatonia is a standalone ICD-11 grouping — a syndrome of marked psychomotor disturbance that can occur across many disorders. This is the big structural change: ICD-10 had no independent catatonia category.

Catatonia is a syndrome of disturbed psychomotor activity and reduced/altered responsiveness, arising in the context of another mental disorder, a medical condition, or substances/medications. It can present with too little movement (stupor, mutism, negativism) or too much (excitement, stereotypies), and with characteristic phenomena.

ICD-10 — scattered

  • Catatonic phenomena were split between catatonic schizophrenia (F20.2) and organic catatonic disorder (F06.1).
  • There was no way to code catatonia occurring with a mood disorder, autism, or a medical illness as a distinct syndrome.

ICD-11 (6A40–6A4Z)

A dedicated grouping. Diagnosis requires several (typically ≥3) of a cluster of features, such as: stupor (unresponsiveness), catalepsy (holding imposed postures), waxy flexibility, mutism, negativism (resisting/opposing instructions), posturing, mannerisms, stereotypies, psychomotor agitation, grimacing, echolalia and echopraxia. Coded by cause:

  • 6A40 Catatonia associated with another mental disorder (e.g. schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, autism).
  • 6A41 Catatonia induced by substances or medications (including antipsychotics).
  • 6A4Z Catatonia, unspecified.
  • (Catatonia due to a medical condition is coded in the secondary mental-syndromes grouping, 6E69.)

Unique / identifying features

Waxy flexibility (limbs stay where placed, like bending a candle), negativism, and echophenomena (echolalia/echopraxia) are the classic signs. A lorazepam challenge (rapid response to a benzodiazepine) supports the diagnosis and is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Malignant catatonia (with fever and autonomic instability) is a medical emergency overlapping with neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

Differential diagnosis

From the underlying disorder itself (catatonia is coded in addition, naming its cause); from neuroleptic malignant syndrome (drug-induced, with rigidity, fever, raised creatine kinase); from a medical/neurological cause (encephalitis, non-convulsive status epilepticus — always exclude); from dissociative stupor and severe depressive/psychomotor retardation.

Remember — catatonia signs “WM-NEP”

Waxy flexibility · Mutism · Negativism · Echolalia/echopraxia · Posturing/stupor. ICD-11 makes catatonia its own grouping that names the cause (mental disorder / substance).

Treatment

  • First-line: benzodiazepines (lorazepam) — often dramatic response; ECT for benzodiazepine-non-response or malignant catatonia.
  • Treat the underlying disorder; stop the offending drug in medication-induced catatonia; supportive medical care.

Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders (6B20–6B2Z)

Group: A dedicated ICD-11 grouping bound together by repetitive thoughts and behaviours. ICD-10 kept OCD inside the “neurotic, stress-related and somatoform” block (F42) and scattered the related conditions elsewhere; ICD-11 gathers them by their shared phenomenology.

Members: OCD (6B20), body dysmorphic disorder (6B21), olfactory reference disorder (6B22), hypochondriasis / health anxiety (6B23), hoarding disorder (6B24), and body-focused repetitive behaviour disorders (6B25). Most carry an insight qualifier: with fair to good insight (.0) or with poor to absent insight (.1).

Etiology & psychological model

Kaplan & Sadock Strong heritability and cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical circuit dysfunction; serotonergic involvement (hence SSRI response). Other evidence The cognitive-behavioural model (Salkovskis, Rachman): intrusive thoughts are normal, but catastrophic appraisal ("having this thought means I am dangerous") drives neutralising compulsions that provide short-term relief and maintain the cycle — the basis of exposure and response prevention (ERP).

Comorbidities across the grouping

Depression and anxiety disorders are very common; tic disorders and Tourette (with OCD); eating disorders and social anxiety (with body dysmorphic disorder). Suicide risk is notably raised in body dysmorphic disorder.

Differential diagnosis (whole grouping)

From anankastic personality disorder (ego-syntonic perfectionism, no true obsessions/compulsions); from a delusional disorder when insight is absent and the belief is held with delusional conviction (ICD-11's “poor to absent insight” qualifier keeps such cases within the OCD-related grouping rather than reclassifying them as psychosis); from normal habits and worries.

6B20 · Obsessive-compulsive disorder

Group: OCD & related → the prototype. ICD-10 origin: F42 (was inside the neurotic block).

ICD-11 requirements (6B20)

  • Persistent obsessions (recurrent, intrusive, unwanted thoughts, images or urges that are distressing) and/or compulsions (repetitive behaviours or mental acts the person feels driven to perform, often to a rigid rule or to neutralise an obsession).
  • The obsessions/compulsions are time-consuming (e.g. more than an hour a day) or cause significant distress or impairment.
  • Insight qualifiers: 6B20.0 with fair to good insight, 6B20.1 with poor to absent insight.

ICD-10 (F42) contrast

ICD-10 required obsessions/compulsions on most days for at least 2 weeks, and subtyped by predominantly thoughts (F42.0), predominantly acts (F42.1), or mixed (F42.2). ICD-11 drops those subtypes in favour of the insight qualifier.

Differential diagnosis

From anankastic/obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (ego-syntonic style, no intrusive obsessions); from generalized anxiety (real-life worries, not intrusive/neutralised); from the repetitive behaviour of autism; from body dysmorphic/hoarding disorders (focus differs); from a psychotic disorder if a belief is truly delusional.

Remember

“Obsessions (intrusive thoughts) + compulsions (neutralising acts), egodystonic, time-consuming” — ICD-11 rates insight instead of thought/act subtypes.

Treatment: ERP (exposure and response prevention) first-line; SSRIs (often higher doses); clomipramine; combine for severe cases.

6B21 · Body dysmorphic disorder

Group: OCD & related → preoccupation with appearance. ICD-10 origin: largely subsumed under hypochondriacal disorder F45.2.

ICD-11 requirements (6B21)

  • Persistent preoccupation with one or more perceived defects or flaws in appearance that are unnoticeable or only slight to others.
  • Excessive self-consciousness, often with ideas of reference (belief that others notice/judge the flaw).
  • Repetitive behaviours (mirror-checking, comparing, camouflaging, skin-picking, reassurance-seeking) or avoidance.
  • Significant distress or impairment. Insight qualifiers (.0 fair-good / .1 poor-absent).
Differential diagnosis

From an eating disorder (there the concern is weight/shape specifically); from normal appearance concern; from a real, noticeable deformity; from OCD (focus is appearance here); from a delusional disorder when insight is absent (kept in this grouping via the qualifier).

Unique features

High rates of cosmetic-procedure seeking (rarely satisfying), high suicidality. “Muscle dysmorphia” is a recognised presentation.

Remember

“Obsessed with an unnoticeable appearance flaw + mirror-checking” — body dysmorphic disorder.

Treatment: CBT (with ERP tailored to appearance rituals); SSRIs. Avoid cosmetic procedures.

6B22 · Olfactory reference disorder NEW

Group: OCD & related → a brand-new ICD-11 diagnosis. No ICD-10 equivalent.

ICD-11 requirements (6B22)

  • Persistent preoccupation with the belief that one is emitting a foul or offensive body odour or breath that is unnoticeable or only slight to others.
  • Excessive self-consciousness, often with ideas of reference (others are noticing, judging, or talking about the odour).
  • Repetitive behaviours: checking for body odour, excessive washing/grooming, changing clothes, using perfume/gum, seeking reassurance, or avoidance of social situations.
  • Significant distress or impairment. Insight qualifiers apply.
Differential diagnosis

From a real detectable odour or medical cause (e.g. trimethylaminuria); from social anxiety disorder (fear of scrutiny generally, not a specific odour belief); from body dysmorphic disorder (appearance, not smell); from an olfactory hallucination or a delusional disorder when insight is absent.

Remember

“Convinced they smell bad when they don't + washing/checking” — olfactory reference disorder (NEW in ICD-11).

Treatment: CBT/ERP and SSRIs, as for the OCD-related grouping.

6B23 · Hypochondriasis (health anxiety disorder)

Group: OCD & related → ICD-11 moved health anxiety here (out of the somatoform block), because its checking/reassurance-seeking is phenomenologically OCD-like. ICD-10 origin: F45.2.

ICD-11 requirements (6B23)

  • Persistent preoccupation with, or fear of, having one or more serious, progressive or life-threatening illnesses.
  • Catastrophic misinterpretation of bodily signs/symptoms (including normal sensations).
  • Repetitive, excessive health-related behaviours (checking the body, seeking reassurance, repeated medical consultations) or maladaptive avoidance (avoiding doctors/hospitals).
  • Persists despite appropriate medical evaluation and reassurance; significant distress/impairment. Insight qualifiers apply.
Differential diagnosis

From bodily distress disorder (6C20) — there the focus is distress about the symptoms themselves, whereas hypochondriasis is fear of having a disease; from a genuine medical illness; from OCD (health is one of several themes there); from panic disorder (acute fear of imminent catastrophe) and from a delusional disease conviction when insight is absent.

Remember

“Fear of HAVING a disease + checking/reassurance” — hypochondriasis; contrast with bodily distress disorder (distress about the symptoms).

Treatment: CBT for health anxiety; SSRIs; a consistent single clinician limiting unnecessary investigations.

6B24 · Hoarding disorder NEW

Group: OCD & related → a brand-new ICD-11 diagnosis. No ICD-10 equivalent.

ICD-11 requirements (6B24)

  • Accumulation of possessions due to excessive acquisition and/or difficulty discarding them, regardless of actual value.
  • Accumulation results in cluttered living spaces so that their use or safety is compromised.
  • Marked distress at, or difficulty with, discarding (a strong urge to save, distress at the idea of getting rid of things).
  • Significant distress or impairment (including risk to health/safety, e.g. fire, falls, sanitation). Insight qualifiers apply.
Differential diagnosis

From normal collecting (organised, not impairing); from hoarding secondary to OCD (there driven by an obsession, e.g. contamination), depression (loss of energy), psychosis, or a neurocognitive/organic disorder (dementia, brain injury); from poverty.

Remember

“Can't discard + cluttered unusable rooms” — hoarding disorder (NEW in ICD-11).

Treatment: specialised CBT for hoarding (motivational work, sorting/discarding skills, reducing acquisition); SSRIs have limited effect.

6B25 · Body-focused repetitive behaviour disorders

Group: OCD & related → repetitive self-grooming behaviours. ICD-10 origin: trichotillomania was F63.3 (habit & impulse); skin-picking had no code.

ICD-11 requirements (6B25)

  • 6B25.0 Trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder): recurrent pulling out of one's hair leading to hair loss, with repeated attempts to stop.
  • 6B25.1 Excoriation (skin-picking) disorder: recurrent picking of one's skin leading to skin lesions, with repeated attempts to stop.
  • Both: the behaviour is preceded by tension or an urge and followed by relief/gratification; causes distress or impairment; not better explained by another disorder or a substance.
Differential diagnosis

From OCD (there the behaviour neutralises an obsession); from stereotyped movement disorder (rhythmic, in intellectual disability/autism); from hair loss/skin lesions due to a dermatological or medical cause; from picking/pulling in response to a delusion or hallucination; from tics.

Remember

“Pull hair / pick skin → relief, tries to stop” — body-focused repetitive behaviours; ICD-11 added skin-picking and moved trichotillomania here from habit-and-impulse.

Treatment: habit-reversal training (first-line behavioural treatment); SSRIs or N-acetylcysteine may be tried.

Disorders specifically associated with stress (6B40–6B4Z)

Group: An ICD-11 grouping defined by the requirement of an identifiable stressor or traumatic event. It is home to the two most examinable NEW ICD-11 diagnoses: complex PTSD and prolonged grief disorder.

Members: PTSD (6B40), complex PTSD (6B41), prolonged grief disorder (6B42), adjustment disorder (6B43), reactive attachment disorder (6B44) and disinhibited social engagement disorder (6B45). Every diagnosis requires a stressor — the disorder is understood as an abnormal response to it.

Etiology & psychological model

Other evidence Ehlers & Clark's cognitive model of PTSD: the trauma memory is poorly elaborated and time-anchored, producing a sense of current threat; negative appraisals and avoidance/safety behaviours maintain it. For grief, the dual-process model (oscillating between loss- and restoration-focused coping) frames prolonged grief as a failure of adaptive oscillation.

Differential diagnosis (whole grouping)

From normal, adaptive stress/grief responses (which resolve and do not disable); from depression and anxiety disorders without the trauma-linked re-experiencing; from acute stress reactions (a normal transient response, coded as a non-disorder Z/QE code in ICD-11).

6B40 · Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)

Group: Stress-associated → refined in ICD-11 to a tighter 3-cluster definition. ICD-10 origin: F43.1.

ICD-11 requirements (6B40) — three clusters

Following exposure to an extremely threatening or horrific event/situation:

  • (1) Re-experiencing the trauma in the here-and-now — vivid intrusive memories, flashbacks or nightmares, accompanied by fear/horror (not merely recalling it).
  • (2) Avoidance of reminders (thoughts, activities, situations, people).
  • (3) Persistent sense of current threat — hypervigilance, exaggerated startle.

Symptoms persist for several weeks and cause significant impairment. ICD-11 deliberately made PTSD narrower and more specific than DSM.

Differential diagnosis

From complex PTSD (6B41) (adds self-organisation disturbances); from adjustment disorder (6B43) (stressor not necessarily traumatic; no re-experiencing/current-threat cluster); from acute stress reaction (transient, normal); from depression/anxiety; from a specific phobia.

Remember — PTSD “R-A-T”

Re-experiencing (in the here-and-now) · Avoidance · Threat (persistent sense of current threat). ICD-11's PTSD is the tight 3-cluster version.

Treatment: trauma-focused CBT and EMDR (eye-movement desensitisation and reprocessing) first-line; SSRIs/SNRIs where medication is used.

6B41 · Complex PTSD NEW

Group: Stress-associated → a brand-new ICD-11 diagnosis. Partly captures what ICD-10 called “enduring personality change after catastrophe (F62.0)”, but is a distinct trauma diagnosis.

ICD-11 requirements (6B41)

Arises after prolonged or repeated trauma from which escape is difficult or impossible (e.g. torture, prolonged domestic violence, repeated childhood abuse). Requires all three PTSD clusters (re-experiencing, avoidance, current threat) PLUS three persistent “disturbances in self-organisation”:

  • (1) Affect dysregulation — heightened emotional reactivity, or emotional numbing/dissociation.
  • (2) Negative self-concept — persistent beliefs of being diminished, defeated or worthless, with shame/guilt.
  • (3) Disturbances in relationships — difficulty feeling close to others, avoidance of relationships.
Differential diagnosis

From PTSD (6B40) (no self-organisation disturbances); from borderline pattern personality disorder — overlapping but complex PTSD has a stable negative self-concept and avoidance of relationships rather than the frantic abandonment-fear/idealisation-devaluation instability of borderline; from depression; from ICD-10's F62.0 enduring personality change (complex PTSD is the modern successor for trauma-related change).

Remember — complex PTSD = “PTSD + D-S-O”

All of PTSD (R-A-T) plus Disturbances in Self-Organisation: affect dysregulation + negative self-concept + relationship difficulties. Follows prolonged, inescapable trauma.

Treatment: phased trauma-focused therapy (stabilisation/affect-regulation skills, then trauma processing, then reconnection); longer course than single-incident PTSD.

6B42 · Prolonged grief disorder NEW

Group: Stress-associated → a brand-new ICD-11 diagnosis. No ICD-10 equivalent.

ICD-11 requirements (6B42)

  • After the death of a partner, parent, child or other close person, a persistent and pervasive grief response characterised by longing/yearning for the deceased or persistent preoccupation with them, accompanied by intense emotional pain (sadness, guilt, anger, denial, difficulty accepting the death, feeling part of oneself is lost, inability to experience positive mood, emotional numbness, difficulty engaging with life).
  • Persists for an atypically long period after the loss — markedly beyond expected social/cultural/religious norms (as a guide, at least ~6 months).
  • Causes significant impairment; the response clearly exceeds cultural/religious mourning norms.
Differential diagnosis

From normal/culturally-normative grief (which softens over time and does not disable); from a depressive episode (pervasive low mood/anhedonia across all domains, not centred on yearning for the deceased — the two can coexist); from PTSD when the death was traumatic (re-experiencing of the death itself); from adjustment disorder.

Remember

“Yearning/preoccupation with the deceased, disabling, well beyond ~6 months & cultural norms” — prolonged grief disorder (NEW in ICD-11).

Treatment: grief-focused CBT/complicated-grief therapy; treat comorbid depression.

6B43 · Adjustment disorder

Group: Stress-associated → refined in ICD-11. ICD-10 origin: F43.2.

ICD-11 requirements (6B43)

  • A maladaptive reaction to an identifiable psychosocial stressor (or multiple stressors, e.g. divorce, illness, job loss), usually emerging within a month.
  • Characterised by preoccupation with the stressor (excessive worry, recurrent distressing thoughts, rumination about its implications) and failure to adapt (symptoms interfering with functioning).
  • Symptoms typically resolve within about 6 months unless the stressor persists; do not meet criteria for another disorder.

ICD-11 gives adjustment disorder a positive definition (preoccupation + failure to adapt), unlike ICD-10's residual “doesn't fit elsewhere” framing.

Differential diagnosis

From a full depressive or anxiety disorder (if criteria are met, code that instead); from PTSD/acute stress (traumatic stressor, re-experiencing); from a normal adaptive stress reaction (not impairing); from prolonged grief (bereavement-specific).

Remember

“Preoccupation with a stressor + failure to adapt, resolves <6 months” — ICD-11 defines it positively, not as a leftover.

Treatment: problem-solving and supportive/brief CBT; resolve or reduce the stressor; short-term symptomatic help.

Also in this grouping (covered in the F90–F98 handbook): reactive attachment disorder (6B44) and disinhibited social engagement disorder (6B45) — ICD-11 places the childhood attachment disorders here because they require a stressor of grossly inadequate care.

Bodily distress & bodily experience (6C20–6C21)

Group: An ICD-11 grouping that replaces the ICD-10 somatoform disorders (F45) concept with a single positive diagnosis, and adds a brand-new body-experience disorder.

6C20 Bodily distress disorder (replaces somatoform)

ICD-11 requirements (6C20)

  • Presence of bodily symptoms that are distressing to the person and to which excessive attention is directed.
  • Repeated contact with health-care providers and excessive health-related behaviour; the attention is not relieved by appropriate clinical examination/reassurance.
  • Symptoms are persistent (most days for several months) and cause significant impairment. Graded mild / moderate / severe by degree of preoccupation and impairment.
  • Crucially, the symptoms may or may not have a medical explanation — the diagnosis rests on the disproportionate distress and preoccupation, not on whether a disease is present.

ICD-10 contrast (F45)

ICD-10 required symptoms to be medically unexplained and split them into somatisation disorder, undifferentiated somatoform, hypochondriacal disorder, autonomic dysfunction, and pain disorder. ICD-11 collapses these into one bodily distress disorder graded by severity, and drops the “medically unexplained” requirement (which was unreliable and stigmatising). Hypochondriasis moves to the OCD-related grouping (6B23).

Differential diagnosis

From hypochondriasis (6B23) (fear of having a disease vs distress about the symptoms); from a purely medical illness with proportionate concern; from depression/anxiety with somatic symptoms; from panic disorder; from factitious disorder (symptoms intentionally produced).

Remember

“Distressing bodily symptoms + excessive attention/health-seeking, disproportionate” — one disorder graded mild/mod/severe; no “medically unexplained” requirement.

6C21 Body integrity dysphoria NEW

ICD-11 requirements (6C21)

  • An intense and persistent desire to become physically disabled in a major way (e.g. amputation of a limb, paraplegia, blindness), beginning by early adolescence.
  • Persistent discomfort or intense feelings of inappropriateness concerning the person's current non-disabled body configuration.
  • The desire results in harmful consequences — e.g. attempts to actually bring about the disability, or the preoccupation significantly impairs functioning.
Differential diagnosis

From body dysmorphic disorder (concern with appearance, not a wish for disability); from a psychotic/delusional belief; from gender incongruence (concerns experienced gender, not disability); from factitious disorder.

Remember

“Wants to be disabled / mismatch with an able body” — body integrity dysphoria (NEW in ICD-11).

Impulse control disorders (6C70–6C7Z)

Group: An ICD-11 grouping for repeated failure to resist an impulse to perform an act that is rewarding in the short term but harmful. Gathers pyromania, kleptomania and intermittent explosive disorder (from ICD-10's habit-and-impulse block) and adds a NEW member.

Members: pyromania (6C70), kleptomania (6C71), compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (6C72, NEW), and intermittent explosive disorder (6C73). Shared pattern: mounting tension before the act, and pleasure/relief/gratification during or after it. (Pyromania and kleptomania are detailed in the F60–F69 handbook; the two most examinable ICD-11 points are below.)

6C72 · Compulsive sexual behaviour disorder NEW

Group: Impulse control → a brand-new ICD-11 diagnosis. Partly captures what ICD-10 loosely called “excessive sexual drive (F52.7)” but is defined by loss of control, not high drive.

ICD-11 requirements (6C72)

  • A persistent pattern of failure to control intense, repetitive sexual impulses or urges, resulting in repetitive sexual behaviour over an extended period (e.g. ≥6 months).
  • Sexual activity becomes a central focus of life to the neglect of health/personal care or other interests, activities and responsibilities.
  • Numerous unsuccessful efforts to reduce the behaviour; continuing it despite adverse consequences or deriving little satisfaction from it.
  • Marked distress or impairment. Distress arising purely from moral judgement/disapproval of sexual impulses is not sufficient.
Differential diagnosis

From increased libido in a manic/hypomanic episode (code the mood disorder); from a paraphilic disorder (defined by the atypical focus of arousal, not loss of control); from behaviour that is distressing only because of moral/religious conflict (explicitly excluded); from substance effects.

Remember

“Can't control repetitive sexual urges, life-dominating, tried to stop” — compulsive sexual behaviour disorder (NEW; an impulse-control, not a paraphilic, disorder). Moral distress alone doesn't count.

Treatment: CBT/relapse-prevention, motivational work; treat comorbid mood/substance disorders; SSRIs may help.

6C73 · Intermittent explosive disorder

Group: Impulse control → recurrent failure to control aggressive impulses. ICD-10 origin: F63.8 (other habit & impulse).

ICD-11 requirements (6C73)

  • Repeated brief episodes of failure to control aggressive impulses, resulting in verbal or physical aggression (e.g. tantrums, tirades, assaults, property damage).
  • The degree of aggression is grossly out of proportion to any provocation or precipitating stressor.
  • Not better explained by another mental/behavioural disorder; causes distress or impairment.
Differential diagnosis

From aggression within conduct-dissocial disorder or an antisocial personality (pervasive pattern, not discrete disproportionate outbursts); from aggression in mania, intoxication, or an organic/neurological disorder; from oppositional defiant disorder.

Remember

“Explosive outbursts out of all proportion to the trigger” — intermittent explosive disorder.

Disorders due to addictive behaviours (6C50–6C5Z)

Group: A new ICD-11 grouping recognising behavioural addictions — addictions without a substance. It sits alongside the substance-use disorders.

Members: gambling disorder (6C50) and gaming disorder (6C51, NEW). Both are defined by the addiction triad of impaired control, increasing priority over other interests, and continuation/escalation despite harm, each specified as predominantly online or predominantly offline.

6C50 · Gambling disorder

Group: Addictive behaviours → moved here from ICD-10's habit-and-impulse block (F63.0) — reconceptualised as a behavioural addiction.

ICD-11 requirements (6C50)

  • Impaired control over gambling (onset, frequency, intensity, duration, context).
  • Gambling given increasing priority over other life interests and daily activities.
  • Continuation or escalation despite negative consequences (debt, relationship, occupational harm).
  • Pattern is sustained (usually ≥12 months) with significant impairment; specified online or offline.
Differential diagnosis

From recreational/social gambling (controlled, curbed on losses); from excessive gambling during a manic episode; from gambling within an antisocial personality pattern.

Remember

“Impaired control + priority + persists despite harm” — the behavioural-addiction triad; ICD-11 moved gambling out of impulse control.

6C51 · Gaming disorder NEW

Group: Addictive behaviours → a brand-new, high-profile ICD-11 diagnosis. No ICD-10 equivalent.

ICD-11 requirements (6C51)

  • Impaired control over gaming (digital or video gaming).
  • Gaming given increasing priority over other interests and daily activities.
  • Continuation or escalation despite negative consequences.
  • Sufficient severity to cause significant impairment; normally evident for at least 12 months (may be shorter if severe). Specified predominantly online (6C51.0) or predominantly offline (6C51.1).
Differential diagnosis

From normal heavy/hobbyist gaming (no impaired control or functional harm); from gaming used to avoid an underlying depression or anxiety disorder (code that); from gambling disorder (money-staking); from ADHD-related over-engagement.

Remember

“Gaming: impaired control + priority + persists despite harm, ~12 months” — gaming disorder (NEW in ICD-11); online vs offline.

Treatment (both): CBT-based interventions, structured limits, treat comorbidity (depression, anxiety, ADHD); family involvement for adolescents.

Factitious disorders (6D50–6D51)

Group: ICD-11 gives factitious disorders explicit codes for the self and the “another person” forms. ICD-10 had only the self form (F68.1).

6D50 Factitious disorder imposed on self

Intentional feigning, falsifying or inducing of medical, psychological or behavioural signs/symptoms in oneself, associated with identified deception. The motivation is internal (to adopt the sick role/obtain care), not an obvious external reward.

6D51 Factitious disorder imposed on another

Intentional feigning/falsifying/inducing of signs/symptoms in another person (typically a dependent, e.g. a child), with deception. Formerly “factitious disorder by proxy” / “Munchausen by proxy”. It is a diagnosis of the perpetrator; the victim is recorded separately (as maltreatment/abuse) — and it is a child-protection emergency.

Differential diagnosis

From malingering — also intentional, but motivated by an obvious external gain (money, avoiding work/prosecution); malingering is not a mental disorder (a Z/QE code). From bodily distress / somatoform presentations — there symptoms are not intentionally produced. From a genuine medical or mental disorder.

Remember — the illness-behaviour trio

Factitious: intentional, motive internal (sick role). Malingering: intentional, motive external/obvious (not a disorder). Bodily distress: not intentional. ICD-11 adds the “imposed on another” code (6D51).

Management: careful, non-confrontational; safeguard the victim in 6D51 (mandatory child-protection); avoid unnecessary investigations; treat comorbidity.

Relocated out of psychiatry: Gender incongruence (HA60 / HA61)

Group: Included here for completeness because it is one of ICD-11's most significant structural moves: the ICD-10 “gender identity disorders (F64)” were removed from the mental-disorders chapter altogether.

ICD-10 (F64)

Classified within mental and behavioural disorders: transsexualism (F64.0), dual-role transvestism (F64.1), gender identity disorder of childhood (F64.2) — framing gender variance as a psychiatric condition.

ICD-11 (HA60 / HA61)

Renamed Gender incongruence and moved to a new chapter, Conditions related to sexual health — it is no longer a mental disorder. A marked and persistent incongruence between experienced gender and assigned sex; distress is not required. Coded HA60 (adolescence/adulthood) and HA61 (childhood). The category is retained chiefly to enable access to gender-affirming health care, deliberately depsychiatrising and destigmatising.

Differential diagnosis

From body dysmorphic disorder and body integrity dysphoria (concern with appearance/disability, not gender); from a transient identity questioning; from an identity disturbance within a psychotic disorder. In ICD-11 this is not diagnosed as a mental disorder at all.

Remember

“F64 gender identity disorder → ICD-11 gender incongruence, out of psychiatry, distress not required” — the headline destigmatising move.
