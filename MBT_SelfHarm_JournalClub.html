Why This Paper Matters

A serious public-health problem

  • Self-harm typically begins and peaks in adolescence.
  • Prevalence ~22.9% in adolescents; ~5.5% in adults.
  • Self-harm is a major risk factor for suicide death, regardless of intent.
  • Very common in borderline personality disorder (BPD): up to 90–95% report a history of self-harm.

Why review the evidence now?

  • MBT is repeatedly promoted as a promising treatment for self-harm.
  • But earlier reviews gave mixed results.
  • No prior review focused specifically on MBT for self-harm.
  • No clear comparison of the adult (MBT) vs. adolescent (MBT-A) versions.

The Clinical Problem: What Is Self-Harm?

Definition used in this paper

Following Hawton et al. (2012): intentional self-poisoning or self-injury, regardless of motivation or degree of suicidal intent.

This umbrella definition includes acts with and without suicidal intent.

Common forms

CuttingBurning Hitting oneselfScratching Self-poisoningInterfering with healing

Functions

Often serves to escape overwhelming negative feelings or to reconnect when feeling isolated.

Clinical caution: Self-harm should always be assessed for suicidal intent, lethality, frequency, triggers, functions, and current safety — even when framed as "non-suicidal."
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction; definition per Hawton et al. (2012).

Self-Harm and Recurrence

Why it repeats

Self-harm brings short-term relief from distress, which reinforces the behaviour — creating a cycle that is hard to break.

Among BPD inpatients, 51–65% report 25+ lifetime episodes (Goodman et al., 2017).

MBT aims to interrupt this cycle by targeting the breakdown in mentalizing that occurs under emotional arousal — not the behaviour alone.

The Existing Treatment Gap

Mixed evidence

Earlier meta-analyses of MBT for BPD gave inconsistent results for self-harm.

Small effects overall

Self-harm interventions in general show only small pooled effects (Fox et al., 2020).

High dropout

Engaging people with severe emotional dysregulation is difficult; dropout is often high.

No focused review

No prior review looked specifically at MBT for self-harm.

Age matters

Findings may differ between adolescents and adults — never directly compared.

Which format?

Unclear whether individual vs. group MBT and different durations work equally.

The gap this paper fills: a single review that pools both pre–post studies and RCTs, and directly contrasts MBT (adults) with MBT-A (adolescents) for self-harm, BPD, and depression.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction.

Why Mentalization-Based Therapy Is Relevant

Mentalizing

The capacity to understand oneself and others in terms of mental states — thoughts, feelings, wishes, beliefs, and intentions.

When it fails

Mentalizing can break down under emotional arousal, interpersonal conflict, perceived rejection, and attachment threat — the same situations that precede self-harm.

Conceptual model

Attachment threat
↓
Mentalizing failure
↓
Misreading self & others
↓
Emotional dysregulation
↓
Self-harm

MBT's goal: improve mentalizing → better affect regulation → less impulsive self-harm.

Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction (Fonagy et al., 2018; Bateman & Fonagy, 1999).

Introduction: Three Key Concepts

Self-harm

Intentional self-poisoning or self-injury, with or without suicidal intent (Hawton et al., 2012). Strongly linked to psychopathology and BPD.

MBT & MBT-A

A manualized therapy integrating psychoanalysis, attachment & cognitive theory (Bateman & Fonagy, 1999). Main aim: strengthen mentalizing. MBT-A is the adapted adolescent version.

Review + meta-analysis

Systematically pools studies to produce an average effect estimate (Hedges' g), while checking heterogeneity (I²) and risk of bias.

Formats of MBT: originally an intensive day-hospital program (5 days/week, 18 months); later a purified outpatient form (individual + group, 18 months); and MBT-G, a group-only, cost-effective version (12–36 months). MBT-A typically lasts 12 months and adds family sessions.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction (Bateman & Fonagy, 1999, 2006; Karterud, 2015; Taubner & Sevecke, 2015).

Research Gap & Rationale

Gaps in prior literature

  • Small samples; few RCTs.
  • Different MBT formats & durations.
  • Varied control conditions & outcome measures.
  • Little direct adult vs. adolescent comparison.
  • Unclear mechanisms of change.

Why this study was needed

  • To synthesise all available MBT self-harm evidence.
  • To estimate the overall effect on self-harm, BPD & depression.
  • To test whether MBT beats active controls.
  • To examine moderators (duration, dropout, age).
  • To guide clinical decision-making.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction.

Aim, Question & Review Objective

Aim (as stated)

To disentangle findings for adolescents and adults on the efficacy of MBT(-A) in reducing self-harm (primary outcome) and symptoms of BPD and depression (secondary outcomes).

Research question

Is MBT(-A) effective in reducing self-harm and improving BPD & depression — and is it superior to control interventions (TAU/SCM)?

Objective vs. hypothesis

As a pre-registered systematic review & meta-analysis, the study used predefined review questions rather than a single experimental hypothesis. (OSF pre-registration: OSF.IO/26JVF)

Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Abstract & Methods.

Framing the Question — PICO

P
Population: Clinical samples with self-harm; largely BPD / personality pathology; adolescents (MBT-A) and adults (MBT).
I
Intervention: Mentalization-based therapy — MBT (adults) or MBT-A (adolescents), including individual, group (MBT-G) & day-hospital formats.
C
Comparison: Treatment-as-usual (TAU) or Structured Clinical Management (SCM). (Pre–post analyses have no control group.)
O
Outcomes: Primary — self-harm frequency. Secondary — BPD symptoms and depression scores.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Methods (inclusion criteria & outcomes).

Methodology at a Glance

Design

Pre-registered systematic review & meta-analysis (OSF). Included RCTs and pre–post studies.

Databases

Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, PubMed/Medline, Cochrane — plus Google Scholar alert & reference screening.

Search window

All studies published up to 30 September 2022.

Effect size

Hedges' g via random-effects models (metafor in R).

Heterogeneity

I²: 25% low, 50% moderate, 75% high (Higgins et al., 2003).

Bias & extras

RoB 2 tool; meta-regression (duration, dropout, age); sensitivity & subgroup analyses; Egger's test.

Because most analyses had <10 studies, formal publication-bias testing was not reported (Egger's regressions run for reference only).
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Methods.

MBT Treatment Protocol (as delivered)

Adult MBT

Weekly individual + group psychotherapy; often 18 months; day-hospital or outpatient. Some added psychoeducation & crisis planning.

MBT-A (adolescents)

Typically 12 months; weekly individual sessions + monthly family/parent sessions (Rossouw & Fonagy, 2012).

MBT-G (group)

Group + parent sessions without individual therapy; cost-effective; used by Beck 2020, Bo 2017, Tromp 2021.

Wide variation: from a 12-week brief introductory group (Griffiths) to a 36-month day-hospital program (Kvarstein). This diversity is a key source of heterogeneity and complicates pooling.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Table 1 & Discussion. Details reported per included study.

Outcome Measures

Primary — Self-harm

Frequency / severity of self-harm episodes.

Instruments varied: RTSHI(-A), DSHI-9, SSHI, SASII, DAPP-SF, plus incident counts.

Secondary — BPD & Depression

BPD: BPFS-C, BPDSI(-IV), GSI, BEST, PAI-BOR, KABOSS-S.

Depression: BDI, BDI-Y, MFQ, RCADS.

Statistical Analysis — In Plain Terms

Effect size (Hedges' g)

A standardized difference: 0.20 small · 0.50 medium · 0.80 large (Cohen, 1988). Negative g = improvement (fewer symptoms).

Two kinds of comparison

Pre–post: change within the MBT group over time.
Vs. control: MBT(-A) compared against TAU/SCM — the real test of efficacy.

Heterogeneity (I²)

How much studies disagree. 25% low · 50% moderate · 75% high. High I² = interpret pooled effect cautiously.

Extras

Random-effects models; meta-regression (duration, dropout, age); sensitivity analysis (excluding Griffiths 2019).

Key idea: A large pre–post change can look impressive, but only a controlled comparison shows whether MBT does better than existing care.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Data extraction & analysis.

Results 4 — Moderators & Sensitivity

Meta-regression

  • Longer treatment → greater self-harm reduction (pre–post b=−0.07, p=.002; vs. control b=−0.22, p=.005).
  • Higher mean age (b=0.10, p=.045) & higher dropout (b=0.06, p=.015) → smaller self-harm benefit vs. control.
  • No moderator effects on BPD or depression.

Sensitivity & format

  • Excluding the brief pilot (Griffiths 2019) did not change the main conclusions.
  • After exclusion, MBT(-A) vs. control on BPD became significant (g=−0.38, p<.05).
  • Individual MBT-A (1 study, n=20) showed a much larger self-harm effect (g=−1.79) than group MBT-A (g=−0.43) — but based on a single study, so interpret with caution.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Meta-regressions & Exploratory sensitivity analyses (Appendix S1–S3).

Discussion — Main Findings

What looked promising

  • Large pre–post improvements on self-harm, BPD & depression (g = −0.82 to −1.1).
  • Consistent with earlier reviews suggesting MBT is effective.

What the RCTs revealed

  • No superiority over TAU/SCM for self-harm or depression.
  • Only adults' BPD symptoms improved more with MBT (g = −0.56).
  • MBT-A effects were lower than typical DBT-A effects.
Headline conclusion: Pre–post results are encouraging, but the controlled evidence does not support prioritising MBT(-A) as a first-line treatment for self-harm.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Discussion & Conclusions.

Discussion — Why No Superiority?

Mentalizing is common

Improved mentalizing may be a general mechanism across many therapies — so control conditions also benefit (Fonagy & Allison, 2014).

Group format limits

Group MBT may cause emotional contagion; adolescents are especially sensitive to peer influence, reducing benefit.

Indirect approach

MBT does not directly teach coping/emotion-regulation skills (unlike DBT), which may limit self-harm reduction.

Adolescent brain

A pubertal "dip" in social cognition may make high-mentalizing demands less effective for adolescents.

Duration helps

Longer treatment predicted greater self-harm reduction — intensity/dose may matter.

Cautious causality

Mentalizing was not measured as an outcome, so the mechanism cannot be confirmed.

Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Discussion.

What This Means for Clinical Practice

Do

  • Consider MBT as one option, especially adults with BPD.
  • Keep risk assessment & safety/crisis planning central — MBT does not replace them.
  • Ensure adequate therapist training & supervision.
  • Match setting to risk; individualise care.

Be cautious

  • Do not prioritise MBT(-A) as first-line for self-harm.
  • For adolescents, weigh shorter, skills-based options (e.g., DBT-A).
  • Long (12–36 month) programs may disrupt school & social development.
  • Consider comorbidity & medication where indicated.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Discussion & Conclusions. (Not individual clinical advice.)

Generalizability

Applies best to…

  • Clinical, BPD-heavy samples
  • Predominantly female patients
  • Specialist outpatient / day-hospital settings
  • Adults for BPD-symptom benefit

Uncertain for…

  • Males & gender-diverse individuals
  • Non-Western / low-resource settings
  • Community, ED, or brief-contact services
  • People without BPD, or with psychosis/severe cognitive impairment
The evidence base is geographically narrow (mainly European) and demographically narrow — caution before generalising to Indian clinical settings.

Future Applications & Implications

Research

  • Standardise MBT format, duration & control conditions
  • Separate suicidal vs. non-suicidal self-harm
  • Measure mentalizing to test the mechanism
  • Larger, more diverse RCTs with longer follow-up

Services

  • Offer MBT within stepped-care, not by default
  • Invest in training & supervision
  • Weigh cost-effectiveness of long programs
  • Improve continuity after discharge

Relapse prevention

  • Identify early warning signs & interpersonal triggers
  • Build alternative coping strategies
  • Strengthen support networks; monitor recurrence

Psychosocial

  • Involve family/caregivers (esp. adolescents)
  • Address trauma & attachment
  • Coordinate multidisciplinary care

What Could I Have Done as a Researcher?

  • Recruit larger, more diverse samples (males, non-Western, community).
  • Use rigorous randomization & blinded outcome assessment.
  • Include active psychotherapy comparators (e.g., DBT).
  • Clearly separate suicidal from non-suicidal self-harm.
  • Add longer follow-up to test durability.
  • Standardise format & dose; monitor treatment fidelity.
  • Measure mentalizing/reflective functioning to test mechanism.
  • Report therapist training, supervision, adverse events, dropout fully.
  • Add patient-reported experience & cost-effectiveness.
  • Pre-register & follow PRISMA (this study did pre-register — a strength).

Key Takeaways

1. Self-harm is common, recurrent, and strongly linked to BPD and suicide risk — effective treatment matters.
2. In pre–post analyses, MBT(-A) showed large improvements (g = −0.82 to −1.1) in self-harm, BPD & depression.
3. But against active controls (RCTs), MBT(-A) was not superior for self-harm or depression.
4. The only significant advantage: MBT reduced BPD symptoms in adults (g = −0.56, moderate).
5. High heterogeneity, small samples, high dropout & high risk of bias lower confidence — especially for MBT-A.
6. MBT(-A) should not be a first-line self-harm treatment; adult BPD is where future MBT research is most encouraged.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Abstract, Results & Conclusions.
