Why This Paper Matters

A serious, widespread problem

  • Self-harm usually starts and is at its worst during the teenage years.
  • About 22.9% of adolescents and 5.5% of adults have self-harmed at some point.
  • Self-harm is a major warning sign for later death by suicide — even when the person did not intend to die.
  • It is very common in borderline personality disorder (BPD = a condition with intense mood swings, unstable relationships and self-image): up to 90–95% have a history of self-harm.

Why review the evidence now?

  • MBT is often called a promising treatment for self-harm.
  • But earlier reviews gave mixed, disagreeing results.
  • No earlier review looked only at MBT for self-harm.
  • No one had clearly compared the adult version (MBT) with the teenage version (MBT-A).

The Clinical Problem: What Is Self-Harm?

Definition used in this paper

Following Hawton et al. (2012), self-harm means deliberately poisoning or injuring oneself, whatever the reason and whether or not the person meant to die.

So it covers acts done with a wish to die and acts done without a wish to die.

In simple words: any act where a person purposely hurts their own body — the paper counts it as self-harm regardless of the motive.

Common forms

CuttingBurning Hitting oneselfScratching Self-poisoningStopping wounds from healing

What it does for the person

It often works as a way to escape unbearable feelings, or to feel connected again when the person feels alone or numb.

Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI)

Hurting the body without a wish to die — usually to relieve unbearable emotion. Often repeated, lower immediate lethality.

Suicidal self-injury

Self-harm carried out with some wish to die. Higher risk; may escalate. These two overlap and can be hard to tell apart.

Clinical caution: Always assess self-harm for suicidal intent (wish to die), lethality (how dangerous the method is), frequency, triggers, function (what it does for the person), and current safety — even when it is called "non-suicidal". This paper's definition deliberately combines both, which is itself a limitation (discussed later).
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction; definition based on Hawton et al. (2012).

Self-Harm and Repetition (Recurrence)

Why it keeps coming back

Self-harm gives quick, short-lived relief from distress. That relief acts as a reward, making the person more likely to do it again — a cycle that is hard to stop.

Among BPD inpatients, 51–65% have self-harmed 25 or more times in their lives (Goodman et al., 2017).

MBT tries to break this cycle by working on the drop in "mentalizing" (the ability to think clearly about feelings) that happens under stress — not just the behaviour itself.

The Gap in Current Treatment

Disagreeing evidence

Earlier reviews of MBT for BPD gave inconsistent results for self-harm.

Small effects overall

Treatments for self-harm in general show only small average benefits (Fox et al., 2020).

People drop out

Patients with strong emotional swings are hard to keep in therapy; many leave early.

No focused review

No earlier review looked specifically at MBT for self-harm.

Age may matter

Results may differ for teenagers and adults — never directly compared before.

Which format?

Unclear if individual vs. group MBT and different lengths work equally well.

What this paper adds: one review that combines both "before–after" studies and randomized controlled trials (RCT = a fair test where people are split into treatment vs. comparison groups by chance), and directly compares MBT (adults) with MBT-A (teenagers).
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction.

Why Mentalization-Based Therapy Is Relevant

Mentalizing

The ability to understand oneself and others through mental states — thoughts, feelings, wishes, beliefs and intentions.

In simple words: "reading" what is going on inside your own mind and other people's minds, instead of jumping to conclusions.

When it breaks down

Mentalizing fails under strong emotion, conflict, feeling rejected, or fear about a close relationship — the very situations that come just before self-harm.

The idea in one picture

Threat to a close relationship
↓
Mentalizing fails (can't think clearly)
↓
Misreads self & others
↓
Emotions become uncontrollable
↓
Self-harm

MBT's goal: rebuild mentalizing → calmer emotions → less impulsive self-harm.

Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction (Fonagy et al., 2018; Bateman & Fonagy, 1999).

Introduction: Three Key Ideas

Self-harm

Deliberate self-poisoning or self-injury, with or without a wish to die (Hawton et al., 2012). Strongly linked to mental illness, especially BPD.

MBT & MBT-A

A manual-based therapy (follows a written treatment guide) built from psychoanalysis, attachment theory and cognitive theory (Bateman & Fonagy, 1999). Main aim: strengthen mentalizing. MBT-A is the version adapted for adolescents.

Review + meta-analysis

A method that gathers all studies and combines their results into one average number, while checking how much studies disagree and how trustworthy they are.

Forms of MBT: it began as an intensive day-hospital program (5 days a week, 18 months); a shorter outpatient form was made later (individual + group, 18 months); MBT-G is a cheaper group-only version (12–36 months); and MBT-A (for teenagers) usually lasts 12 months and adds family sessions.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction (Bateman & Fonagy, 1999, 2006; Karterud, 2015; Taubner & Sevecke, 2015).

Research Gap & Why the Study Was Done

Gaps in earlier research

  • Studies had few participants; few were fair trials (RCTs).
  • MBT was given in different forms and lengths.
  • Comparison groups and outcome measures varied a lot.
  • Little direct comparison of adults vs. teenagers.
  • Unclear how MBT actually helps.

Why this study was needed

  • To combine all the MBT self-harm evidence in one place.
  • To estimate the overall benefit on self-harm, BPD & depression.
  • To test whether MBT is better than usual care.
  • To check what changes the size of the benefit (length, dropout, age).
  • To help clinicians choose treatments.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Introduction.

Aim, Question & Review Objective

Aim (as stated in the paper)

To separate out the findings for teenagers and adults on how well MBT(-A) reduces self-harm (the main outcome) and symptoms of BPD and depression (the secondary outcomes).

Research question

Does MBT(-A) reduce self-harm and improve BPD & depression — and is it better than comparison treatments (usual care / structured care)?

Objective vs. hypothesis

Because this is a pre-registered review (the plan was published before the work started, which prevents cherry-picking), it used set review questions rather than one experimental hypothesis. (OSF plan: OSF.IO/26JVF)

Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Abstract & Methods.

Framing the Question — PICO

PICO is a standard way to build a clear research question: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome.

P
Population (who): clinical patients who self-harm; most had BPD or personality difficulties; teenagers (MBT-A) and adults (MBT).
I
Intervention (the treatment): mentalization-based therapy — MBT (adults) or MBT-A (teenagers), including individual, group (MBT-G) and day-hospital forms.
C
Comparison (compared against): Treatment-as-Usual (TAU = the normal care people would get anyway) or Structured Clinical Management (SCM = organised, non-specialist psychiatric support). Note: "before–after" analyses have no comparison group.
O
Outcomes (what was measured): main = how often self-harm happened; secondary = BPD symptoms and depression.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Methods (inclusion criteria & outcomes).

Methodology at a Glance

Design

Pre-registered systematic review & meta-analysis. Included both fair trials (RCTs) and before–after studies.

Databases searched

Web of Science, Scopus, Embase, PubMed/Medline, Cochrane — plus a Google Scholar alert and checking reference lists.

Search window

All studies published up to 30 September 2022.

Effect size

Hedges' g (a standard number showing how big a change is), using random-effects models in R.

Heterogeneity (I²)

How much studies disagree: 25% = low, 50% = moderate, 75% = high (Higgins et al., 2003).

Quality & extras

RoB 2 tool for bias; meta-regression (does length/dropout/age change results?); sensitivity & subgroup checks.

Because most analyses had fewer than 10 studies, the authors did not report a formal publication-bias test (a check for missing negative studies) — it needs more studies to be reliable.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Methods.

How MBT Was Actually Delivered

Adult MBT

Weekly individual + group therapy; often 18 months; in a day hospital or outpatient clinic. Some added education about the illness and crisis planning.

MBT-A (teenagers)

Usually 12 months; weekly individual sessions plus monthly family sessions (Rossouw & Fonagy, 2012).

MBT-G (group)

Group + parent sessions without individual therapy; cheaper option; used by Beck 2020, Bo 2017, Tromp 2021.

Wide variation: from a 12-week brief starter group (Griffiths) to a 36-month day-hospital program (Kvarstein). This range is a big reason the studies "disagree" (high heterogeneity) and are hard to pool.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Table 1 & Discussion. Details reported per included study.

MBT Core Therapeutic Principles

MBT works indirectly: instead of attacking the self-harm behaviour head-on, it builds the patient's ability to reflect on feelings and thoughts — especially when relationships feel stressful.

"Not-knowing" stance: the therapist stays genuinely curious and does not assume they already know what the patient feels — this models good mentalizing.

What Was Measured (Outcome Measures)

Main outcome — Self-harm

How often and how severe self-harm was.

Different questionnaires were used: RTSHI(-A), DSHI-9, SSHI, SASII, DAPP-SF, plus simple episode counts. (these are all validated self-harm rating scales)

Secondary — BPD & Depression

BPD symptoms: BPFS-C, BPDSI, GSI, BEST, PAI-BOR, KABOSS-S.

Depression: BDI, BDI-Y, MFQ, RCADS.

Notice the many different tools. This is both good and bad: it covers a lot, but it makes studies harder to compare (a limitation the authors mention). Also, MBT's core target — mentalizing itself — was not measured as an outcome.

Risk of Bias (How Trustworthy Are the Studies?)

"Bias" means anything in how a study was run that could push the result in the wrong direction.

Headline finding

Of the 6 RCTs checked: 4 (66.7%) had a high risk of bias; 2 (33.3%) had a moderate risk (some concerns).

Bias was worst in the teenage (MBT-A) trials, especially in "measurement of the outcome" (how self-harm was recorded). All MBT-A studies had overall high risk of bias.

  • Randomization (was the group split truly by chance?)

Statistical Analysis — In Plain Words

Effect size (Hedges' g)

A single number showing how big the change is: 0.20 = small, 0.50 = medium, 0.80 = large (Cohen, 1988). A negative g means improvement (fewer symptoms).

Two kinds of comparison

Before–after: change within the MBT group over time.
Vs. comparison: MBT(-A) against usual/structured care — this is the real test of whether MBT truly helps.

Heterogeneity (I²)

How much the studies disagree with each other. 25% low · 50% moderate · 75% high. High I² = be careful trusting the combined number.

Extra checks

Random-effects model (assumes true effects differ across studies); meta-regression; sensitivity check (removing the odd brief pilot study).

The key idea to remember: a big before–after change can look impressive, but only a controlled comparison shows whether MBT does better than the care people already get.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Data extraction & analysis.

Results 4 — What Changes the Benefit? (Moderators)

Meta-regression (what affects the result)

  • Longer treatment → more self-harm reduction (before–after b=−0.07, p=.002; vs. comparison b=−0.22, p=.005).
  • Older average age (b=0.10, p=.045) & higher dropout (b=0.06, p=.015) → smaller self-harm benefit.
  • No such effects on BPD or depression.
p-value: smaller than 0.05 means the finding is unlikely to be due to chance.

Sensitivity & format checks

  • Removing the brief 12-week pilot (Griffiths 2019) did not change the main conclusions.
  • After removing it, MBT(-A) vs. comparison on BPD became significant (g=−0.38).
  • Individual MBT-A (1 study, 20 teens) showed a much bigger self-harm effect (g=−1.79) than group MBT-A (g=−0.43) — but it is only one small study, so treat with caution.
What this tells us: a moderator is a factor that makes the treatment work better or worse. Here, giving MBT for longer helped most, while older age and dropping out weakened the benefit. The individual-format hint is interesting but rests on a single tiny study.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Meta-regressions & exploratory sensitivity analyses (Appendix S1–S3).

Discussion — Main Findings

What looked promising

  • Big before–after improvements in self-harm, BPD & depression (g = −0.82 to −1.1).
  • Fits with earlier reviews suggesting MBT is effective.

What the fair trials (RCTs) showed

  • Not better than usual/structured care for self-harm or depression.
  • Only adults' BPD symptoms improved more with MBT (g = −0.56).
  • MBT-A's effects were smaller than typical DBT-A effects.
Main take-home message: the before–after results look encouraging, but the stronger controlled evidence does not support using MBT(-A) as a first-choice treatment for self-harm.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Discussion & Conclusions.

Discussion — Why No Extra Benefit Over Usual Care?

Mentalizing is common

Better mentalizing may happen in many good therapies, not just MBT — so the comparison groups improved too (Fonagy & Allison, 2014).

Group format limits

In group MBT, distress can spread between members; teenagers are very sensitive to peers, which may reduce benefit.

Indirect approach

MBT does not directly teach coping/emotion-control skills (unlike DBT), which may limit self-harm reduction.

The teenage brain

A normal dip in "social thinking" during puberty may make high-mentalizing demands harder for teenagers.

Longer helps

Longer treatment predicted more self-harm reduction — the dose/length may matter.

Be careful about "why"

Mentalizing itself was not measured, so the mechanism cannot be confirmed from this data.

Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Discussion.

What This Means for Clinical Practice

Reasonable to do

  • Consider MBT as one option, especially for adults with BPD.
  • Keep risk assessment & safety/crisis planning central — MBT does not replace them.
  • Make sure therapists have proper training and supervision.
  • Match the setting to the risk level; tailor to the individual.

Be cautious about

  • Do not make MBT(-A) the first-choice treatment for self-harm.
  • For teenagers, weigh shorter, skills-based options (e.g., DBT-A).
  • Long (12–36 month) programs may disrupt school and social life.
  • Consider other conditions and medication where needed.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Discussion & Conclusions. (This is general guidance, not advice for an individual patient.)

Applies best to…

  • Clinical samples with a lot of BPD
  • Mostly female patients
  • Specialist outpatient / day-hospital settings
  • Adults, for the BPD-symptom benefit

Uncertain for…

  • Males & gender-diverse people
  • Non-Western / low-resource settings (e.g., much of India)
  • Community, emergency, or brief-contact services
  • People without BPD, or with psychosis / serious cognitive problems
The evidence comes mostly from European settings and mostly female samples — so be cautious before assuming the same results in Indian clinical settings.
Source: Interpretation of Hajek Gross et al. (2024) sample characteristics & Limitations.

Future Applications & Implications

For research

  • Standardise MBT format, length & comparison groups
  • Separate suicidal vs. non-suicidal self-harm
  • Measure mentalizing to test how MBT works
  • Larger, more diverse trials with longer follow-up

For services

  • Offer MBT within stepped care (start simple, step up if needed), not by default
  • Invest in training & supervision
  • Weigh the cost of long programs
  • Improve follow-up after discharge

For relapse prevention

  • Spot early warning signs & relationship triggers
  • Build alternative coping strategies
  • Strengthen support; monitor for recurrence

For psychosocial care

  • Involve family/caregivers (especially for teenagers)
  • Address trauma & attachment difficulties
  • Coordinate team-based care
Source: Extends Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Discussion & Conclusions.

What Could I Have Done as a Researcher?

A reflective, critical-appraisal slide — how the study could be made stronger.

  • Recruit larger, more diverse samples (males, non-Western, community).
  • Use proper randomization and blinded (independent) outcome raters.
  • Include active comparison therapies (e.g., DBT), not just usual care.
  • Clearly separate suicidal from non-suicidal self-harm.
  • Add longer follow-up to see if benefits last.
  • Standardise the format and length; check treatment fidelity (that MBT was delivered as intended).
  • Measure mentalizing to test the mechanism.
  • Report training, supervision, side-effects and dropout fully.
  • Add patient experience and cost-effectiveness data.
  • Pre-register and follow PRISMA (this study did pre-register — a real strength).

Key Takeaways

1. Self-harm is common, repeats often, and is strongly linked to BPD and suicide risk — effective treatment really matters.
2. In before–after results, MBT(-A) showed large improvements (g = −0.82 to −1.1) in self-harm, BPD & depression.
3. But in fair trials (RCTs), MBT(-A) was not better than usual/structured care for self-harm or depression.
4. The only real advantage: MBT reduced BPD symptoms in adults (g = −0.56, medium effect).
5. Studies disagreed a lot, were small, had high dropout and high risk of bias — so confidence is limited, especially for teenagers.
6. MBT(-A) should not be a first-choice self-harm treatment; adult BPD is where more MBT research is most worthwhile.
Source: Hajek Gross et al. (2024) — Abstract, Results & Conclusions.
