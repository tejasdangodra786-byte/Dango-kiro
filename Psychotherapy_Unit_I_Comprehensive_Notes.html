Topic A 1 · Definitions of Psychotherapy

What psychotherapy is — the problem of definition, the canonical working definition, and how different traditions circumscribe the term.

1.1 Introduction

Psychotherapy (literally "treatment of the mind/soul"; broadly, the treatment of psychological problems by psychological means) is one of the most ambiguous terms in the mental-health lexicon. Everyday speech blends it with helping, treating, advising, guiding, educating and influencing, and different schools attach different meanings. Wolberg observes that the term is genuinely slippery and that published definitions agree on only one point — that psychotherapy addresses problems of an emotional nature — while disagreeing on almost everything else. (Wolberg-Technique, p.29; p.41)

For the trainee, a precise definition matters because it circumscribes the boundaries of operation — it tells us who may practise, on whom, by what means, and towards what end — which in turn makes the activity teachable, regulable and researchable. A definition is therefore not a pedantic exercise: it is the hinge on which training standards (Topic 3), ethics and law (Topic 4), rights and responsibilities (Topic 5) and the whole evidence base (Topic 10) all turn.

Etymology and historical origin

The word itself tells a story. "Psychotherapy means caring for another person's soul or being, as derived from the Greek psyche (soul or being) and therapeutikos (caring for another)." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.0) (Kleinke, 1994, cited in Notes-Psychotherapies.) The earliest and most famous informal description is "the talking cure" (the phrase coined by Anna O., Breuer's early psychoanalytic patient, to capture that talking through and verbalising one's pain can itself be healing). It is "an elegant, albeit vague description" that "does not satisfy contemporary psychotherapy researchers, but … does provide an historical and foundational frame." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.0) Modern practice, by contrast, "combines the synergistic power of a wealth of biological, psychological, and social resources … requiring a great deal of creativity, competence, and collaboration." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.0)

Psychotherapy as a unilateral professional relationship

A defining structural feature, often examined, is that psychotherapy is "a unilateral professional relationship (a deliberately 'one-way' relationship in which the patient's problems — not the therapist's needs — are the sole focus) … circumscribed by limits on time, frequency of contact, content of discussions, and level of intimacy." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.1) This is why "a psychologist will not be effective if he or she tries to be a friend, a colleague, or a lover," and why confidentiality — with explicit "ethical and legal limits … including serious and immediate danger to self and others" — and the prohibition on dual relationships are built into the very concept of what psychotherapy is, not bolted on afterwards. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.1) These limits anticipate Topics 4 and 5 directly.

1.2 Definitions (identify source / author)

💬 Canonical working definition — Wolberg

"Psychotherapy is the treatment, by psychological means, of problems of an emotional nature in which a trained person deliberately establishes a professional relationship with the patient with the object of (1) removing, modifying, or retarding existing symptoms, (2) mediating disturbed patterns of behavior, and (3) promoting positive personality growth and development." (Wolberg-Technique, p.29)

Wolberg deconstructs the definition clause by clause: "treatment" (it is a form of treatment, not merely re-education, helping or guidance — those words only describe what happens inside it); and "psychological means" (a generic umbrella covering the whole spectrum of psychological methods, e.g. interpretation, support, behavioural techniques). (Wolberg-Technique, p.29)

⚠️ The sceptical "definition" — Boulder Conference (1949)

Corsini quotes the wry Boulder-era description of psychotherapy as "an undefined technique which is applied to unspecified problems with a nonpredictable outcome. For this technique we recommend rigorous training" (Lehner, 1952). Corsini stresses there is still "no general agreement about the definition, methodology, or value" of evidence-based psychotherapy. The Boulder Conference (the 1949 conference that established the scientist-practitioner model of clinical-psychology training) arose from the APA's Shakow committee, appointed under then-president Carl Rogers. (Corsini, p.592)

💬 Contemporary / APA-adopted definition — Norcross (1990)

Psychotherapy is "the informed and intentional application of clinical methods and interpersonal stances derived from established psychological principles for the purpose of assisting people to modify their behaviors, cognitions, emotions, and/or other personal characteristics in directions that the participants deem desirable." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.0–1; APA, 2012 Resolution) (Norcross, 1990, p.218, cited in Notes-Psychotherapies and adopted in the APA's 2012 Resolution on the Recognition of Psychotherapy Effectiveness.)

The American Psychological Association (APA) (the major US professional body for psychology) describes psychotherapy, in general terms, as any psychological service provided by a trained professional that primarily uses communication and interaction to assess, diagnose and treat dysfunctional emotional reactions, ways of thinking and behaviour patterns. (APA Dictionary; verify against live entry) Note how this echoes Wolberg's three clauses: it names a trained professional, a relationship of communication, and defined aims.

A spectrum of historical definitions (identify the author)

The Notes list a telling spectrum, running from the playful to the near-scientific, each attributable to a named author — a frequent exam gift, because quoting two or three with attribution signals breadth: (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.0; all authors cited therein)

  1. "A conversation with a therapeutic purpose" (Korchin, 1976).
  2. "The purchase of friendship" (Schofield, 1964) — a deliberately provocative framing of the paid, bounded relationship.
  3. Two people interacting "with the specific goal of accomplishing something beneficial for the complaining person" (Bruch, 1981).
  4. "When one person with an emotional disorder gets help from another person who has less of an emotional disorder" (J. Watkins, 1983).
  5. Wolberg (1995) — the operational, clause-by-clause definition reproduced above.

Other framings observed across sources

  • Problem-centred (Wolberg-506Q). The recurring "general aspects" of psychotherapy are its basic ingredients, the factors producing therapeutic change, the therapeutic alliance (the collaborative, affective working bond between therapist and client), insight, resistance and termination. (Wolberg-506Q, p.16)
  • Person-centred / anti-reductionist (McWilliams). Treatment "ought to flow from a comprehensive understanding of the client" rather than fitting patients "à la Procrustes" to a fixed technique — a caution against defining therapy as a single method. (McWilliams, p.8)
  • Common-factors framing. Beyond any single definition, the Notes identify four common factors present whenever psychotherapy helps: (1) a relationship carrying confidence that the therapist is competent and caring; (2) a shared expectancy/hope of benefit; (3) an underlying conceptual framework / rationale with procedures requiring the active participation of both; and (4) a socially sanctioned healing setting. On this view, psychotherapy is partly defined by the common ingredients that make any helping conversation curative. "Generally 70% to 80% of those treated in psychotherapy are improved (Smith et al., 1980)." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.1) (Smith et al., 1980, cited in Notes-Psychotherapies.)

1.3 Core concepts within the definition

  • A trained person. Competence and training are built into the definition (hence Topic 3). An untrained listener giving advice is not doing psychotherapy.
  • Deliberate, professional relationship. Distinguishes therapy from friendship or spontaneous emotional support; the relationship is purposeful, bounded and ethically governed (Topics 4–5).
  • Psychological means. Change is sought through psychological processes (learning, relationship, insight, emotional processing) rather than primarily physical means such as medication or surgery.
  • Problems of an emotional nature. The target is psychological distress, maladaptive patterns and impaired functioning.
  • Defined objectives. Symptom change, behavioural change and personality growth (expanded in Topic 2).

1.4 Theoretical foundations: why traditions define it differently

The definitional disagreement maps onto the major theoretical families. The CBT/empirical tradition (Wolberg's operational stance) prefers a precise, bounded definition so the activity can be measured and studied; the psychodynamic/humanistic voice resists reducing a deeply personal, relational process to a replicable "technique." (Wolberg-Technique, p.29; McWilliams, p.8) This is a classic exam contrast: operational definition vs. understanding-based practice.

The Notes name four foundational schools, each of which implicitly defines psychotherapy by what it treats as the agent of change: (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.2)

  • Psychoanalytic / psychodynamic — change comes through insight and the relationship itself, offering a "corrective emotional experience" (re-experiencing an old conflict with a new, healthier outcome in the therapy relationship); attends to transference (the client's feelings redirected onto the therapist) and countertransference (the therapist's own reactions to the client). (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.2)
  • Behavioural / cognitive-behavioural (CBT) — change comes through new learning; CBT "places an emphasis on analyzing current problems, defining concrete goals, and developing strategies so that the client can achieve specific, measurable changes." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.3)
  • Humanistic / experiential — change comes through growth, acceptance and the actualising tendency within an authentic, empathic relationship.
  • Family systems — change comes through altering patterns of relating within the family or wider system.

1.6 Clinical application

🧩 Illustrative example

Illustrative teaching vignette. A trainee receives a referral for "a 28-year-old with panic attacks." Applying the definition operationally, the trainee asks: is a trained person forming a deliberate professional relationship, using psychological means, targeting emotional problems, with defined objectives? If medication alone were offered with no psychological work, it would be psychiatric treatment, not psychotherapy; if a friend simply offered reassurance, it would be neither.

🧩 Illustrative example — the "unilateral relationship" test

Illustrative teaching vignette. A client asks the therapist out for coffee "as friends." The therapist uses the unilateral professional relationship criterion: the relationship is bounded in time, frequency, content and intimacy, and the patient's — not the therapist's — concerns are the focus. Accepting the invitation would blur it into a dual relationship and compromise the very thing that defines the work. The therapist warmly declines and explores the wish in session. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.1)

1.8 Advantages, limitations & challenges of defining psychotherapy

  • Advantage of a tight definition: enables regulation, training standards and outcome research.
  • Limitation: an overly narrow definition may exclude legitimate relational/humanistic work that resists manualisation (McWilliams). (McWilliams, p.8)
  • Challenge: boundary overlap with counselling, coaching and psychiatric care makes any single definition contestable (Boulder critique). (Corsini, p.592)
Supplementary depth — "What is psychotherapy?" as an exam essay spine

A strong answer (i) opens with the definitional ambiguity (Wolberg; Boulder), (ii) gives Wolberg's working definition verbatim, (iii) unpacks its five core concepts, (iv) contrasts operational vs understanding-based traditions, and (v) closes by situating it against counselling, assessment and psychiatric treatment. This structure doubles as revision scaffolding for Topics 1–2.

  • One-line definition: treatment, by psychological means, of emotional problems, by a trained person in a deliberate professional relationship, aiming at symptom change + pattern change + growth (Wolberg, p.29).

Topic A 2 · Objectives of Psychotherapy

Why we do it — the canonical goal statement, how goals are framed across traditions, and how global goals become measurable objectives.

2.1 Introduction

The objectives (the intended ends or purposes of the treatment) of psychotherapy follow directly from the definition. Clear objectives guide technique selection, allow progress to be monitored, and protect the client from aimless or interminable treatment. Objectives are negotiated collaboratively, not imposed. (Corey, p.63)

A crucial opening caution: "the list of possible goals of psychotherapy is endless" — behavioural change, better relationships, insight, support, or concrete outcomes such as keeping a job, a partner, staying out of hospital, or staying alive. Goals "may also be different for the patient, for the significant others … and for the psychologist." For example, the patient may want anxiety reduction, the psychologist may want increased insight, and a spouse may want increased independence. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.3) Because of this, objectives must be explicitly negotiated; unspoken, divergent goals are a major source of drift, dropout and ethical difficulty (links to Topics 4 and 8).

Overall, psychotherapy "is intended to enhance the client's mental health, flexibility, quality of relationships, and well-being" through change in "cognition, perception, self-understanding, relational capacities, and overt behavior." For children and adolescents, collaborative work with families and schools promotes complementary change. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.2)

The ethical grounding of all objectives — beneficence & nonmaleficence

💬 Why the aims are what they are

"If we apply the traditional medical ethical principles of beneficence (acting for the patient's good) and nonmaleficence (doing no harm), then psychotherapy … should aim to help the patients with their problems, make them feel better and do them no harm." Most therapists pursue this by increasing the patient's capacity for self-reflection and awareness of the links between feelings and actions. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.4)

2.2 The canonical objectives (source: Wolberg)

💬 Wolberg's three objectives
  1. Removing, modifying or retarding existing symptoms — e.g. reducing panic attacks or low mood.
  2. Mediating (changing) disturbed patterns of behaviour — altering maladaptive interpersonal or coping patterns.
  3. Promoting positive personality growth and development — fostering maturation, resilience and fuller functioning.

(Wolberg-Technique, p.29)

2.3 Kleinke's six common therapeutic goals

Cutting across the 450+ schools, Kleinke (1994) identified six fundamental therapeutic goals common to almost all psychotherapies — the single most citable "objectives" list for an exam because it names the shared change processes rather than any one school's technique: (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.3–4) (Kleinke, 1994, cited in Notes-Psychotherapies.)

  1. Overcoming demoralisation and gaining hope — restoring the expectancy that change is possible (links to the "hope" common factor, Topic 1).
  2. Enhancing mastery and self-efficacy (the belief in one's capacity to succeed at a task) — building a sense of competence and agency.
  3. Overcoming avoidance — approaching, rather than escaping, feared situations and emotions.
  4. Becoming aware of one's misconceptions — identifying and correcting distorted beliefs about self, others and the world.
  5. Accepting life's realities — coming to terms with what cannot be changed.
  6. Achieving insight — understanding the links between history, feelings and current patterns.

2.4 Core concepts & theoretical foundations

  • Adaptive change in affect, behaviour and mentation. Corsini (Dumont) frames the "core objectives" as adaptive change across feeling, action and thought, helping the patient "fashion alternative and future memories" and build social, interpersonal, self-disciplinary and technical skills — described neurologically as learning-driven neuronal restructuring. (Corsini, p.23)
  • Collaborative goal-setting. Corey: the therapist should discuss general goals (and length, duration, confidentiality and methods) early, developing goals with the client. (Corey, p.63)
  • Over-responsibility is a risk. Assuming full responsibility for outcomes disempowers clients and courts burnout (emotional exhaustion and reduced efficacy from chronic occupational stress). (Corey, p.63)
  • Goals can conflict with the modality. "In CBT, the patient may want to remove all their negative emotion, while the therapist wants to decrease excess emotion"; CBT may relieve symptoms short-term while "psychodynamic therapies may not" — the clinician's goal "may not be what the patient sees as the goal of therapy." Surfacing this difference is itself a therapeutic task. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.4)

2.5 From goals to measurable objectives (Jongsma)

2.6 Clinical application & examples

🧩 Illustrative example

Illustrative teaching vignette. For a client with social anxiety, a long-term goal might be "reduce social anxiety and participate comfortably in work meetings." A matched short-term objective might be "attend 2 team meetings per week and speak at least once, rated for anxiety 0–10, over 4 weeks." The goal gives direction; the objective makes progress auditable (links to Topics 7 and 9).

2.8 Advantages, limitations & challenges

  • Advantage: explicit objectives enable outcome measurement and third-party accountability.
  • Limitation / tension: symptom-focused measurable objectives (CBT/behavioural) may under-represent insight and personality-growth aims valued by psychodynamic work.
  • Challenge: balancing the client's priorities with clinically indicated goals without the therapist over-owning the outcome. (Corey, p.63)

Topic B 3 · Training Professional Therapists

How therapists are made — the self as instrument, structure of training, supervision, personal therapy, continuing professional development and competence measurement.

3.1 Introduction

Because the definition of psychotherapy builds in "a trained person," the quality of training (the formal, supervised preparation of therapists) is central to the field's legitimacy and to client safety. Training is unusual among professions because the practitioner's own personality is a primary working tool.

Training is also, fundamentally, a risk-management system. The Notes put it bluntly: "Negligence and incompetence are potential risks for all clinicians, and these can (and should) be addressed by training, licensing or registration, and supervision processes." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.5) On this view the whole pipeline — selection, training, personal therapy, supervision and continuing development — exists to protect clients from a practitioner's blind spots and to make competence demonstrable rather than assumed. In India this is operationalised by the Rehabilitation Council of India (RCI) (the statutory body that regulates training and maintains the register of clinical psychologists) under the RCI Act, 1992 — stated generally; verify against the primary statute.

3.2 Core concept — the self as instrument

💬 Lister-Ford

"The chief instrument for most psychotherapists is the self." Effective practice is a "well-blended synthesis of art, science and reflection," and trainees must "come to know themselves at least as well as the theories they are studying," usually through personal therapy (the trainee undergoing their own therapy to increase self-awareness and manage personal reactions). (Lister-Ford, p.120)

3.3 Structure and length of training

  • UK / Europe (Lister-Ford). The UKCP (UK Council for Psychotherapy) requires trainees to be 25 before commencing; minimum training length is 3–4 years depending on the regulatory body; the European minimum (EAP, European Association for Psychotherapy) is 4 years; most qualify around age 30. Required personal qualities: maturity, sensitivity, and the capacity for self-reflection — using one's own responses as a "barometer." (Lister-Ford, p.121)
⚠️ Context note

These age/length figures are UK/European regulatory requirements reported by Lister-Ford (2007); Indian training routes (e.g. the RCI-regulated M.Phil. Clinical Psychology) differ. See Topic 4 for the Indian regulatory frame, stated at a general level.

3.4 Supervision, personal therapy & CPD

  • Supervision & monitoring. The CORE (Clinical Outcomes in Routine Evaluation) system monitors effectiveness and helps identify continuing professional development (CPD) (ongoing, lifelong learning to maintain and extend competence after qualification) needs. (Lister-Ford, p.118)
  • Personal therapy & countertransference. Corey: personal therapy helps the counsellor recognise and manage countertransference (the therapist's own, often unconscious, emotional reactions to the client that can distort the work); beginning-therapist issues include anxiety, perfectionism and tolerating ambiguity. (Corey, p.63; p.12)
  • Oral tradition & workforce risk. McWilliams warns new therapists may carry caseloads of ~60 patients "with no provision for supervision or continuing education," and that much learning is an "oral tradition" transmitted by supervisors and personal therapy. (McWilliams, p.8; p.9)
  • Manualised training. The MET manual notes therapists require "intensive training and supervision" on a standardised protocol; Project MATCH documented procedures to select, train and supervise therapists. (MET, p.13)

3.5 Measuring competence & fidelity

  • CTRS (Beck). The Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS) is "used in many research studies and training programs to measure therapist competency," alongside a Cognitive Case Write-Up based on the Academy of Cognitive Therapy certification template. (Beck-CBT, p.16)
  • Fidelity vs competence. Fidelity (adherence — delivering the protocol as intended) and competence (skilful delivery) are distinct; EBP dissemination depends on both (see Topic 10). The 1995 Division 12 report was titled Training in and Dissemination of Empirically-Validated Psychological Treatments. (Hofmann-ArtScience, p.53)
  • Assessment skill is a core, trainable competence. "Successful psychotherapy matches the right therapy and right therapist to the patient, which is why therapists need to develop good quality assessment skills, not just for their own discipline but for others" — being able to formulate the same problem in several ways. Therapist characteristics (competence and personality features) themselves influence process and outcome. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.2; p.4–5)
  • Record-keeping supports thinking and supervision. Keeping a record "is a way to do this; and also helps with the process of thinking and supervision" — the clinical record is not only a legal document but a reflective tool that feeds supervision (links to Topic 7). (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.6)

🧩 Illustrative example

Illustrative teaching vignette. A first-year trainee notices intense irritation towards a demanding client. Rather than acting on it, she brings it to supervision and personal therapy, recognising it as countertransference. She also audio-records a session (with consent) to be rated on the CTRS, converting self-awareness into measurable competence feedback.

3.9 Advantages, limitations & challenges

  • Advantage: supervised, self-aware, CPD-driven training protects clients and sustains competence.
  • Limitation: competence measures (CTRS) fit manualised CBT better than open-ended psychodynamic work.
  • Challenge: under-resourced services may provide little supervision/CPD, risking harm and burnout. (McWilliams, p.8)

Topic C 4 · Ethical & Legal Issues

The ethical spine of practice — informed consent, confidentiality vs privilege, exceptions/duty to report, boundaries and dual relationships, technology ethics, a decision-making framework, and the Indian legal frame (general level).

4.1 Introduction

Ethics (professional standards of right conduct that protect clients and the profession) and law (binding rules enforced by the state) overlap but are not identical. A key exam skill is distinguishing ethical guidance (professional codes, aspirational) from binding law (statutes, enforceable). Where they conflict, practitioners must reason carefully, document, and often consult.

The Notes offer an elegant definition of ethics itself: any discussion of an ethical dilemma "involves a special type of dialog; the discourse of ought and should in interpersonal relationships" — how therapists ought to behave and what they should do in difficult situations with patients and colleagues. There is a "close kinship" between ethics and therapy: "just as ethical debate is all about how individuals should treat other people … so psychotherapy explores interpersonal relating." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.8) Crucially, law is not automatically ethical: legal cases "should not be understood as legal advice … nor does the law always provide an ethically justifiable source of guidance, as is clear if we remember the impact of both the Nazi and South African race laws." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.8–9)

Why this matters so much: there are "over 450 schools of psychotherapy currently in existence (Simon, 2001, p. 90)," yet "basic clinical–legal principles" apply across most of them, and negligence claims against psychotherapists have "substantially increased." Every clinician needs a working knowledge of these principles to avoid "maladaptive defensive practices." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7) (Simon, 2001, cited in Notes-Psychotherapies.) Ethical dilemmas cluster in three domains: "(1) goals and objectives of therapy; (2) the boundaries between their different identities; (3) the social and political frameworks in which they work." The overarching duty is simply "to make the best quality decision that can be made" (one of fourteen approaches to ethical reasoning catalogued by Tjelveit, 1999). (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.8–9) (Tjelveit, 1999, cited in Notes-Psychotherapies.)

⚖️ The APA Ethics Code — the standards most often examined

The APA Ethical Principles of Psychologists and Code of Conduct (the APA's binding-on-members ethics code, 2002, amended 2010 & 2017) opens with five aspirational General Principles — A: Beneficence & Nonmaleficence; B: Fidelity & Responsibility; C: Integrity; D: Justice; E: Respect for People's Rights & Dignity — and then enforceable Ethical Standards, including Informed Consent (Standard 3.10 general; 10.01 therapy-specific), Confidentiality (Standard 4, with its limits and mandated disclosures), and Multiple Relationships (Standard 3.05). (APA Ethics Code, apa.org/ethics) Stated at a general level; verify exact standard wording and numbers against the live APA Ethics Code. These are professional-guideline standards, distinct from binding statute.

4.2 Informed consent (source: Corey)

💬 Corey

"Informed consent is an ethical and legal requirement that is an integral part of the therapeutic process." It establishes the foundation for the working alliance and is "far broader than simply making sure clients sign the appropriate forms." (Corey, p.75)

Informed consent (the client's voluntary, knowing agreement to treatment after disclosure of relevant information) elements include: general goals, counsellor and client responsibilities, the limitations of and exceptions to confidentiality, legal/ethical parameters, and the practitioner's qualifications. Consent may be written, oral or combined; if oral, the therapist must document its nature and extent in the clinical record. (Corey, p.75; p.76)

⚠️ Consent is a legal duty — and psychotherapy has risks

Consent is not a courtesy. "Some states that have informed consent statutes declare the failure to obtain informed consent to be negligence (Slovenko, 1989)." Even a purely "talking" treatment needs informed consent because "all psychotherapies have risks and benefits" — for example untoward transference reactions, regressive dependency, or a general worsening before improvement. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7–8) (Slovenko, 1989, cited in Notes-Psychotherapies.) This is also why the therapist acquires a legal duty of care (an enforceable obligation to practise with reasonable skill): to practise "in accordance with a reasonable body of … psychotherapeutic opinion," make logical treatment decisions, conform to ethical guidance, obtain informed consent, tell the truth when asked, and abide by mandatory-reporting law. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.10)

4.4 Exceptions to confidentiality / duty to report

⚠️ Mandatory breaches (Corey)

Confidentiality must be broken, as a legal requirement, for: child abuse, elder abuse, abuse of dependent adults, and danger to self or others. (Corey, p.77)

Four sources of the duty of confidentiality

The duty is not a single rule but a convergence of four sources, worth listing in an answer: (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7)

  1. Ethical codes of the mental-health professions (e.g. APA Standard 4).
  2. Professional licensure regulations and confidentiality & privilege statutes.
  3. Common-law privilege — the attorney–client privilege extended (less stringently) to psychotherapists by case law.
  4. Constitutional privacy guarantees (noting that no explicit constitutional right of privacy exists in that source).

A landmark illustration is Jaffe v. Redmond (1996) (the US Supreme Court case), which held that psychotherapist–patient communications are confidential/privileged in federal trials (though it did not bind state courts). (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7) (US case law reported in Notes-Psychotherapies; illustrative of the principle, not Indian law.)

4.5 Boundaries & dual/multiple relationships

  • Sexual involvement — absolute prohibition. Corsini (Pope & Wedding): "No circumstances or rationale justify sexual involvement with a patient"; the prohibition predates the Hippocratic Oath. Harm clusters into ten areas (e.g. guilt, emptiness/isolation, impaired trust, confused roles/boundaries, emotional lability, suppressed rage, increased suicidal risk, cognitive dysfunction). A survey of 5,148 therapists found ~4.4% reported sex with a patient (male 6.8% vs female 1.6%). (Corsini, p.597; p.598)
  • Nonsexual touch & multiple relationships. Dual / multiple relationship (having a second, non-therapeutic role with a client, e.g. business or social, that risks impaired judgement or exploitation). Nonsexual touch is distinct from sexual involvement and has no inherent harm, but context, culture, competence and consent must be weighed; the same gender patterns extend to supervisor–supervisee and professor–student relationships. (Corsini, p.597; p.598)

4.6 Technology / telepsychology ethics

Corey notes that Section H of the ACA (American Counseling Association) Code of Ethics (2014) governs technology and telepsychology (delivering psychological services remotely via phone/video/internet) — covering computer-mediated communication, social media as a delivery platform, distance-counselling informed consent and security, client verification, records, and professional boundaries. (Corey, p.76; p.77)

Topic D 5 · Rights & Responsibilities

The collaborative contract — client rights, shared responsibilities, the therapist's duties, and how these map onto informed consent.

5.1 Introduction

Rights and responsibilities make the therapeutic relationship a collaborative partnership (a working alliance in which duties and decisions are explicitly shared rather than imposed). Corey frames informed consent itself as the vehicle for client rights: it "involves the right of clients to be informed about their therapy and to make autonomous decisions pertaining to it," turning clients into "active partners and true collaborators." (Corey, p.75)

Rights and responsibilities are two sides of one coin: every client right implies a matching therapist duty. The Notes state the core pairing directly: "Patients have the right and psychotherapists the duty to have spoken or written communications during the course of treatment kept confidential." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7) The underpinning ethical principle is respect for autonomy (the client's freedom to choose or refuse treatment for themselves): "patients should be free to choose or refuse for themselves what treatment they have." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.4)

5.2 Client rights (sources: Corey; Notes-Psychotherapies)

  • Right to be informed & to decide (autonomy). Clients have the right to information about therapy and to make autonomous decisions about it — including the right to refuse or withdraw; educating clients about rights and responsibilities empowers them and builds trust. (Corey, p.75; Notes-Psychotherapies, p.4)
  • Right to confidentiality (within limits). The client holds the right, and the therapist the duty, that communications be kept confidential — subject to the documented exceptions in Topic 4. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7)
  • Right to be warned of the risks of therapy. The Notes argue that "few therapists warn patients of the risks of a proposed psychotherapy, although the potential benefits may be emphasized" — yet clients should be told "that the process of psychotherapy is not always comfortable, and that many patients feel worse before they feel better." This is a genuine, often-neglected right flowing from informed consent. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7; p.4)
  • Right to know about supervision. Clients have a right to know their therapist may discuss details of the relationship with a supervisor or colleague. (Corey, p.76)
  • Rights embedded in consent elements. The "client bill of rights" maps directly onto the informed-consent elements — goals, responsibilities, confidentiality limits, parameters and qualifications (Topic 4). (Corey, p.75)

5.3 Shared & therapist responsibilities

  • Two responsibility errors (Corey). (a) Assuming full responsibility for direction/outcome — disempowers clients and risks burnout; (b) refusing responsibility for accurate assessment and treatment planning. How responsibility is shared "should be addressed early in the course of counseling." (Corey, p.63)
  • Therapist's explicit duties. Discuss length and duration of sessions, confidentiality, general goals, and methods. (Corey, p.63)
  • Responsibility for the whole person (Corsini/Dumont). Therapists are "responsible to some degree for both the natural and nurturant components of the patients' lives"; prescribing psychoactive medication "without assuming responsibility for the patient's concurrent experience" is described as irresponsible. (Corsini, p.23; p.24)
  • Duty to third parties — and to the client's confidentiality. When family members or employers contact the therapist, the therapist "may have to balance the patient's claim to honesty and confidentiality against a possible harm to them," and must seek the patient's consent before disclosing. The Notes reframe confidentiality itself as "the principle of informed consent to disclosure." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.5; p.6)
  • Duty not to exploit the relationship. "A psychologist will not be effective if he or she tries to be a friend, a colleague, or a lover"; the professional relationship must not be compromised by dual relationships — the therapist's core fiduciary responsibility. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.1)

Topic E 6 · Issues of Consent & Assent in Minors

Who may agree to a child's therapy — the distinct roles of consent and assent, developmental capacity, confidentiality limits, safeguarding, and why there is no single universal rule.

6.1 Introduction

When the client is a minor (a person below the legal age of majority; in India, below 18 years under the Indian Majority Act 1875 — general knowledge, verify against the primary statute), the ordinary adult model of autonomous informed consent cannot simply be transferred. A child may lack the legal standing and sometimes the developmental capacity to authorise their own treatment, yet forcing therapy on an unwilling child is both unethical and clinically self-defeating. The solution adopted across professional ethics is a two-part arrangement: legally valid consent from a parent or guardian, plus developmentally appropriate assent from the minor. (Corey, p.75; p.76)

⚠️ No single universal rule

There is no one rule that governs every minor in every setting. What is required depends on (a) the applicable law and jurisdiction, (b) the circumstances (age, maturity, risk, whether abuse or self-harm is involved, custody arrangements), and (c) the type of service (routine counselling vs. emergency/crisis vs. research vs. court-ordered assessment). Trainees must check local statute and institutional policy rather than apply a blanket formula.

6.2 Definitions (identify source / author)

💬 Consent vs. Assent
  • Informed consent (the legally and ethically valid authorisation to proceed, given by a person with the legal capacity to do so) — for a minor this is normally given by a parent or legal guardian. Corey stresses informed consent is "an ethical and legal requirement … far broader than simply making sure clients sign the appropriate forms." (Corey, p.75)
  • Assent (the minor's own developmentally appropriate agreement to participate — an affirmative "yes" that is not, by itself, legally sufficient but is ethically essential). Assent respects the child as a person and is sought using language the child can understand. (Assent as a term is general professional/ethics knowledge; the repository texts did not yield a page-level definition, so it is presented here as standard practice rather than cited to a PDF page.)
  • Guardian (the adult with legal authority and responsibility for the minor — a parent, or a court-appointed legal guardian).

6.4 Theoretical / professional foundations — autonomy & the capacity to consent

The ethical principle that underpins all consent — adult or minor — is respect for autonomy: patients should be free to choose or refuse for themselves what treatment they have. The new source states the legal test crisply: "for consent to be valid it must be given voluntarily, by a patient who is competent to make that decision, and the patient must be adequately informed about what the treatment involves, including any possible negative side-effects." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.4) This yields the three legal ingredients of valid consent — voluntariness, capacity/competence, and adequate information — each of which becomes problematic with minors.

💬 Capacity / competence (define at first use)

Capacity (competence) (the ability to understand relevant information, weigh it in the light of one's values, and communicate a choice — i.e. to exercise autonomy). The source notes capacity is "given by competent patients, who are capable of exercising their autonomy in a way that expresses their values and interests, while giving weight to conflicting values and facts." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.5) Capacity is decision-specific and can fluctuate; it is assessed, not assumed from age or diagnosis.

  • Groups who may lack competence. "In a general sense, most psychotherapy patients will be competent to make decisions about their own treatment … this may not be true for all." The source names children, patients with psychotic disorders, and patients with learning disabilities, and adds the recently traumatised or bereaved. Crucially, "the problem is usually not a global lack of competence, but rather that patients experience fluctuating levels of capacity, or experience rapid changes in how they make decisions." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.5)
  • The child-refusal puzzle. "It is hard to know what to make of a child's refusal to have therapy, which they agreed to only a day before. Can this be understood as an informed choice to refuse treatment? Or is this refusal merely evidence that the work has begun?" (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.5) A minor's day-to-day change of mind is therefore read clinically, not just as a binary consent/withdrawal.
  • Coercion & "true" voluntariness. Some patients are coerced into therapy — "children who only agree under pressure from family," or forensic clients "mandated to have treatment, as an alternative to prison." Such pressures "raise dilemmas about the nature of 'true' voluntariness." The first ingredient of valid consent — voluntariness — is thus especially fragile for minors embedded in a family's "emotional network." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.5)
  • Privilege does not extend to child/adolescent therapy. Corey: privileged communication (a legal protection preventing confidential communications from being revealed in court) "does not apply to … child and adolescent therapy, or whenever there are more than two people in the room." The parent/guardian is typically involved, so the minor's communications are not shielded in the same way as an adult's. (Corey, p.76)
  • Collateral involvement in assessment. Judith Beck lists "Involving a Family Member" as a formal part of the evaluation session — directly relevant when the client is a minor and collateral information, consent or assent is needed. (Beck-CBT, p.18 [ToC])

6.5 Confidentiality, parental involvement & safeguarding

With minors, confidentiality is shared rather than absolute. Good practice is to agree at the outset, with both the adolescent and the guardian, what will and will not be shared — for example, that general progress is shared with parents but session details are kept private unless there is a risk of serious harm. The new source reframes confidentiality itself as "better understood as the principle of informed consent to disclosure": the therapist is "under an ethical duty to obtain their patient's consent before they disclose details of their psychotherapeutic treatment to anyone; including those close to the patient." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.5) With minors this cuts both ways — a parent's legitimate wish to know must be balanced against the adolescent's claim to privacy, and against the special case where "the patient discloses material, which indicates that someone else is (or has been) at risk of harm."

⚠️ Limits of confidentiality & duty to report

Corey lists the legal requirement to break confidentiality for child abuse, elder abuse, abuse of dependent adults, and danger to self or others. With minors, a disclosure of abuse or of serious self-harm risk triggers safeguarding (action to protect a child from harm) and, where mandated, reporting — which overrides ordinary confidentiality. The new source confirms this is a legal duty of care: "psychotherapists in the USA are mandated to report suspected child abuse, even when the information comes from their patient, who may be the abuser," while acknowledging the clinician "may feel a conflict between respect for the law, and the avoidance of harm to children." (Corey, p.77; Notes-Psychotherapies, p.10)

⚠️ Third-party pressure & the "don't tell the patient" trap

Clinicians "may also come under pressure from others (family members or employers) … to seek information about the therapy." A specific ethical problem arises "when the third party asks the therapist not to tell the patient about the contact" — common with a parent of an adolescent. The clinician must then "balance the patient's claim to honesty and confidentiality against a possible harm to them." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.6)

6.5b Consent to use material for research or teaching

A distinct consent is needed before a minor's (or any patient's) material is used for teaching or research. The source warns that therapists can no longer "assume that it is ethically unproblematic to use patient material for teaching and research without consent, even if it is disguised," because of "the increased social emphasis on respect for individual patient autonomy." Some journals now require not only consent but that "the patient has read the article in which their case is mentioned"; and "getting consent after therapy has ended is not necessarily the answer, as this might be just as intrusive or distressing." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.6) For minors this means guardian consent plus the minor's assent to any teaching/research use, handled separately from consent to treatment.

6.6 Minor–guardian disagreement

Disagreements are common and must be anticipated. Examples: a guardian consents but the adolescent refuses to engage; the adolescent wants therapy a parent opposes; separated parents disagree about treatment; an adolescent discloses something they do not want their parents told. The clinician's task is to (i) clarify the legal position (who holds authority to consent), (ii) work clinically with the minor's reluctance or the family conflict, and (iii) be transparent about the limits of what can be kept private.

  • Mental Healthcare Act (MHCA), 2017 — contains provisions governing the treatment of minors and the role of the nominated representative/guardian. (Statute — verify against the primary text.)
  • Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 — treats children's personal data as requiring special protection, generally needing verifiable parental consent for processing. (Statute — verify against the primary text.)

Professional standards (e.g. the Rehabilitation Council of India framework for clinical psychologists, and ethics texts such as Corey) reinforce: obtain guardian consent, seek the minor's assent, define confidentiality limits in advance, and document. These are ethical guidance, distinct from statute.

⚖️ Clinical–legal framing — consent failures and negligence

The new source situates consent within malpractice law. "As psychotherapy is a 'talking' treatment, is informed consent necessary? The answer is yes, because all psychotherapies have risks and benefits that patients need to understand." Crucially, "informed consent doctrine provides a basis for legal recovery of compensation for patients who are harmed by failures of therapists to obtain informed consent. Some states that have informed consent statutes declare the failure to obtain informed consent to be negligence (Slovenko, 1989 — cited in Notes-Psychotherapies)." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7–8) It also notes "there are over 450 schools of psychotherapy … (Simon, 2001)" yet "basic clinical–legal principles generally apply to most, if not all, forms" — so the consent/assent duty is near-universal, not school-specific. (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7) (These are binding-law observations about US jurisdictions reported in the source; verify the position in your own jurisdiction.)

6.8 Clinical application — illustrative examples

🧩 Illustrative example — a child (age 7)

Illustrative teaching vignette. A 7-year-old is referred for anxiety. The parent gives written consent; the clinician obtains the child's assent with concrete language ("We'll play some games and talk about scary feelings — is that okay with you?"), involves the parent in the evaluation (cf. Beck's "Involving a Family Member"), and explains to the parent that session content is generally private but safety concerns will be shared. (Beck-CBT, p.18 [ToC]; Corey, p.77)

🧩 Illustrative example — an adolescent (age 16)

Illustrative teaching vignette. A 16-year-old attends with low mood and asks that her disclosures not be shared with her parents. The clinician negotiates a confidentiality agreement up front: routine content stays private, but risk of serious self-harm or abuse must be shared/reported. When she later discloses passive suicidal ideation, the clinician applies the safety exception, involves the guardian appropriately, and keeps the therapeutic alliance by having forewarned her of exactly this limit. (Corey, p.77)

6.9 Procedure — steps for consent/assent with a minor

  1. Establish who holds legal authority to consent (parent/guardian/court order).
  2. Obtain informed consent from the guardian (goals, methods, risks, confidentiality limits, fees, recording).
  3. Obtain the minor's assent using developmentally appropriate language.
  4. Agree a confidentiality framework with both parties, including the exceptions (abuse, danger to self/others).
  5. Document consent, assent and the confidentiality agreement; if consent is oral, record its nature and extent. (Corey, p.75–76)
  6. Revisit as the minor matures or circumstances change.

6.10 Advantages, limitations & challenges

  • Advantage: the consent+assent model protects the minor legally while honouring their emerging autonomy and improving engagement.
  • Limitation: shared confidentiality can deter adolescents from full disclosure; the clinician must balance candour and trust.
  • Challenge: minor–guardian disagreement, custody disputes, and jurisdiction-specific law mean there is no uniform answer — the clinician must reason case by case.

Topic F 7 · Planning & Recording of Therapy Sessions

How a session is planned before it starts and documented after it ends — treatment planning, note formats (SOAP, DAP), documentation principles, recording consent and data handling, plus a worked sample note and a reusable blank template.

7.1 Introduction

Planning and recording are the two bookends of every session. Planning (preparing the aims, structure and interventions before/at the session) keeps therapy coherent and goal-directed; recording (documenting what occurred, clinically and legally) provides continuity of care, accountability, communication with other professionals, and protection for both client and clinician. Documentation is increasingly driven by third-party payors and accrediting agencies demanding fast, auditable records. (Jongsma, p.14; p.19)

7.2 Planning a session — the preparation cycle

  • Initial preparation & review of previous sessions. Before the session, review prior notes, homework and progress against the plan.
  • Case formulation. Hold a working case formulation (a hypothesis linking the client's history, triggers and maintaining factors to their presenting problems) that the session's work tests and refines.
  • Agenda. Set a collaborative agenda for what the session will cover (see Topic 8).
  • Intervention selection. Choose techniques matched to the formulation and stage of therapy.
  • Session closure. Plan time to summarise, assign between-session tasks and gather feedback.

7.3 Jongsma's six-step treatment plan (canonical)

💬 The six steps — Jongsma
  1. Problem Selection — identify the primary problem(s) for treatment.
  2. Problem Definition — specify it behaviourally, tied to DSM-5/ICD criteria.
  3. Goal Development — broad, global, long-term; need not be measurable.
  4. Objective Construction — behavioural, measurable/quantifiable targets.
  5. Intervention Creation — clinician actions matched to each objective.
  6. Diagnosis Determination — compare presentation to DSM-5 criteria (needed for third-party reimbursement).

(Jongsma, p.25; p.26)

Plans must be tailored to the individual — considering strengths, weaknesses, stressors, social network, family circumstances and symptom patterns — not mass-produced. (Jongsma, p.26) Companion tools (Progress Notes Planners, Homework Planners, Client Education Handout Planners) integrate the plan's definitions and interventions with the record. (Jongsma, p.14; p.19)

💬 The "two-file" practice — brief medical note + themes file

The new source describes a widely-recommended split: "My own practice is to make a brief note in the medical record that the session has taken place, and then keep some brief notes of the main themes of the session in a file in my office. The American Psychiatric Association recommends this practice." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.6) The rationale: the therapist keeps a record "to assist … the process of thinking and supervision," but "generally speaking, the therapist does not share her thoughts and feelings about the patient with the patient" — "it is unlikely that the patient will find it helpful to discover what their therapist thinks about them from simply reading the process notes." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.6)

⚖️ Patients' legal access to notes — the therapist does not "own" the notes

A crucial honesty point for exams: "legal jurisdictions, both in the UK and in the US, do … make it theoretically possible for the patient to have access to those notes, unless this access would constitute harm to them or another person." Hence "therapists cannot assume that they own the notes of their meetings." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.6) In CBT specifically, "it is common practice for the therapist to record the patient's experiences in therapy (thoughts, feelings, behaviors) … sharing of these records and notes is often therapeutically helpful." The problem arises when the patient records "information that may be misunderstood out of context," or when the therapist keeps "impressions of the patient … they do not wish the patient to see; and to which the patient may have legal access." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.7)

7.6 Documentation principles

  • Objectivity — record facts and clearly label interpretations as such.
  • Accuracy — faithful to what occurred; correct errors transparently.
  • Relevance — clinically pertinent; avoid gratuitous personal detail.
  • Confidentiality — secure storage and controlled access. (Corsini, p.592)
  • Timeliness — written promptly after the session while memory is fresh.
  • Documenting consent & ethical decisions. If consent is oral, "therapists must make an entry in the client's clinical record documenting the nature and extent of informed consent," and document how the client was included in ethical decisions. (Corey, p.75; p.76)

7.7 Audio / video recording and data handling

⚖️ Recording consent, storage, access, retention, disposal
  • Consent: obtain specific, informed consent to audio/video recording (for supervision, training or research), separate from consent to therapy itself; for minors, guardian consent + child assent.
  • Storage: encrypted/secure storage with restricted access.
  • Access: limited to those with a legitimate need (clinician, supervisor).
  • Retention: keep only as long as clinically/legally required by policy and law.
  • Disposal: secure deletion/destruction at the end of the retention period.

Specific retention periods and data-protection duties (e.g. DPDP Act 2023 in India) are law/policy — verify against the primary statute and your institution's policy.

Topic G 8 · Structuring Therapy Sessions & Setting Goals

The arc of a whole course of therapy and the anatomy of one session — plus how global goals become SMART, measurable targets, and how structure varies across approaches.

8.1 Introduction

Structuring (organising therapy — and each session — so time is used purposefully toward agreed goals) prevents drift, builds the client's sense of progress, and makes outcomes measurable. The degree and type of structure vary sharply between approaches, which is itself a classic exam contrast.

8.2 The arc of therapy — intake to termination

A widely taught sequence (integrating Jongsma's planning and Beck's session model):

  1. Intake — referral, consent, presenting problem.
  2. Assessment — history, measures, risk (see Topic 9).
  3. Formulation — a working hypothesis linking factors to problems.
  4. Treatment planning — goals + measurable objectives + interventions (Jongsma's six steps). (Jongsma, p.25; p.26)
  5. Intervention — the main body of therapy.
  6. Review — monitor progress; revise the plan.
  7. Termination — consolidate gains, relapse prevention, ending.

8.3 Anatomy of a single session (CBT model — Judith Beck)

Beck sets out an explicit structure. The first therapy session: set the agenda → mood check → obtain an update → discuss the diagnosis → problem identification and goal setting → educate the patient about the cognitive model → behavioural activation → end-of-session summary and homework → feedback. (Beck-CBT, p.18 [ToC])

Session 2 and beyond has a repeating structure: first part (mood check, brief update, bridge from last session, review homework, set agenda) → middle part (discuss agenda items, set new homework) → final (summary and feedback). Beck devotes a chapter to "problems with structuring the session" (therapist cognitions about structure, interrupting the patient, strengthening the alliance). (Beck-CBT, p.18 [ToC]; p.19 [ToC])

🧩 The six beats of a structured session
  1. Rapport / mood check — reconnect, brief mood rating.
  2. Review — bridge from last session; review homework.
  3. Set agenda — collaboratively prioritise today's items.
  4. Interventions — work the agenda.
  5. Summarise — capstone summary, check understanding.
  6. Between-session tasks — assign homework; gather feedback.

8.5 SMART goals and collaborative goal-setting

The SMART framework (general professional knowledge) turns a vague aim into a workable objective:

SMARTGoal framework
  • SSpecific — clearly defined, not vague.
  • MMeasurable — progress can be quantified.
  • AAchievable — realistic for this client now.
  • RRelevant — tied to the client's values & the long-term goal.
  • TTime-bound — has a timeframe for review.
Apply: A client says "I want to be happier." Run SMART to co-write: "Walk with a friend twice a week for 4 weeks, then review mood on the PHQ-9" — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound.

Goal-setting is collaborative: the therapist discusses goals, length and methods early and develops them with the client, avoiding over-responsibility for outcomes. (Corey, p.63) Goals are then prioritised, monitored and revised as therapy proceeds. The new source grounds this in ethics: psychotherapy should pursue beneficence and nonmaleficence, yet "there may still be uncertainty or conflict about what the goals of therapy are, and who should set them, which can make the issue of informed consent in psychotherapy particularly complex" — so the clinician should "warn patients that the process of psychotherapy is not always comfortable, and that many patients feel worse before they feel better (although this may not be true for CBT)." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.4)

Topic H 9 · Pre- and Post-Assessment

Measuring where the client starts, tracking change, and judging whether it is real and meaningful — baseline, ongoing monitoring, post-treatment and follow-up, with the methods, psychometric criteria, and the limits of a single test.

9.1 Introduction

Pre- and post-assessment (measuring the client's status before, during and after treatment to establish a baseline and to detect change) turns subjective impressions of "improvement" into something that can be observed, compared and acted on. It feeds treatment planning, detects non-response or deterioration early, and contributes to the evidence base.

9.2 The four time-points

  • Baseline (pre-treatment) — the reference point before therapy begins.
  • Ongoing monitoring (during) — repeated measurement to track trajectory.
  • Post-treatment — status at the end of therapy.
  • Follow-up — later re-assessment to check maintenance of gains.

Model from the manuals. In Motivational Enhancement Therapy the first two sessions "focus on structured feedback from the initial assessment," and in Project MATCH clients were "evaluated at intake and again at 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 months" — a template of baseline plus repeated follow-up on drinking behaviour, psychological/social function and consequences. (MET, p.11) The new source underscores why accurate assessment matters: "successful psychotherapy matches the right therapy and right therapist to the patient, which is why therapists need to develop good quality assessment skills" — including the ability "to formulate problems in different ways." (Notes-Psychotherapies, p.4)

9.3 Methods of assessment

  • Clinical interview — the core method; history, presenting problem, risk.
  • Behavioural observation — directly observing behaviour in session or in vivo.
  • Standardised measures — validated questionnaires/rating scales (e.g. symptom scales). Instrument types such as the MCMI, Rorschach, TAT, MISIC or VSMS may serve as pre/post examples, but their content is not described here because those manuals are image-only scans and were not reviewed.
  • Functional assessment — analysing antecedents, behaviour and consequences to understand what maintains a problem.
  • Client-reported outcomes — the client's own ratings of symptoms, functioning and goal attainment. Routine systems such as CORE collect such data in practice. (Lister-Ford, p.118)

9.5 Selecting measures

Choose instruments by client (age, literacy), language (validated translation), population (norms that fit), purpose (screening vs. outcome vs. diagnosis), and evidence (established reliability/validity for this use). A measure validated for one group may not be valid for another.

9.6 Outcome monitoring — response, non-response, deterioration

  • Response — meaningful improvement on repeated measurement → continue/consolidate.
  • Non-response — no improvement by the expected point → review formulation, dose, fit, alliance; modify treatment.
  • Deterioration — worsening scores → urgent review, risk check, possible change of plan or referral.

Monitoring data also inform the therapist's own development; CORE is used to monitor effectiveness and flag CPD needs. (Lister-Ford, p.118)

9.7 Why a single test does not establish treatment effectiveness

⚠️ The single-test caveat (important)

Improvement on one test from pre to post does not, by itself, prove the therapy caused the change. Rival explanations include: spontaneous remission (natural recovery over time), regression to the mean (extreme baseline scores drifting toward average on re-test), measurement error, practice/placebo effects, and life events. Establishing effectiveness requires controlled comparison, replication and multiple convergent measures — which is why outcome research uses control groups and meta-analysis (Topic 10), not a single pre/post score. Smith & Glass's (1977) meta-analysis of 475 studies found the average treated patient better off than ~80% of untreated controls — a conclusion that depended on aggregated controlled data, not one test. (Lister-Ford, p.62)

  • Post (end of treatment): PHQ-9 = 6 (below clinical threshold), WSAS = 11 — change is both statistically and clinically significant (crossed into the non-clinical range) and shows functional improvement (returned to work part-time).
  • Follow-up (3 & 6 months): PHQ-9 = 7 then 6 — gains maintained, mirroring the MET/Project MATCH repeated-follow-up logic. (MET, p.11)
  • Interpretation caveat: this pattern is consistent with benefit, but on its own cannot rule out other causes; convergent measures + the broader evidence base support (not prove) effectiveness.

9.9 Advantages, limitations & challenges

  • Advantage: systematic measurement catches non-response/deterioration early and demonstrates meaningful change.
  • Limitation: a single measure at two time-points cannot establish causation; measures can lack sensitivity or cultural validity.
  • Challenge: selecting valid, change-sensitive, culturally appropriate measures and interpreting change as clinically (not just statistically) significant.

Topic I/J 10 · Practice of Evidence-Based Therapies

What evidence-based practice actually means — the integration of research, expertise and client values — why that differs from merely using a therapy "labelled" evidence-based, and how to select, implement, monitor and modify treatment responsibly.

10.1 Introduction

The push for evidence-based practice (EBP) reshaped the field: clinicians are expected to justify what they do with research while still tailoring it to the individual. But EBP is frequently misunderstood as "just use a brand-name therapy that has been researched" — a narrowing that the definition below explicitly resists.

10.2 Definition — the three-pillar model (APA 2006 style)

💬 EBP as integration (APA 2006 definition — general knowledge, labelled)

Evidence-based practice in psychology is the integration of (1) the best available research evidence with (2) clinical expertise in the context of (3) patient characteristics, culture, preferences and values. (This three-pillar formulation is the American Psychological Association's 2006 definition, stated here as general professional knowledge — not drawn from a repository page.)

The crucial point: EBP is a process of integration and clinical decision-making, not a product. All three pillars are required; research evidence alone is necessary but not sufficient.

10.3 EBP vs. an "evidence-based" brand therapy (key distinction)

Table 10.1 — Doing EBP vs. merely using an "evidence-based" therapy
AspectEvidence-based practice (the process)Using an "evidence-based" brand therapy
What it isIntegrating research + expertise + client values for this clientDelivering a named protocol because it has research support
Client fitExplicitly weighed (culture, preferences, comorbidity)May be applied generically, regardless of fit
Clinical judgementCentral — selects, adapts, monitors, modifiesMay be minimal if the manual is followed rigidly
Risk—Assuming a therapy "works for everyone," ignoring individual differences
⚠️ Do not claim a therapy "works equally for everyone"

An EST having research support in trials does not mean it works equally for every client, culture or condition. EBP requires judging fit and monitoring response — not assuming universal efficacy.

10.4 Empirically supported treatments (ESTs) & the RCT "gold standard"

The 1995 APA Division 12 Task Force report (Training in and Dissemination of Empirically-Validated Psychological Treatments, Chambless et al.) produced a preliminary list of Empirically Validated Therapies (EVT, later "supported", EST) with hierarchical research-quality criteria; the RCT (randomised controlled trial — participants randomly allocated to treatment vs. a control condition), borrowed from psychopharmacology, became the apparent "gold standard." Hofmann's authors critique this "methodcentric reasoning." (Hofmann-ArtScience, p.53)

10.5 Research quality — RCTs, reviews, meta-analyses, effect sizes

Table 10.2 — Levels of outcome evidence
MethodWhat it doesStrength / limitation
RCTRandomises clients to treatment vs. control (waiting-list/placebo); uses manuals/protocolsStrong internal validity; may have limited generalisability to routine practice
Systematic reviewStructured synthesis of all eligible studiesReduces selective citation; depends on study quality
Meta-analysisStatistically combines many studies into a pooled effect size (a standardised measure of how large an effect is)Smith & Glass (1977): avg treated patient better off than ~80% of untreated; Lambert et al. (1986) ~70% vs ~40% (Lister-Ford, p.62)

RCT in practice (example). The NIMH Treatment of Depression Collaborative Research Program compared CBT vs. IPT vs. two non-psychotherapy conditions. (Lister-Ford, p.64) Efficacy vs. effectiveness. Efficacy studies (RCTs) test treatment under controlled conditions; effectiveness/"practice-based evidence" tests it in routine settings (CORE, Practice Research Networks). "No evidence of effectiveness" is not "evidence of ineffectiveness" (Roth & Fonagy, 1996). (Lister-Ford, p.118)

10.6 Manuals, guidelines, fidelity, adaptation, competence

  • Treatment manuals specify the protocol so it can be delivered consistently and studied.
  • Fidelity (the degree to which a treatment is delivered as intended) is measured by tools such as the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale (CTRS), used to assess therapist competence in research and training. (Beck-CBT, p.16)
  • Adaptation & competence — manuals must be applied with skill and adapted to the client, balancing fidelity with responsiveness.
  • Guidelines controversy. Corsini: proponents want standardisation; critics reject "cookbook" protocols; many "guidelines" are actually expert-consensus reports (Shaneyfelt & Centor, 2009); there is "no general agreement about the definition, methodology, or value of evidence-based psychotherapy." Each chapter examines the evidence base and "the current controversy regarding the importance — and limitations — of evidence-based practice." (Corsini, p.592; p.16)

10.7 Common factors & integration

Wolberg's 506-Questions notes that ~two-thirds of all patients improve irrespective of type of therapy, with little superiority of any single method — raising whether specific techniques are "epiphenomena" secondary to common factors (ingredients shared across therapies, e.g. the alliance, expectation, a credible rationale). (Wolberg-506Q, p.16) Norcross's four routes to integration:

Table 10.3 — Norcross's four routes to integration; Eclecticism vs. Integration
RouteCore idea
Technical eclecticismUse procedures from different sources without adopting their theories (e.g. Lazarus's multimodal therapy; Beutler's STS)
Theoretical integrationBlend two or more theories into a new framework
Common factorsIdentify and use the core ingredients shared across therapies
Assimilative integrationWork from one theory while incorporating techniques from others

Norcross's Table 1.1 contrasts Eclecticism (divergent, choosing, atheoretical-but-empirical, "sum of the parts") with Integration (convergent, combining, more theoretical, "more than the sum of the parts"). (Norcross-Handbook, p.29; p.30)

10.8 The responsible EBP cycle — select → implement → monitor → modify

  1. Select — choose an approach supported by evidence and suited to this client's characteristics, culture and preferences.
  2. Implement — deliver competently, with adequate fidelity, adapting sensibly.
  3. Monitor — use pre/post and ongoing measures (Topic 9) to track response.
  4. Modify — if non-response/deterioration, revise the plan, approach or referral.
🧩 Illustrative decision-making example

Illustrative teaching vignette. A clinician considering an EST for panic weighs the RCT evidence (research), their own skill in delivering it (expertise), and the client's preference to avoid interoceptive exposure plus a cultural factor affecting homework (client values). They begin the protocol, monitor with a panic diary + measure, and when progress stalls, adapt the pacing rather than abandoning or rigidly continuing — EBP as integration, not protocol-worship.

10.9 Advantages, limitations & challenges

  • Advantage: grounds practice in the best evidence while keeping the client central; improves accountability.
  • Limitation: RCT evidence may not generalise to routine, comorbid or culturally diverse clients ("methodcentric" critique). (Hofmann-ArtScience, p.53)
  • Challenge: balancing fidelity with adaptation; the unresolved controversy over guidelines and the role of common factors. (Corsini, p.592; Wolberg-506Q, p.16)

10.10 Mnemonic

PIEREBP pillars + cycle
  • PPatient characteristics, culture, preferences, values (pillar 3).
  • IIntegrate — EBP is integration, not one pillar alone.
  • EEvidence (best available research) + Expertise (clinical skill) (pillars 1 & 2).
  • RReview & revise — monitor response and modify.
Apply: Choosing a treatment, run PIER — the "P" stops you assuming an evidence-based brand "works for everyone" and forces you to weigh this client's culture and preferences.

E.6 Case-based questions with model answers / frameworks (practice)

Case 1 — Minor with disclosed abuse (T6)

Case. A 12-year-old in therapy (parent consented, child assented) discloses physical abuse by a relative.

🧩 Answer framework
  1. Recall the limit: abuse of a minor is a legal exception to confidentiality and may trigger mandatory reporting (Corey p.77; POCSO 2012 — verify against the statute).
  2. Act: follow safeguarding policy; report to the designated authority; prioritise the child's safety.
  3. Alliance: because the confidentiality limit was explained at the outset (part of assent), the child was forewarned; handle the disclosure supportively.
  4. Document: record the disclosure, actions and rationale contemporaneously.
Case 2 — Outcome monitoring and non-response (T9)

Case. A client with depression shows PHQ-9 of 18 → 17 → 18 across six sessions of CBT.

🧩 Answer framework
  1. Label it: this is non-response (no meaningful change by the expected point).
  2. Review: re-check formulation, fidelity/competence (CTRS), alliance, comorbidity, adherence to homework, and external stressors.
  3. Modify: adjust the approach, intensify, add a component, or consider referral/medication review.
  4. Caveat: interpret scores alongside clinical judgement — a single measure is not the whole picture (single-test caveat).
Case 3 — Choosing a treatment (EBP) (T10)

Case. Two therapies have RCT support for the client's condition, but one conflicts with the client's cultural preferences.

🧩 Answer framework (PIER)
  1. Evidence: both have research support — necessary but not sufficient.
  2. Expertise: consider which you can deliver competently.
  3. Patient values: weigh culture/preferences — the three-pillar model requires it; avoid "works for everyone."
  4. Review: select the better-fitting option, monitor response, and modify if needed.

E.7 Common conceptual confusions & how to avoid them

⚠️ Don't mix these up
  • Confidentiality ≠ privilege. Confidentiality is ethical/clinical; privilege is a legal court-protection (and does not cover child/adolescent therapy).
  • Consent ≠ assent. Consent is the guardian's legally valid authorisation; assent is the minor's ethical agreement.
  • Goal ≠ objective. Long-term goals are global and need not be measurable; short-term objectives must be measurable (Jongsma).
  • Statistical ≠ clinical significance. A detectable change may still be trivial in real life.
  • Pre/post change ≠ proof of effectiveness. Rule out spontaneous remission, regression to the mean, measurement error.
  • EBP ≠ a brand therapy. EBP is integration of three pillars, not merely delivering a researched protocol.
  • Efficacy ≠ effectiveness. Efficacy = controlled (RCT) conditions; effectiveness = routine practice.
