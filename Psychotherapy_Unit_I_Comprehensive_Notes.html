1Definitions of Psychotherapy

Topic 1 asks you to say precisely what psychotherapy is, who may do it, how it differs from neighbouring activities, and why definitions differ between schools. Examiners reward answers that (a) quote two or three recognised definitions with authors, (b) extract their common elements, and (c) separate psychotherapy from counselling, assessment and psychiatric treatment.

At a glance
  • Core idea: psychotherapy is the planned, theory-based use of psychological methods, within a deliberate professional relationship, by a trained person, to relieve distress, change behaviour/thinking/emotion and promote growth.
  • Must-know definitions: Wolberg (treatment by psychological means; three aims); Norcross (1990; informed, intentional application of methods from established psychological principles); Korchin, Bruch, Watkins (short classic definitions); Corey (collaborative process in which both persons change).
  • Must-know contrasts: counselling, psychological assessment, psychiatric treatment, education, casework, friendship.
  • Mnemonic: TRAPS (Trained person · Relationship · Approach · Purpose · Shared direction).

1. Introduction: why a definition matters

Wolberg opens his chapter “What Is Psychotherapy?” by observing that few words in the mental-health lexicon are as ambiguous: the term is loosely used to mean helping, treating, advising, guiding, educating and even influencing, and this “diffuseness” has fostered many divergent theories and techniques [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.29]. He still insists that a brief, precise description is needed to mark the boundaries of operation and to permit theory-building and empirical study.

Definitions matter in at least five practical ways:

  1. Professional boundaries (what a clinical psychologist may and may not do, and how this differs from advice-giving or friendship).
  2. Regulation and training (a regulator such as the RCI needs to say who is competent to deliver a defined service).
  3. Research (outcome studies need an agreed independent variable; otherwise “therapy” means anything).
  4. Consent and rights (clients can only consent to something that can be described to them; see Topics 5 and 6).
  5. Payment and accountability (insurers, employers and courts ask “what was the treatment?”).

2. Origin of the word and the “talking cure”

Etymology word origin (where the word comes from): psyche (Greek, soul or being) + therapeutikos (caring for another), so psychotherapy literally means “caring for another person's soul or being” (Kleinke, 1994, cited in [Notes p.1]). Anna O., an early psychoanalytic patient of Breuer, called her treatment “the talking cure” [Notes p.1]. The Notes comment that this is elegant but vague: talking, expressing and sharing pain may be healing in itself, yet that does not satisfy contemporary researchers who need operational definitions.

Historically, psychoanalytic psychotherapy was the exclusive form in the early twentieth century; behavioural therapy emerged as an alternative in the 1950s; humanistic therapy moved to the forefront in the 1960s; and integrative approaches have gathered strength in recent decades [Notes p.3–4]. Today there are said to be more than 400 named approaches (Karasu, 1986, cited in [Notes p.72]) and over 450 schools (Simon, 2001, cited in [Notes p.8]). This proliferation is exactly why a common definition is difficult.

Points of difference between definitions

  • Medical versus psychological model. Wolberg speaks of “treatment” of “problems of an emotional nature”; Norcross speaks of “assisting people” and leaves the goal to the participants. Wolberg even devotes a section to whether the medical model is outmoded [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.32].
  • Who defines success. Wolberg's three aims are therapist-defined; Norcross's “directions participants deem desirable” is participant-defined.
  • Symptoms versus growth. Behavioural/CBT definitions stress specific, measurable change; humanistic definitions stress actualisation and growth [Notes p.4].
  • Role of insight. Psychodynamic definitions treat insight as central; behavioural ones treat it as optional.
  • Informal versus professional. Korchin, Schofield and Watkins are informal; Wolberg and Norcross are professional.

5. The healing frame: four features and four theory families

5.1 Four features of psychotherapy common to all schools

The Notes summarise psychotherapy as including four major factors [Notes p.2]:

  1. A relationship in which the client has confidence that the therapist is competent and cares about his or her welfare.
  2. Expectancy or hope shared by therapist and client that benefit will result.
  3. An underlying conceptual framework giving a rationale and a set of procedures that need the active participation of both.
  4. A practice setting socially defined and accepted as a place of healing.

Additional influences are client factors (nature of the problem, ability to engage, motivation, outside resources such as family support) and therapist factors (competence, personality) [Notes p.2–3]. Gabbard adds that the therapeutic alliance is applicable to all forms of psychotherapy and that, in one large depression study, the alliance accounted for more variance in outcome (21%) than the interventions themselves [Gabbard 2004, PDF p.58].

6.1 Is the counselling–psychotherapy line real?

Authorities disagree. Some writers claim that psychotherapy is a deeper, more involved process for more disturbed clients; others hold that counsellors and psychotherapists do the same work under different titles dictated by employers [Notes p.71]. Wolberg concludes in his legal discussion (US context) that “a sharp distinction between counseling and psychotherapy cannot be drawn” and that no law can prevent people doing psychotherapy under the name of guidance or counselling [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.676]. From the Adlerian viewpoint, Dreikurs argued that psychotherapy is needed if the lifestyle must change, whereas counselling suffices when change can occur within the lifestyle [Sharf 2010, PDF p.159].

The Notes also give a simplified contrast: psychotherapy implies treating or curing problems, whereas counselling refers to advice and suggestions [Notes p.2]. Treat this as an exam shorthand only; many counselling models (e.g., person-centred counselling) are explicitly non-advisory.

6.2 Other neighbours

  • Education / psychoeducation: transmits information and skills to a learner; psychotherapy additionally targets the person's emotional patterns. Wolberg compares educational, casework and counselling approaches and notes they overlap with psychotherapy in aiming at better adjustment but differ in emphasis [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.125].
  • Casework / social work: classically focuses on the external problem or social situation rather than the individual's inner distress [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.130].
  • Friendship: the professional relationship is primarily one-way (the client's concerns are discussed, not the therapist's), bounded in time, fee and intimacy, and a therapist cannot be a friend, colleague or lover to the client at the same time [Notes p.2].
  • Creative-arts, occupational and psychodrama methods: Wolberg regards these “trial action” therapies as psychotherapeutic in effect but not, strictly, psychotherapy [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.30]; later textbooks include arts therapies as psychotherapies, so state your source's position.

7. The Indian legal and professional context

  • Binding law RCI Act 1992, s.13: only a rehabilitation professional who has a recognised qualification and is enrolled on the Register may practise as a rehabilitation professional in India, hold such an office in Government or sign certificates required by law; contravention is punishable (the Act text prescribes imprisonment up to one year, or fine up to ₹1,000, or both). The Act does not define “psychotherapy”; psychological treatment falls within the broader idea of mental healthcare.
  • Binding law MHCA s.2(1)(g): a “clinical psychologist” is a person with a recognised qualification in Clinical Psychology from an institution approved by the RCI, or a postgraduate degree in Psychology/Clinical/Applied Psychology plus a two-year full-time M.Phil. in Clinical Psychology (or Medical and Social Psychology) including supervised clinical training from a UGC-recognised university approved by the RCI.
  • Guideline RCI Professional Diploma document (2011) lists the services expected of diplomate clinical psychologists as interviewing and diagnosing, psychological assessment, psychoeducation, counselling, psychotherapy and behaviour therapy, always applying the code of conduct and ethics. 8. Clinical application: explaining psychotherapy in plain language

Because consent depends on understanding (Topic 6), you must be able to define therapy simply. Good practice is to explain (i) what therapy is, (ii) what you and the client will each do, (iii) what it can and cannot promise, (iv) confidentiality limits, and (v) how progress will be checked.

Illustrative example Therapist–client situation (illustrative)

Client (Meera, 27, panic attacks): “Will you just tell me what to do?”

Therapist: “Therapy is a planned series of conversations and practice exercises. I bring training in methods that have helped many people with panic; you bring knowledge of your own life. Together we will understand what keeps the panic going, set goals you choose, and test new ways of responding. I won't be giving orders, and it won't always feel comfortable, but we will check your progress with simple questionnaires every week and change the plan if it isn't helping. What you tell me stays private, apart from a few safety exceptions I will explain now.”

Analysis: this speech uses the TRAPS elements (training, relationship, approach, purpose, shared direction) and the HERS features (hope, environment, relationship, scheme); it prepares consent (Topic 6) and outcome monitoring (Topic 9).

9. Advantages, limitations and criticisms of definitions

Advantages of a clear definitionLimitations / criticisms

  • Supports consent and rights.
  • Makes research and training possible.

  • Over 400 approaches; no single definition fits all (Notes p.72).
  • Medical-model wording can pathologise ordinary suffering.
  • Definitions that stress “treatment” may exclude growth-oriented work; those that stress “conversation” are too broad.

2Objectives of Psychotherapy

Objectives answer the question “What is therapy for?” A strong answer distinguishes aims (broad purposes), goals (what this client wants) and objectives (measurable steps), lists the major objective types with reasons, and explains why they differ by client, diagnosis, approach and setting.

At a glance
  • Wolberg's three aims: relieve (remove/modify/retard) symptoms · mediate disturbed patterns of behaviour · promote positive personality growth.
  • Kleinke's six common goals: hope · mastery/self-efficacy · overcome avoidance · become aware of misconceptions · accept life's realities · insight.
  • Major objective domains: symptom reduction · functioning · behaviour change · emotion regulation · insight · coping · relationships · relapse prevention. Mnemonic SCRIBE-FM.
  • Principle: goals are negotiated, because patient, therapist and family may want different things.

1. Introduction and terminology

Writers use “aims”, “goals” and “objectives” loosely, but clinical documents (including treatment plans that insurers, institutions or the RCI-style records expect) use them in a stepwise sense. Jongsma and colleagues write long-term goals as broad, global statements of desired positive outcome that need not be measurable, whereas objectives must be stated in behaviourally measurable language so that it is clear when they have been achieved [Jongsma 2014, PDF p.25].

2. Wolberg's three aims in detail

Wolberg's definition (Topic 1) names three objects of therapy. Understanding each, with its limits, lets you write far more than a list.

  1. Removing, modifying or retarding existing symptoms. A prime goal is to eliminate the patient's suffering and the handicaps imposed by symptoms. But complete relief is not always possible: inadequate motivation, limited ego strength ego strength (the positive personality assets that let a person overcome anxieties and acquire new, more adequate defences) and limits of time or money may restrict help to modification rather than cure. In some malignant conditions (Wolberg cites certain fulminating schizophrenic and organic brain disorders) psychotherapy can only retard a deteriorative process, but this palliative effect palliative (easing, not curing) is worthwhile because it may preserve contact with reality [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.31].
  2. Mediating disturbed patterns of behaviour. Many occupational, educational, marital, interpersonal and social problems are emotionally rooted. Recognising that character structure is involved in all emotional illness has broadened the objectives from mere symptom relief to correcting disturbed interpersonal patterns and relationships [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.31].
  3. Promoting positive personality growth and development. Therapy is also a vehicle for maturation, addressing blocks in psychosocial development so that a person can achieve more creative self-fulfilment, more productive attitudes towards life and more gratifying relationships [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.32]. Wolberg summarises that the goals extend “from the limited objective of helping to control symptoms to the liberation of the rich resources of the human mind” from neurotic obstructions.
Exam trap

Do not write “Wolberg's aims are cure, adjustment and growth”. The three verbs are remove/modify/retard, mediate and promote. Also note that the terminology changes by author: Wolberg prefers “patient” (he argues the person receiving treatment is best called a patient) [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.31], while most counselling texts say “client”.

3. Kleinke's six common goals

Kleinke (1994) described six fundamental therapeutic goals common to almost all psychotherapies [Notes p.4–5]:

  1. Overcoming demoralization and gaining hope. People often arrive believing nothing can change. Restoring hope is a goal in itself and a precondition for effort.
  2. Enhancing mastery and self-efficacy self-efficacy (belief in one's ability to carry out the actions needed to reach a goal). Success experiences (graded tasks, skill practice) convert hope into evidence.
  3. Overcoming avoidance. Avoidance maintains anxiety and many disorders; exposure and behavioural experiments target it directly.
  4. Becoming aware of one's misconceptions about self, others or the world (cognitive restructuring, interpretation).
  5. Accepting life's realities. Some losses and limits cannot be undone; acceptance is a therapeutic outcome in acceptance-based and existential work.
  6. Achieving insight insight (an understanding of the origins and development of one's emotional difficulties), leading to more rational control over feelings and actions.

6. Who sets the objectives? Competing goals and ethical issues

The goals of therapy differ for the patient, the significant others (spouse, parents, teacher) and the therapist. A patient may want less anxiety, the therapist greater insight, and the spouse more independence and responsibility from the patient [Notes p.4]. Goal conflict is therefore normal, and managing it is part of treatment.

  • Beneficence and non-maleficence beneficence and non-maleficence (the duties to do good and to do no harm): therapy should help patients with their problems, make them feel better and do them no harm. Most therapists pursue this by increasing the patient's capacity for self-reflection and awareness of links between feelings and actions [Notes p.5].
  • Different time-courses. CBT may make people feel better in the short term by removing symptoms, whereas psychodynamic therapy may not. A patient with trauma may want horrible feelings taken away; psychotherapists do not remove feelings and memories but help patients deal with them better [Notes p.5]. A CBT patient may wish to eliminate all negative emotion while the therapist aims to decrease excessive emotion.
  • Consent link. Because therapists cannot always predict what patients will experience or the outcome, informed consent is complex; Notes advise warning that therapy is not always comfortable and that many patients feel worse before they feel better (though possibly not in CBT) and that some people do not get better [Notes p.5].
  • Ethical rule of thumb. The client's autonomy is respected through negotiation, not imposition (Topics 5, 6, 8). The therapist may still decline goals that are harmful, illegal or beyond competence.

7. How objectives vary

7.1 By client factors

  • Age and development. For children and adolescents, work with families and schools aims to promote and maintain complementary change [Notes p.3]. Objectives are often behavioural and environmental.
  • Severity and chronicity. Severe mental illness may require long-term support, symptom containment and periodic booster sessions, while many patients progress with weekly sessions [Beck 2011, PDF p.78]. Brief therapies are not usually appropriate for chronic and severe illness [Notes p.24].
  • Motivation and readiness. Following Prochaska and DiClemente, brief therapists target changes the client is ready for; if the client is not aware of needed changes, the goal becomes advancing readiness [Notes p.24].
  • Culture and values. Individual choice and autonomy are not universal values; collectivist cultures may prioritise what is good for the group [Corey 2024, PDF p.78]. Goals must be culturally congruent.

7.2 By diagnosis

Jongsma's planner shows how objectives are written differently for each problem; for anxiety, for example, one objective is to “verbalize an understanding of the role that cognitive biases play” in worry, which is directly measurable because it either happens or not [Jongsma 2014, PDF p.24]. Compare: depression (activity, mood, hopelessness), OCD (rituals), substance use (abstinence/harm reduction), psychosis (insight, relapse signature, adherence, social functioning).

9. Limitations and challenges

  • Unrealistic or vague goals ("be happy") cannot be monitored (see SMART goals, Topic 8).
  • Goal conflict among client, family and therapist (Section 6).
  • Not everyone improves. Some people do not get better with psychotherapy and some deteriorate (Lambert & Bergin, 1994, cited in [Notes p.5]); objectives and consent should acknowledge this.
  • Culture-bound objectives. Insight and autonomy may be less valued in some cultures [Corey 2024, PDF p.78].
  • Measurement. What matters to the client may be hard to measure (meaning, acceptance).
  • Changing aims. Objectives should be revised as formulation evolves; RCI's record requirements explicitly ask for changes in objectives and their reasons [RCI Diploma 2011, p.9].

Because psychotherapy works through a relationship and can harm when done badly, who may deliver it and how they are trained are central professional issues. Examiners expect you to cover education and registration, supervised practice, personal qualities, reflective practice and supervision, cultural competence, countertransference and burnout, limits of competence and referral, and the problems of training together with workable solutions.

1. Introduction: why training is an ethical and legal issue

Wolberg warns that dealing adequately with emotional problems “requires a high degree of skill” best acquired through extensive postgraduate training and experience, and that when attempts are made to handle turmoil in depth without skill the relationship “may become disastrous to both participants” [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.30]. Training therefore serves three functions: it protects clients (non-maleficence), protects the profession (public trust) and protects the therapist (from error, burnout and liability). In India, the state backs this with law: only enrolled rehabilitation professionals (including clinical psychologists) may practise as such Binding law [RCI Act 1992, s.13].

    • Training centres need an average monthly turnover of at least 125 patients, at least two registered faculty with RCI-recognised M.Phil. in Clinical Psychology (one full-time), and at least half of cases receiving psychological treatment; face-to-face interaction only counts (tele-counselling is excluded from the count) [RCI Diploma 2011, p.4–5].
    • Intake is capped at 16 per batch because training requires on-site faculty supervision; student–teacher ratios of 1:4 (lecturer level) and 1:6 (associate professor and above) [RCI Diploma 2011, p.6].
    • Minimum clinical work in the year: 50 detailed work-ups, 30 psychodiagnostic cases and 20 therapy/counselling cases (about 200 hours of therapy, with at least 5 children), with a logbook of supervised work, and three fully worked-out psychotherapy records [RCI Diploma 2011, p.8–9].
  • Secondary source The RCI M.Phil. curriculum and exact case numbers were not accessible. Quote them only from the RCI's current document.

2.2 Elsewhere (for comparison)

  • UK. Psychotherapy as recognised by the UKCP (United Kingdom Council for Psychotherapy) is a postgraduate training of at least four years part-time, with many humanistic and integrative trainings lasting six to nine years; the aim is readiness for independent practice of long- and short-term therapies with a varied caseload, with commitments to equal opportunities and intercultural practice [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.114]. Training for public-sector (NHS) work additionally stresses diagnostic systems, psychopharmacology, assessment and formulation, multidisciplinary teamwork and legal–ethical frameworks [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.115].
  • Research trials. In Project MATCH, therapist candidates needed at least a master's degree in a relevant field, at least two years of post-degree clinical experience and an acceptable videotaped session; training was a 3-day seminar followed by at least two supervised training cases (average three), with an average of 26 supervised sessions before certification, and ongoing monitoring against therapist “drift” [MET Manual, Appendix C, PDF p.138–140]. This shows how rigorous skill training looks when quality is the priority.
  • Task-sharing. WHO's brief, manualised programmes (e.g., Problem Management Plus) are designed for delivery by trained and supervised non-specialists in low-resource settings Guideline [WHO PM+ article, PMC4592660]; they extend access but do not replace specialist training.

4. Personal qualities and professional boundaries

Wolberg observes that the therapist's character traits and technical skills are among the most important variables in outcome. “Effective helpers” inspire hope, faith, trust, liking and freedom to respond, and are sincere, honest, respectful and confident; Rogers' conditions of genuineness, empathic understanding and non-possessive warmth are cited, and Truax and Carkhuff's scales of positive regard, accurate empathy and congruence [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.161]. Corey lists characteristics of effective therapists: they have found their own identity, respect and appreciate themselves, are open to change, make life-oriented choices, are authentic and sincere, have a sense of humour, admit mistakes, live in the present, appreciate culture, have a sincere interest in others' welfare, possess good interpersonal skills, find meaning in their work and are passionate. He stresses that no one embodies all of them; “the willingness to struggle to become a more therapeutic person is the crucial variable” [Corey 2024, PDF p.47–49]. The relationship and the person of the therapist matter at least as much as technique in outcome research: both the relationship and methods influence results, and effective therapists use specific methods, offer strong relationships and customise both to the person [Corey 2024, PDF p.47].

Boundaries are the limits that separate the professional role from other roles. Beginning therapists must learn that the therapist cannot also be friend, colleague or lover to the client [Notes p.2], that boundary crossings (e.g., lateness, self-disclosure, accidental contact) differ from boundary violations (e.g., exploitation, sexual contact), and that all sexual or erotic contact between therapist and patient is a violation [Notes p.10]. Details are in Topic 4.

5. Reflective practice, self-awareness, supervision and personal therapy

  • Supervision clinical supervision (regular, structured discussion of one's cases with a more experienced professional to protect clients and develop the supervisee) is, in the psychodynamic literature, essential to reflect on transference and countertransference [Polnay 2023, PDF p.64]. Gabbard points out that the hardest part is creating an environment where trainees feel they can disclose anything that happens in therapy; trainees often feel “narcissistic vulnerability”, may report only the patient's behaviour, and may edit or distort process material to impress. He advises supervisors to say they want to hear about mistakes and uncertainties and not to expect competence at this stage [Gabbard 2004, PDF p.192]. A key supervisory rule: the thing you most wish to avoid sharing is probably the most important to discuss [Gabbard 2004, PDF p.193].
  • Observation and recording aid learning: behind a one-way mirror or on videotape, under a competent supervisor [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.106]. Recording requires client permission (APA Std 4.03; see Topic 7).
  • Personal therapy. The debate is whether to require it. Corey strongly supports some form of self-exploration, noting most mental-health professionals have had personal therapy, and that the benefits include self-awareness, self-care and relationship gains [Corey 2024, PDF p.49]. Wolberg notes that a “frank encounter with oneself, buttressed if necessary by personal therapy” helps because patients may uncritically absorb the therapist's moral precepts [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.82]. Many psychodynamic trainings include personal therapy [Polnay 2023, PDF p.77]. Professional code APA's Code regulates required therapy: where therapy is a programme requirement, students must be allowed to choose a practitioner unaffiliated with the programme, and faculty who evaluate the student's performance must not provide that therapy (Std 7.05); programmes may not require students or supervisees to disclose personal information such as sexual or abuse history, psychological treatment or family relationships unless this was clearly stated in admission materials or is needed to evaluate or help a student whose personal problems may impair training or pose a threat (Std 7.04).
  • Reflective learning cycle. Experience » reflection » supervisory feedback » re-formulation » refined practice (Figure 3.3).
  • Practice-based learning. A US survey reported in Lister-Ford found that even among therapists with PhDs, the most useful sources were ongoing client experience, supervision and being a client themselves, more than research articles [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.78]; this supports supervision-centred training but does not replace evidence (Topic 10).

6. Cultural competence

Competent multicultural practice involves cultural awareness, cultural knowledge and cultural skills (Sue et al., 2022, in [Corey 2024, PDF p.56]). Meta-analytic evidence indicates clients are more likely to engage, stay and benefit when treatment is tailored to their characteristics, so therapists should seek client feedback about cultural fit [Corey 2024, PDF p.56]. Cultural humility cultural humility (an ongoing stance of learning from the client about their culture rather than claiming expertise) and tracking satisfaction with cultural responsiveness improve engagement, retention and outcomes [Corey 2024, PDF p.59]. Therapists neglecting culture risk practising unethically; values such as individual autonomy are not universal [Corey 2024, PDF p.78]. Professional code APA Standard 2.01(b) requires training, consultation, supervision or referral when age, gender, culture, religion, language, disability or socio-economic status are essential to effective service. In India this means language, caste, religion, family structure, gender norms and help-seeking beliefs must be part of training.

7. Countertransference, impairment and burnout

  • Countertransference countertransference (the therapist's emotional reactions to the client, shaped partly by the therapist's own history). Corey defines it broadly as any projection that influences how we perceive and react to a client; when unaware we may be overwhelmed or use the hour for our own purposes; personal therapy can help us recognise and manage it [Corey 2024, PDF p.62–63]. Supervision helps separate what derives from the patient from what derives from the therapist [Polnay 2023, PDF p.64].
  • Impairment impairment (a decline in professional functioning due to personal distress, illness, substance use or burnout). You are not competent if experiencing burnout or psychological distress; it is an ethical duty to watch for impairment and act [Murdock 2017, PDF p.40]. Professional code APA Std 2.06: do not begin an activity when personal problems are substantially likely to prevent competent work; if problems arise, obtain consultation or assistance and decide whether to limit, suspend or terminate duties.
  • Burnout. Corey stresses that therapists have considerable control over burnout, that self-care is an ethical mandate, that the helping professions involve much “one-way caring” that needs to be balanced personally, and that a wellness perspective (exercise, nutrition, sleep, relationships, meaning) is essential [Corey 2024, PDF p.64–65]. Taking excessive responsibility for clients' decisions increases burnout risk [Corey 2024, PDF p.63].

8. Competence limits, referral and multidisciplinary collaboration

  • Professional code APA 2.01(a): provide services only within the boundaries of competence based on education, training, supervised experience, consultation, study or professional experience. 2.03: undertake ongoing efforts to maintain competence.
  • Murdock adds that attending one workshop does not necessarily make you competent in a technique, that you should ask whether it is evidence-based and whether presenters are qualified, and that competence includes knowing the areas where you are not competent [Murdock 2017, PDF p.40].
  • Secondary source RCI conduct regulations (as summarised by Nyaaya) forbid practising in a field other than one's specialisation (Regulation 11(k)) [Nyaaya 2022].
  • When to refer: client needs outside your training (e.g., acute psychosis needing medication, severe risk); a conflict that impairs objectivity (dual relationship); client not benefiting; need for a specialist assessment; your own impairment.
  • Collaboration. Working in a multidisciplinary team with psychiatrists, social workers, nurses, school staff and family is part of training [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.115]. Professional code APA 10.04 advises care when offering therapy to someone already receiving services elsewhere: discuss with the client and consult other providers when appropriate.

4Ethical and Legal Issues Involved in Therapy Work

Ethics asks “What should I do?”; law asks “What must I do or avoid?”. They overlap but are not identical. This topic is the heart of professional practice: consent, confidentiality and its limits, competence, boundaries, records and technology, risk and safeguarding, and a systematic way of resolving dilemmas, all anchored in the Indian legal framework plus the RCI, IACP, APA and other bodies.

At a glance
  • Ethical principles: autonomy · beneficence · non-maleficence · justice (+ fidelity, integrity, respect). APA's five General Principles A–E; BPS's four: respect, competence, responsibility, integrity.
  • Binding Indian law: RCI Act 1992 (s.13 practice; s.21 conduct/ethics), MHCA 2017 (capacity, consent, confidentiality, records), POCSO 2012 (mandatory reporting), DPDP Act 2023 (data, children).
  • Confidentiality is a duty with limits. MHCA s.23(2) lists the exceptions; mnemonic NOCAPS.
  • Decisions: Corey's eight steps; mnemonic ISCLCBED. Document each step.
  • Conflict rule: APA 1.02 says to clarify the conflict between ethics and law, state commitment to the Code, try to resolve it, and (if unresolvable) follow the law, but never to violate human rights.

1. Introduction: ethics, law and professional codes

The Notes define the ethical discourse as one of “ought and should” in interpersonal relationships: in psychotherapy, how therapists should behave with patients and colleagues, and what they ought to do in difficult situations. There is a close relationship between ethics and psychotherapy because ethical debate is about how we treat others, and psychotherapy explores exactly how patients relate to others; every school uses the therapeutic relationship to address the patient's problems [Notes p.9]. Whatever the school, ethical dilemmas must be resolved one way or another, and the overarching duty is to make the best-quality decision possible [Notes p.9].

4. Informed consent

Corey calls informed consent “an ethical and legal requirement” that is integral to therapy, establishes the basis of the working alliance and gives clients the right to be informed and to make autonomous decisions. It is “something far broader than simply making sure clients sign the appropriate forms” [Corey 2024, PDF p.75].

  • Content (Corey): general goals; responsibilities of counsellor and client; limits and exceptions to confidentiality; legal and ethical parameters; qualifications of the practitioner; fees; services expected; approximate length; benefits and risks; and the possibility that the case will be discussed with colleagues or supervisors [Corey 2024, PDF p.75]. Written, oral or both; if oral, document it in the record [Corey 2024, PDF p.76].
  • Professional code APA 3.10: obtain informed consent using language reasonably understandable to the person; for persons legally incapable of consent, provide an explanation, seek assent, consider preferences and best interests and obtain permission from a legally authorised person; if services are court-ordered, tell the person; document consent, permission and assent. APA 10.01: inform clients as early as feasible about the nature and anticipated course of therapy, fees, involvement of third parties and limits of confidentiality and allow questions; for developing treatments, inform them of the developing nature, risks, alternatives and voluntariness; if a trainee, tell the client and give the supervisor's name.
  • Binding law MHCA 2017: “informed consent” means consent given for a specific intervention without force, undue influence, fraud, threat, mistake or misrepresentation, after disclosure of adequate information including risks, benefits and alternatives in a language and manner the person understands (s.2(1)). Capacity exists if the person can understand relevant information, appreciate reasonably foreseeable consequences, and communicate the decision; information must be given in simple language or other aids; an unwise decision does not itself show lack of capacity (s.4).
  • Difficulties specific to psychotherapy [Notes p.5–9]: (i) therapists may not predict what patients will experience or what the outcome will be; (ii) the nature of risk is unusual: untoward transference reactions, mismanaged transference/countertransference, regressive dependency and general worsening are real risks, yet few therapists warn of them; (iii) fluctuating capacity, e.g., after trauma or bereavement or in psychosis; (iv) children who agree under family pressure; (v) voluntariness in family, couple and group therapy and in forensic settings; (vi) failure to obtain consent can amount to negligence in jurisdictions with consent statutes.

5. Confidentiality, privilege and their limits

5.1 Concepts

Confidentiality confidentiality (the therapist's duty not to disclose what the client reveals) is an ethical concept and usually a legal duty. Privileged communication privilege (a legal rule protecting confidential communications from being revealed in court without the client's permission) is a legal concept; both rest on the right to privacy [Corey 2024, PDF p.76]. Corey adds that privilege generally does not apply to group, couple, family or child/adolescent therapy, or wherever more than two people are in the room (US framework). The Notes argue that confidentiality is better understood as informed consent to disclosure: the therapist must obtain the patient's consent before disclosing details to anyone, including those close to the patient [Notes p.6]. The duty arises from ethical codes, licensing regulation and statute, common law and (in the US) constitutional privacy [Notes p.8].

5.2 Limits of confidentiality

Binding law MHCA 2017 s.23 gives a person with mental illness the right to confidentiality in respect of his mental health, mental healthcare, treatment and physical healthcare, and imposes a duty on all health professionals to keep information confidential, with these exceptions:

  1. release to the nominated representative to enable him to fulfil his duties;
  2. release to other mental health professionals and health professionals to enable them to provide care and treatment;
  3. release if necessary to protect any other person from harm or violence;
  4. only such information as is necessary to protect against the harm identified shall be released;
  5. release only such information as is necessary to prevent a threat to life;
  6. release on an order of the Board, Central Authority, High Court, Supreme Court or other competent statutory authority;
  7. release in the interests of public safety and security.

s.24: no photograph or other information about a person undergoing treatment at a mental health establishment may be released to the media without that person's consent, and the right to confidentiality applies to information stored in electronic or digital form. s.25: persons with mental illness have a right to access their basic medical records; the mental health professional may withhold specific information if disclosure would cause serious mental harm to the person or likelihood of harm to others, and must tell the person of the right to apply to the Board for release.

  2. Assess risk: intent, plan, means, history of violence, alcohol, access.
  3. Consult supervisor immediately; check relevant law (MHCA s.23(2)(c),(d)).
  4. Options: intensive de-escalation and safety planning with Sandeep; request voluntary hospital evaluation; notify potential victim and/or police only as necessary.
  5. Act and document: what was disclosed, to whom, why, the minimum information shared, and the follow-up.
  6. Tell the client where it does not increase danger, and revisit the consent discussion.

Ethical balance: autonomy and privacy (his) versus non-maleficence and protection of life (hers). The law explicitly permits releasing only the information necessary to protect against the harm identified.

6. Competence and integrity

Professional code APA 2.01: practise within the boundaries of competence based on education, training, supervised experience, consultation, study or professional experience; obtain training, consultation, supervision or referral when diversity factors are essential. 2.03: maintain competence. 2.04: base work on established scientific and professional knowledge. 2.06: personal problems and conflicts (Topic 3). Murdock stresses that a workshop alone does not equal competence and that being burnt out or impaired means you are not competent [Murdock 2017, PDF p.40]. Secondary source RCI Reg. 11(k): practise only in your field of specialisation. Integrity: APA Principle C and Standard 5.01 (heading: avoidance of false or deceptive statements); RCI regulations restrict advertising (Reg. 3) and require truthful display of qualifications (Reg. 8) Secondary source.

Illustrative example Dual-relationship vignette

In a town with one school, a school counsellor's own child studies in the class of a student she counsels. Analysis: multiple relationship risk (counsellor + parent of classmate). 8. Records, privacy, digital communication and telepsychology

  • Professional code APA 6.01–6.02: create, maintain, store, retain and dispose of records to facilitate later services, meet institutional requirements, ensure billing accuracy and comply with law; maintain confidentiality in creating, storing, accessing, transferring and disposing of records in any medium; remove personal identifiers when data enter shared databases; plan in advance for protecting records if you withdraw from practice. APA 4.03: obtain permission from all persons (or legal representatives) before recording voices or images.
  • Guideline APA Record Keeping Guidelines (2007): in the absence of a superseding requirement, psychologists may consider retaining full records until 7 years after the last service for adults, or 3 years after a minor reaches majority, whichever is later; protect security of paper and electronic records. Details in Topic 7.
  • Binding law MHCA s.24(2): confidentiality extends to information stored in electronic or digital format “in real or virtual space”.
  • Technology and privacy. Corey advises discussing technology risks during consent, being careful with emails or texts to a client's workplace or home, and limiting exchanges to basic information such as appointment times [Corey 2024, PDF p.75–76].
  • Telepsychology. Pope and Wedding's scenario shows the legal puzzles when therapist and client are in different jurisdictions: licensing, authority of boards, standards of competence for online therapy, which state's confidentiality and reporting laws apply, emergency plans, and billing transparency [Corsini 2011, PDF p.594–595]. Guideline APA's Guidelines for the Practice of Telepsychology (2013) cover eight areas: competence with the technology; standards of care; informed consent; confidentiality of data; security and transmission of data; disposal of data and technologies; testing and assessment; and interjurisdictional practice. For India: apply RCI/MHCA obligations, secure platforms, verify identity and location, plan for emergencies, and see DPDP duties Secondary source.

9. Risk assessment, safeguarding and protective action

  • Risk assessment risk assessment (a structured estimate of danger to self or others, including intent, plan, means, history and protective factors) is a core duty. CBT therapists review items on hopelessness and suicidality on measures such as the BDI-II and, if elevated, conduct a risk assessment and develop a safety plan before proceeding [Beck 2011, PDF p.85]. Structured tools such as the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (in the repository) support this.
  • Binding law MHCA s.115(1): a person who attempts suicide is presumed, unless proved otherwise, to have severe stress and shall not be tried and punished under the Indian Penal Code provision cited in the Act. Therapists should therefore treat suicidality as a clinical, not criminal, matter.
  • Children: POCSO s.19 and s.21 (above). Corey reports that there is a legal requirement to break confidentiality in cases of child abuse, abuse of the elderly or dependent adults, and danger to self or others, and that practitioners must know these duties [Corey 2024, PDF p.77]; Murdock adds that in the United States every state mandates reporting of child abuse [Murdock 2017, PDF p.39] (a US comparison; Indian duties are in POCSO). See Topic 6 for adolescents.
  • Principles of protective action: assess; consult; try less intrusive options first (safety plan, voluntary admission, family involvement with consent); disclose the minimum necessary; document reasons; follow up.

10. Law and the therapist: duty of care and standard of care

Psychotherapeutic relationships are not outside civil or criminal law. Statutes and case law that apply to medical treatments also apply to therapy: therapists acquire a legal duty of care and must practise in accordance with a reasonable body of (psychotherapeutic) opinion; exercise reasonable skill; make logical treatment decisions; conform to ethical guidance where it exists; gain informed consent; and tell the truth when asked [Notes p.11]. Negligence claims against psychotherapists have increased [Notes p.8]. Distinguish: civil liability (compensation), criminal liability (e.g., POCSO non-reporting), and professional discipline (RCI removal from the Register).

When ethics and law seem to conflict

Professional code APA 1.02: clarify the nature of the conflict, make known your commitment to the Ethics Code, and take reasonable steps to resolve it consistent with the Code's principles; if it is unresolvable, you may adhere to the law, but “under no circumstances” may this be used to justify violating human rights. Example: a statutory reporting duty (POCSO) overrides a client's wish for secrecy, and the therapist should inform the client of this limit at the outset and handle the report supportively.

5Rights and Responsibilities in Psychotherapy

A therapy relationship is a two-way arrangement with unequal power. Rights protect the weaker party (the client); responsibilities ensure the process works and protect the therapist. A complete answer presents client rights, client responsibilities, therapist rights and therapist responsibilities in a comparison table, and then explains how to handle attendance, fees, communication, termination, complaints, disagreements, treatment refusal and requests for records.

At a glance
  • Client rights (PRIDE): Privacy & confidentiality · Respect & non-discrimination · Information · Decide (consent, refuse, withdraw) · Exit & Escalate (end therapy, complain).
  • Client responsibilities (HAFT): Honesty · Attendance · Fees · Taking part (collaboration, homework, safety plan).
  • Therapist responsibilities (3C + B + 3R): Competence · Consent · Confidentiality · Boundaries · Records · Referral/continuity · Respect.
  • Law: MHCA 2017 Chapter V (ss.18–28) is the key statute of rights for persons with mental illness; APA 3.10, 4.02, 6.03–6.04, 10.01, 10.10 give the professional detail.

1. Introduction: rights, duties and the therapeutic contract

A right is an entitlement that others must respect; a duty is an obligation owed to someone; a responsibility is a role-related expectation that may be ethical, contractual or legal. In therapy, the client's rights usually create matching duties for the therapist (Figure 5.1). Client responsibilities are mostly collaborative expectations agreed in the consent process, not legal obligations: failing to attend or pay does not entitle a therapist to breach confidentiality or abandon an unsafe client.

Corey notes that by educating clients about their rights and responsibilities the therapist both empowers them and builds trust, and that this education begins in the first session and continues throughout [Corey 2024, PDF p.75]. Murdock lists what clients should be told: the approach, the rights and responsibilities of each party, potential risks, expected benefits, the counsellor's qualifications, fees and billing, and the limits of confidentiality [Murdock 2017, PDF p.39].

The Indian statutory statement of rights is the Mental Healthcare Act 2017, whose long title commits it to provide mental healthcare and to protect, promote and fulfil the rights of persons with mental illness during delivery of care. It was drafted to harmonise Indian law with the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (preamble). Where a client is not a “person with mental illness” as defined, the same principles still apply as ethical standards (APA, RCI conduct rules, IACP code).

4. Therapist rights

  • To practise within competence and decline cases beyond it (APA 2.01; Secondary source RCI Reg. 11(k)). Not every request must be accepted; for example, a therapist is not obliged to write a diagnostic letter simply because a client asks, and may decide on clinical grounds (view expressed in Nyaaya's discussion; check local rules) Secondary source.
  • To fair remuneration as agreed, and to use collection procedures after giving notice (APA 6.04(e)), within law; RCI prohibits exorbitant fees Secondary source.
  • To safety and respect. A psychologist may terminate therapy when threatened or otherwise endangered by the client or by someone connected with the client (APA 10.10(b)).
  • To consult supervisors and colleagues (APA 4.06) and to receive supervision.
  • To set professional policies (cancellation, availability, communication channels, social media), provided they are disclosed in advance and are lawful.
  • To fair process. Before removing a name from the Register the RCI must give the person a reasonable opportunity of being heard (RCI Act s.21(3)) Binding law.
  • To withhold specific record information if disclosure would cause serious mental harm to the person or likely harm to others (MHCA s.25(2)), while informing the person of the right to apply to the Board Binding law.
  • Limits: the therapist cannot assume to “own” the notes absolutely: patients may have legal access to records in some jurisdictions unless access would harm them or others [Notes p.7].

7. Handling difficult situations

7.1 Complaints

  1. Invite the client to voice the concern to you; listen without defensiveness; clarify, apologise where appropriate, and agree remedies.
  2. Offer the institution's grievance procedure or supervisor.
  3. Inform about external options: RCI (professional misconduct; Register); State/Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities where the client has a disability; and, for care in a mental health establishment, the MHCA s.28 ladder (professional in charge, then the Board, then the State Authority). Judicial remedies remain open [MHCA 2017 s.28(2)]. Routes 2–3 are described by Nyaaya Secondary source.
  4. Never retaliate, threaten or breach confidentiality to defend yourself except as law permits; consult your supervisor or legal adviser; document.

7.2 Disagreements about the plan

Revisit goals collaboratively (Topic 8). If the disagreement persists, consider a second opinion, a different approach or referral. Therapists should be honest that outcomes are uncertain and that people may feel worse before better [Notes p.5].

7.3 Treatment refusal

Competent adults may refuse. Binding law MHCA s.4(3): a decision perceived as inappropriate or wrong does not by itself mean lack of capacity. Steps: check capacity (understand, appreciate, communicate); explore reasons (fear, cost, stigma, family pressure); provide information in simple language; offer alternatives (lower intensity, different approach, family sessions); document informed refusal; maintain an open door; if risk is high, follow the risk pathway (Topic 4). For minors, see Topic 6.

7.4 Requests for records

  1. Confirm identity and authority (client, nominated representative, court order, other professional with consent).
  2. For persons with mental illness, MHCA s.25(1) grants access to basic medical records; specific information may be withheld if serious mental harm or harm to others is likely (s.25(2)); inform the person of the right to apply to the Board (s.25(3)).
  3. Separate your clinical record from personal process notes (Topic 7); do not assume process notes are beyond reach [Notes p.7].
  4. Do not withhold records needed for emergency treatment solely because of unpaid fees (APA 6.03).
  5. Document the request, decision, date and what was released.

7.5 Missed sessions, non-payment and communication

Discuss early: APA 6.04(d) requires discussion as early as feasible of limits to services because of financing. If a client frequently misses sessions, treat it clinically first (ambivalence, avoidance, practical barriers) before applying penalties. State response times and emergency routes in writing.

7.6 Termination

APA 10.10: terminate when it is reasonably clear that the client no longer needs the service, is not likely to benefit, or is being harmed; may terminate if threatened; before termination provide pretermination counselling and suggest alternative providers as appropriate (except where precluded by client or payor actions). Notes record that about half of terminations appear to be unilateral, initiated by the client [Notes p.24]. Wolberg's Q&A explains why patients resist termination: failure to resolve a positive transference, fear of giving up the comfortable niche of treatment, separation anxiety, and sometimes the therapist's own difficulty in letting go [Wolberg 506 Q&A, PDF p.31]. CBT plans termination from the start, spacing sessions, building relapse-prevention skills and offering booster sessions [Beck 2011, PDF p.78].

6Issues Related to Consent (and Assent in the Case of Minors)

Adults give informed consent. Children and adolescents usually cannot give legally valid consent, so a parent or legal guardian consents while the minor gives assent assent (the child's own agreement to take part, after an age-appropriate explanation). A good answer explains both, the minor's developing capacity, confidentiality and parental involvement, risk disclosures, disagreements, and the Indian legal position, without pretending there is one rule for every minor.

At a glance
  • Consent = legally effective permission (capacity + voluntariness + information + documentation: CVID). Assent = the minor's agreement, sought as an ethical duty, not a legal substitute for consent.
  • APA Std 3.10(b) for persons legally incapable of consent: Explain · seek Assent · consider Preferences/best interests · obtain Permission from a legally authorised person (mnemonic EAPP).
  • Indian law: MHCA “minor” = under 18; legal guardian is the nominated representative (s.15) unless the Board orders otherwise; POCSO s.19 reporting; DPDP s.9 parental consent for children's data Secondary source.
  • No single rule: what is required depends on the applicable law, the circumstances and the type of service (therapy, assessment, research, school counselling, emergency).

1. Introduction

The Notes remind us that the ethical principle behind consent is respect for autonomy, and that legally valid consent must be voluntary, given by a competent person, and adequately informed [Notes p.5]. Children are a group who may lack competence to make treatment decisions, and the problem is rarely global incompetence but fluctuating capacity: the Notes ask how to interpret a child's refusal of therapy that was accepted a day earlier, as an informed choice or as evidence that the work has begun; they also warn of children who “agree” only under family pressure [Notes p.6]. Consent in family, couple and group work is harder still because members may agree only because of pressure from their emotional network.

3. Legal and professional framework

  • Professional code APA Standard 3.10(b): for persons who are legally incapable of giving informed consent, psychologists nevertheless (1) provide an appropriate explanation, (2) seek the individual's assent, (3) consider such persons' preferences and best interests, and (4) obtain appropriate permission from a legally authorised person if substitute consent is permitted or required by law; if not, they take reasonable steps to protect the person's rights and welfare. 3.10(d): document consent, permission and assent. 4.02(a): discuss limits of confidentiality with persons legally incapable of consent and their representatives to the extent feasible.
  • Binding law MHCA 2017: a “minor” is a person who has not completed 18 years (s.2(1)). The legal guardian is the minor's nominated representative unless the Board, on application by a mental health professional or other person acting in the minor's best interest, finds the guardian is not acting in the minor's best interest or is unfit, and appoints another suitable person (s.15). A legal guardian may make an advance directive for a minor until majority (s.5(4)). Admission of a minor to a mental health establishment follows s.87: the nominated representative applies; two professionals independently examine the minor; admission must be in the minor's best interests, taking the minor's wishes into account if ascertainable, with community alternatives having failed. s.4 capacity (understand, appreciate, communicate) is framed for any person, with information given in simple language or other aids.
  • Binding law POCSO 2012: “child” = a person below 18 (s.2(d)); any person, including the child, who has knowledge or apprehension of a sexual offence shall report to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or local police (s.19(1)); reports from children are recorded in simple language, with an interpreter if needed (s.19(3),(4)); good-faith reporters are protected (s.19(7)); failure to report is an offence (s.21), but s.21 does not apply to a child (s.21(3)).
  • Binding law Secondary source DPDP Act 2023, s.9: a data fiduciary must obtain verifiable consent of the parent or lawful guardian before processing personal data of a child (under 18), with exemptions in the Rules for certain healthcare uses. Commencement is phased; check the current position when handling digital records, tele-sessions or apps with minors.
  • Guideline ICMR 2017 (research): children are individuals who have not attained the legal age of consent (up to 18); parental/LAR consent is required; in addition verbal/oral or written assent, as approved by the ethics committee, should be obtained from children aged 7–18; if the child objects, the wish must be respected; mere failure to object is not assent (§6.5.3–6.5.4). This is a research rule and should not be transferred automatically to routine therapy.
  • Secondary source RCI: no separate consent rule for minors was found in the sources accessed; RCI's diploma document requires at least 5 children among the 20 supervised therapy cases, which makes this a core skill [RCI Diploma 2011, p.9].
There is no single universal rule

Whether a 16-year-old can consent alone to counselling, whether school counselling needs written parental consent, and whether a clinician may see a minor in an emergency without a parent depend on the statute, the setting, institutional policy and urgency. The sources I could verify do not set a specific age at which a minor can consent alone to outpatient psychological therapy in India. In practice, obtain guardian consent as the legal default, seek the minor's assent, and ask your institution or lawyer when unsure. Never claim a rule you cannot cite.

4. Developmental capacity: how much should a minor decide?

Capacity is not all-or-nothing and grows with age and experience. It involves four abilities (reflected in MHCA s.4): understanding relevant information; appreciating consequences; reasoning about options; and expressing a choice. ICMR notes that at younger ages children are vulnerable because they cannot fully understand details, and at older ages they may understand but still lack legal capacity to consent [ICMR 2017, PDF p.76]. Hence the ethical aim is progressive involvement: the child participates as far as development allows.

6. Confidentiality, parental involvement and safeguarding

  • Agree the confidentiality contract with both the minor and the parent at the start: what is private; what parents will be told (e.g., attendance, progress in general terms, safety concerns); and what overrides privacy. Corey stresses that it is too late to tell a minor you will consult parents after they have disclosed a highly personal matter; young clients have a right to know the limits of confidentiality before they disclose [Corey 2024, PDF p.75].
  • Privilege usually does not apply in child and adolescent therapy in the US framework Corey describes [Corey 2024, PDF p.76]; Indian courts and institutions should be consulted for local rules.
  • Parents as partners: work with families and schools to promote and maintain change [Notes p.3]; recruit parents as collaborators [Murdock 2017, PDF p.39]. Still keep the child's content private unless safety requires disclosure.
  • Risk disclosures: suicide risk, self-harm, abuse, neglect, trafficking, substance use, pregnancy and relationship violence may require action. Sexual offences against a child trigger the POCSO s.19 duty regardless of the child's wishes. MHCA s.23(2)(c),(e) allows release of the minimum information needed to protect from harm or prevent a threat to life.
  • Who the client is: in child work the child is the client even when the parent pays; clarify roles (APA 3.07 third-party requests; 10.02 on therapy with several related persons, heading) and avoid secret alliances.
  • Suspected abuse by a parent: do not confront the suspected parent; follow the reporting pathway and child-protection agencies, and ensure safety first.

7Planning and Recording of Therapy Sessions

Good therapy is planned and good notes are the memory of that plan. This topic covers preparing for a session, selecting interventions, closing the session, and the documentation system: clinical notes, progress notes, process notes, SOAP/DAP formats, recording of audio/video, storage, access, retention and disposal. It ends with a realistic sample note and a reusable blank template.

At a glance
  • Before: review previous note and plan, check measures and risk, choose intervention, prepare practicalities (PREP).
  • During: agenda, work on priority problems, jot key facts. After: write a timely, objective note; update formulation and plan; take it to supervision.
  • Notes: SOAP (Subjective · Objective · Assessment · Plan) or DAP (Data · Assessment · Plan). Qualities: CARTO (Confidential, Accurate, Relevant, Timely, Objective).
  • Kinds of records: clinical record (shared, accountable) · personal process notes (therapist's thinking) · research records (separate, consented).
  • Law and ethics: APA 6.01–6.02, 4.03; MHCA ss.24–25; RCI records and register Secondary source; RCI diploma psychotherapy record elements.

1. Introduction: why plan and why record?

APA's Record Keeping Guidelines explain that records benefit both client and psychologist through documentation of treatment plans, services provided and client progress; they allow the psychologist to monitor their own work, are especially important when there are significant gaps between contacts or when the client moves to another professional, and help protect both parties in legal or ethical proceedings Guideline [APA Record Keeping Guidelines 2007]. The APA Ethics Code states the purposes: facilitate later services, meet institutional requirements, ensure accurate billing and comply with law (Std 6.01) Professional code. Beck adds a clinical reason: even experienced therapists cannot remember all the important items without written notes; notes also help refine the conceptualisation, track what is covered and plan future sessions [Beck 2011, PDF p.143].

2. Planning therapy: from the whole course to a single session

2.1 Course-level plan (treatment plan)

Jongsma and colleagues describe six steps: (1) problem selection (choose the most significant problems; set others aside); (2) problem definition (behaviourally specific, linked to diagnostic criteria); (3) goal development (broad, long-term); (4) objective construction (behaviourally measurable); (5) intervention creation (at least one per objective; add new ones if the objective is not met); (6) diagnosis determination (using DSM-5/ICD criteria). The plan must be individualised, not mass-produced [Jongsma 2014, PDF p.24–26]. Beck's chapter 19 treats planning treatment across sessions and planning individual sessions [Beck 2011, PDF p.20].

2.2 Session-level plan

  1. Review the previous note and plan: agenda items left over, homework assigned, risk status.
  2. Check measures and risk: review scores before the session; if hopelessness or suicidality items are raised, plan a risk assessment [Beck 2011, PDF p.85].
  3. Update the formulation case formulation (a working explanation of how the client's problems developed and what maintains them) and decide the session's purpose (assessment, skill teaching, exposure, review).
  4. Select the intervention matched to formulation, objectives and evidence (Topic 10); prepare materials (worksheets, scales).
  5. Prepare the agenda skeleton but remain collaborative: the client's agenda items come first.
  6. Practicalities: room privacy, time, consent for recording, interpreter, digital platform security.
  7. Plan the close: summary, homework, feedback, next appointment.

  • Notes p.7: the author keeps a brief note in the medical record that the session took place and some brief notes of the main themes in a separate file in the office; the American Psychiatric Association recommends this practice and it is consistent with Royal College of Psychiatrists advice.   • Research records are collected for a study under a protocol; they need ethics-committee approval and research consent (APA 8.02–8.03; ICMR 2017) and should be stored separately from clinical files; do not use clinical notes as research data without permission.

7. Recording audio or video of sessions

  1. Permission first. Professional code APA 4.03: obtain permission from all persons (or legal representatives) before recording voices or images. For minors, obtain guardian consent and the child's assent.
  2. Explain purposes (supervision, training, accuracy, research), who will see or hear it, storage and deletion; the client may refuse or stop at any time without penalty to care.
  3. Secure storage: encrypted devices, restricted access, no personal phones/cloud without safeguards; de-identified file names.
  4. Limit use to the stated purpose; destroy after supervision or per policy.
  5. Benefits: supervision, adherence feedback (e.g., rating on the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale; Beck PDF p.369 suggests listening to sessions and rating them), and accuracy. Wolberg notes videotape helps supervision [Wolberg 2013, PDF p.106]. Risks: loss of privacy, inhibition, data breach.

8Structuring Therapy Sessions and Setting Goals

Structure gives therapy direction and safety; goals give it purpose and a way of knowing when it has worked. This topic covers the whole course (intake » assessment » formulation » plan » intervention » review » termination), the structure of a single session, short- and long-term goals, SMART and other frameworks, collaborative goal setting, monitoring and revising goals, and how structure varies across approaches and contexts.

At a glance
  • Process phases: Intake · Assessment · Formulation · Plan · Intervention · Review · Termination (IAF-PIRT).
  • Session (CBT): beginning (mood check, agenda, update, homework review, prioritise) · middle (work on problem, homework) · end (summary, new homework, feedback). Mnemonic: My Aunt Usually Helps Prepare Wonderful Sweet Hot Food.
  • Goals: long-term goals are broad; objectives are measurable (Jongsma). SMART = Specific · Measurable · Achievable · Relevant · Time-bound.
  • Principle: structure is explained to the patient, because it “demystifies” therapy and keeps it on track (Beck).

1. Introduction: why structure?

Beck writes that most patients feel comfortable when the therapist explains how and why sessions are structured; doing so demystifies the process and keeps treatment on track [Beck 2011, PDF p.81]. Structure ensures that the most important problems are addressed, that time is used well, that homework links sessions, and that the patient is an active partner. It is not rigid: a good therapist flexes the structure to the client's needs (for example, a crisis takes priority over the agenda).

2.1 The three broad stages: beginnings, middles and ends

Many writers divide the process into three phases (Mearns & Thorne's ‘beginnings, middles, ends’; Egan's three stages: identify and clarify problems, develop programmes for constructive change, implement goals) [Notes p.77]. The beginning phase includes negotiating expectations, assessing suitability, forming the alliance, agreeing a contract and helping the client tell their story. The middle phase is where most learning and change occurs (assimilation of problematic experiences is one model of change, Notes p.78). The final phase involves negotiating the ending, referral, dealing with loss, ensuring transfer of learning to real life and anticipating and preventing relapse [Notes p.77–78].

2.2 Process units

Elliott suggests viewing therapy at different time scales: the speaking turn (micro-process of a minute or two); the episode (a task or topic over several minutes); the session; and the whole treatment relationship [Notes p.77]. Structuring operates at each level.

3. Structuring an individual session

3.1 First session (CBT example)

Beck's format for session 1 [Beck 2011, PDF p.82]: Initial part: 1 set the agenda (with a rationale); 2 do a mood check; 3 obtain an update since the evaluation; 4 discuss the diagnosis and do psychoeducation. Middle: 5 identify problems and set goals; 6 educate the patient about the cognitive model; 7 discuss a problem. End: 8 provide or elicit a summary; 9 review homework; 10 elicit feedback.

3.2 Second session and beyond (CBT)

Beck's typical agenda [Beck 2011, PDF p.122–123]: Initial part: 1 mood check; 2 set the agenda; 3 obtain an update (bridge from previous session); 4 review homework; 5 prioritise the agenda. Middle: 6 work on a specific problem and teach skills in that context; 7 follow-up discussion with relevant, collaboratively set homework; 8 work on a second problem. End: 9 provide or elicit a summary; 10 review new homework; 11 elicit feedback. Because experienced and novice therapists differ, a novice can usually discuss in depth about two problems from the agenda in a session [Beck 2011, PDF p.143].

3.3 Establishing rapport, reviewing progress and applying interventions

  • Rapport and alliance: warmth, empathy, collaboration. The alliance is present when the patient feels helped and feels the therapist is collaborating towards common goals [Gabbard 2004, PDF p.58].
  • Mood check and update: a brief review (symptom scores, week's events) so that the session starts with the client's current state; teach the client to be concise [Beck 2011, PDF p.125].
  • Agenda: explain the rationale; ask for the client's items; if the client wants another use of time, negotiate [Beck 2011, PDF p.83–84].
  • Apply interventions inside the agenda (e.g., thought record, activity scheduling, exposure planning).
  • Summarise (capsule summaries during the session; final summary) and assign homework collaboratively; elicit feedback, which strengthens the alliance [Beck 2011, PDF p.42].

5.2 Collaborative goal setting in practice

  1. Elicit problems in the client's words and list them (Beck's problem identification: break big concerns into manageable problems) [Beck 2011, PDF p.90].
  2. Turn problems into goals framed positively (what the client wants more of) and in behavioural terms.
  3. Prioritise: risk and safety first; then the problem most distressing or most likely to yield early success; then the others (Jongsma: an effective plan deals with only a few selected problems or loses direction).
  4. Check feasibility and consent: do the client and relevant others agree? Are resources and time adequate?
  5. Write down goals and objectives; give the client a copy.
  6. Monitor progress with measures and homework; revise goals when achieved, unrealistic or no longer relevant, and record the reason for the change (RCI record outline asks for changes in objectives and reasons).

9Pre- and Post-Assessment

You cannot say therapy worked unless you know where the client started, how they changed during treatment, where they ended up, and whether the change lasted. This topic covers baseline assessment, ongoing monitoring, post-treatment and follow-up assessment; methods; psychometric properties; selecting measures; clinical significance; responding to non-response and deterioration; and why one test score is not proof of effectiveness.

At a glance
  • Four time points: Baseline · Monitoring (during) · Post · Follow-up (B-M-P-F).
  • Methods: clinical interview, behavioural observation, standardised measures, functional assessment, client-reported outcomes, collateral information.
  • Good measure: Valid, Reliable, Sensitive to change, Practical, Appropriate to language/population, with Norms (SPAN-VR).
  • Meaning of change: statistical (reliable change), clinical significance (moved to the functional range), and meaningful to the client.
  • Caution: improvement on one test does not prove treatment worked.

1. Introduction

Assessment is part of every phase of therapy, not just the start. Beck's practice illustrates this: patients complete symptom measures at the evaluation, and the therapist reviews them for hopelessness and suicidality items before deciding on the session's focus [Beck 2011, PDF p.85]; scores are compared each week ([Beck 2011, PDF p.125]), recorded on the session note ([Beck 2011, PDF p.143]), and reviewed at follow-up and booster sessions ([Beck 2011, PDF p.350]). A lack of progress or deterioration, identified from self-report or objective tests, is a signal to change the plan [Beck 2011, PDF p.370].

RCI treats assessment as a core competency: its diploma document lists “competency in selecting, administering, scoring and interpreting psychological tests” as Practical I [RCI Diploma 2011, p.8]. APA's Ethics Code has a whole section on assessment (9.01 bases for assessments; 9.02 use of assessments; 9.03 informed consent in assessments) Professional code.

Worked example: reliable change and clinical significance (hypothetical psychometrics)

Illustrative example Suppose a depression scale has SD = 5.0 in the clinical group and test–retest reliability r = .85.

  1. Standard error of measurement: SEM = SD × sqrt(1 − r) = 5.0 × sqrt0.15 = 1.94.
  2. Standard error of the difference: Sdiff = sqrt2 × SEM = 2.74.
  3. Reliable Change Index: RCI = (scorepost − scorepre) / Sdiff. For 95% confidence, |RCI| > 1.96, i.e., a change of about 1.96 × 2.74 = 5.4 points is needed.
  4. A client falling from 21 to 6 (change of 15) exceeds 5.4, so the change is reliable. If the clinical cut-off is 10, a post-score of 6 is also below the cut-off, so the change is clinically significant.
  5. A client falling from 21 to 17 (change of 4) is below 5.4: not reliable (could be noise).

Note: the numbers are invented to show the method; use the real reliability and SD from the manual or validation study of your measure. The Jacobson–Truax method is a standard psychometric approach, not drawn from the repository books.

5. Selecting measures

  1. Purpose: screening, diagnosis, severity, monitoring, outcome, risk, functioning.
  2. Client factors: age, literacy, language, culture, disability, willingness; some patients cannot or will not fill out tests, and the therapist can teach them to rate mood or anxiety on a simple scale [Beck 2011, PDF p.85].
  3. Population evidence: validation in similar Indian populations if available; avoid using translated tools without checking.
  4. Burden: brief measures for weekly use; longer batteries for baseline and post.
  5. Ethical use: informed consent for assessment (APA 9.03), test security, interpretation by qualified persons (APA 9.07), and sharing results in understandable language.
  6. Multiple sources: self-report plus clinician rating plus function plus collateral.

8. Why improvement on a single test does not establish that therapy worked

  • Regression to the mean: extreme scores tend to move toward the average on retest.
  • Natural remission and time: many problems improve spontaneously; Lister-Ford reports improvement rates of about 40% even among untreated groups in some analyses [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.62].
  • Other influences: medication, life events, social support, placebo/expectancy effects.
  • Measurement issues: unreliable test, demand characteristics (client wishes to please), practice effects.
  • Narrow outcome: symptoms may drop while functioning or quality of life do not.
  • No comparison group: causal claims need controlled designs (RCTs) or at least multiple baselines; routine monitoring shows change, not necessarily cause (Topic 10).
  • Individual vs group results: statistics from trials describe averages, not your client.

  • Record scores and decisions in the clinical record; share results in plain language.

10Practice of Evidence-Based Therapies

Evidence-based practice in psychology (EBPP) is a decision process, not just a list of therapies with research support. A strong answer defines EBPP, distinguishes it from merely using an “evidence-based treatment”, explains the three components, covers research designs and their limits, manuals, fidelity and adaptation, and shows how to select, implement, monitor and modify a treatment responsibly.

At a glance
  • APA (2006): EBPP = integration of the best available research with clinical expertise in the context of patient characteristics, culture and preferences.
  • Three circles: Research · Expertise · Client (REC). Five steps: Ask · Acquire · Appraise · Apply · Assess (5A).
  • Evidence sources: RCTs (efficacy), effectiveness and practice-based studies, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, process research, single-case designs.
  • Limits: generalisability, researcher allegiance, manuals vs responsiveness, not all clients/conditions respond equally.
  • Responsible practice: consent, fidelity with flexibility, outcome monitoring, modify or refer.

EBPP is not the same as “using an evidence-based therapy”. Corey notes that evidence-based practices tend to emphasise only the first pillar (best available research), while Norcross and colleagues advocate inclusive evidence-based practice with three pillars: best research, clinical expertise, and the client's characteristics, culture and preferences. Using a manualised protocol mechanically, without attention to the client and the alliance, is therefore not EBPP.

APA's Council of Representatives (2012) concluded that psychotherapy is effective and highly cost-effective, produces benefits that markedly exceed those of not receiving it, and should be included in health care as an established evidence-based practice; it encouraged further research on comparative effectiveness [APA 2012 Resolution, conclusion] Professional code. The APA Ethics Code (2.04) requires that work be based upon established scientific and professional knowledge of the discipline.

“Evidence-based” refers to evidence for a specific treatment for a specific problem in specific populations.   • Effect size effect size (the magnitude of an effect, independent of sample size): Cohen's d conventions (about 0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large) are common guides but are context dependent. They are general statistical conventions, not drawn from the repository books.
  • Generalisability generalisability (how far findings apply to other people and settings): most trials come from high-income countries; clients in routine practice have complex and multiple problems; the notion of discrete diagnostic categories is questioned. Lister-Ford argues that these differences reduce the external validity of RCTs for practitioners [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.116–117]. Hofmann and Weinberger's volume proposes triangulating on conclusions using multiple designs, including hybrid designs that run RCT methodology in the community [Hofmann 2007, PDF p.28–29].
  • Common factors vs specific ingredients. Lambert (1992) estimated that outcome variance is explained by client/extra-therapeutic factors (about 40%), the therapeutic relationship (about 30%), expectancy/placebo (about 15%) and technique (about 15%) [Corsini 2011, PDF p.194]. These are rough estimates but show why EBPP must include the relationship and the client. Wampold's meta-analysis (cited by Corey) found personal and interpersonal components essential and technique relatively less influential [Corey 2024, PDF p.47].
  • Danger of over-reliance: Parry warns that valuable therapies may become unavailable and innovation damaged if research is used to drive funding cuts rather than inform practice (cited in Lister-Ford PDF p.117). In the US, emphasis on empirically supported treatments, shaped by managed care, led to lists of approved therapies; in the UK, evidence reviews (Roth & Fonagy 1996) supported a range of psychotherapies, an example of two conclusions from similar evidence [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.76, 116].

5. Treatment manuals, guidelines, fidelity, adaptation and competence

  • Treatment manual treatment manual (a written guide specifying the principles, structure and techniques of a therapy). Manuals are intended to define and differentiate psychotherapies, to standardise therapist technique and to permit replication; but they must be implemented by qualified therapists trained to perform them (Project MATCH Appendix C) [MET Manual, PDF p.138].
  • Clinical practice guidelines summarise evidence and recommend treatments (e.g., NICE, APA, WHO mhGAP). Guidelines inform, but do not replace, individual decision-making. Lister-Ford warns that guidelines can become paternalistic if clients are given what is thought best for them rather than involved in choice [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.79].
  • Treatment fidelity fidelity (how closely the treatment delivered matches the intended protocol) has three parts: adherence (delivering the specified elements), competence (skill in delivery) and differentiation (distinct from other therapies). In Project MATCH, therapists were selected for experience, trained in a 3-day seminar plus supervised training cases (average 3 cases; 26 supervised sessions), all sessions videotaped and reviewed against adherence, skillfulness, structure, empathy and alliance, with a proportion rated and supervisors alerted to therapist “drift” [MET Manual, PDF p.138–140]. In routine practice, rating scales such as the Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale measure CBT competence [Beck 2011, PDF p.16, 369].
  • Flexibility within fidelity. Manuals do not undermine the relationship: a strong alliance is essential even in manual-based behaviour therapy, because it engages the patient, enhances motivation and facilitates homework adherence [Corsini 2011, PDF p.267]. Lister-Ford argues that outside research, strict adherence to a formula can be anti-therapeutic if it blocks responsiveness [Lister-Ford 2007, PDF p.117].
  • Adaptation. Culturally and individually adapt content (language, examples, family involvement, pace) while preserving core therapeutic mechanisms. Monitor outcomes after adaptation. Therapist competence limits what you may deliver (APA 2.01).

A · C · D

6. Selecting, implementing, monitoring and modifying an intervention

Mnemonic — the 5A cycle
A · A · A · A · A

Ask (PICO question) · Acquire evidence · Appraise it · Apply with the client · Assess outcome. PICO = Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome. Apply: “In adolescents with panic disorder (P), is CBT (I) better than supportive therapy (C) for panic frequency (O)?”

  1. Assess and formulate the problem (Topics 8–9).
  2. Ask a focused question and acquire the best evidence (guidelines, reviews).
  3. Appraise the evidence for validity, effect size and relevance to your client (culture, comorbidity, setting).
  4. Match to expertise: can you deliver it competently? If not, train, supervise or refer (APA 2.01).
  5. Share the options with the client: benefits, risks, alternatives, time and cost; obtain informed consent (Topic 6).
  6. Implement with fidelity and responsiveness; maintain the alliance.
  7. Monitor outcomes with agreed measures (Topic 9); review at set points.
  8. Modify if non-response/deterioration: check delivery, alliance, formulation; adapt, add, switch or refer.
  9. Document the evidence considered, rationale and client preferences.

7. Examples of evidence-based decision-making Illustrative example

Example A: OCD

Rohan, 34, has contamination OCD. Research: ERP is a first-line psychological treatment (Foa et al. therapist guide; Clark). Expertise: the clinician is trained in ERP under supervision. Client: Rohan fears exposure and prefers a gradual approach; family accommodates rituals. Decision: graded ERP with MET-style motivational work first, family psychoeducation, Y-BOCS at baseline and every 4 weeks, weekly ritual-time log. Review: if no reliable change by week 8, check exposure adherence, ritual accommodation and comorbid depression; consider psychiatric consultation.

Example B: Alcohol use with ambivalence

Ajay, 41, drinks heavily but is unsure he wants to stop. Evidence: MET (four sessions) was developed to produce rapid, internally motivated change by mobilising the client's own resources [MET Manual, PDF p.11]. Client preference: he rejects abstinence-only goals. Decision: start MET with personalised feedback from assessment, negotiate goals (reduction vs abstinence), re-evaluate at midpoint and end, link to aftercare.

Example C: When evidence does not fit

A client with complex trauma, learning disability and low literacy cannot complete a standard manualised protocol. The clinician adapts: shorter sessions, pictorial worksheets, caregiver involvement, and a stabilisation phase; sets simple goals; tracks function and mood with a one-item rating; and documents the departures from the manual and the reasons. This is EBPP: research informs, expertise adapts, client characteristics lead.

8. Ethical and legal considerations

  • Competence (APA 2.01, 2.03): do not offer an evidence-based treatment you are not trained in.
  • Informed consent (APA 10.01(b)): for treatments without established techniques, inform clients of the developing nature, risks, alternatives and voluntariness.
  • Honesty in claims (APA 5.01 and RCI advertising rules Secondary source): do not claim guaranteed outcomes or exaggerate the evidence.
  • Justice: access to evidence-based care should be fair across language, region and income; consider task-sharing (WHO) with supervision.
  • Autonomy: respect refusal or preference for another approach; guidelines should not become coercive.
  • Accountability: document the evidence and reasoning; monitor outcomes; report results honestly.

5. Case-based and ethical-dilemma questions with model answers

Case 1: Threat to a third party Illustrative example

A client says he will “finish” his wife if she goes to her mother's house. Answer framework: ISCLCBED. Identify a possible threat to life; assess risk; consult supervisor; check MHCA s.23(2)(c),(e) and APA 4.05; options include safety planning, voluntary admission, notification of potential victim or police with minimum necessary information; document; revisit consent. See Topic 4 §5.

Case 2: Parents and a 15-year-old disagree Illustrative example

The mother consents to therapy for her 15-year-old son; he refuses to attend and says he will not speak. Answer framework: explore his reasons; explain therapy and confidentiality limits to him in age-appropriate language; seek assent but do not coerce; negotiate a trial session; keep the mother informed in general terms; if refusal persists, discuss with mother and supervisor; document. If he had asked for therapy and the mother refused, legal consent rests with the guardian; explore her concerns; escalate through safeguarding only if there is risk. See Topic 6 §7.

Case 3: Dual relationship in a small town Illustrative example

A client who is also your neighbour's cousin asks you to attend her wedding. Answer framework: APA 3.05 (multiple relationships) and 3.06 (conflict of interest); consider whether attendance could impair objectivity or exploit; decline politely, explain boundaries; discuss within therapy; consult supervisor; document. See Topic 4 §7.

Case 4: Tele-therapy across states Illustrative example

A client moves to another state and wants to continue online. Answer framework: competence in tele-therapy (APA Telepsychology Guideline 1); informed consent specific to telepsychology (Guideline 3); security and disposal of data (Guidelines 5–6); legal and registration questions across jurisdictions (Guideline 8; Corsini PDF p.594); emergency plan; confidentiality limits in the client's location; DPDP duties. See Topic 4 §8.

Case 5: Request for records from a client with unpaid fees Illustrative example

See Topic 5 §9 model answer: consent exists; release a clinically adequate summary (MHCA s.23(2)(b), s.25); do not withhold for non-payment where it affects care (APA 6.03); address fees separately with notice (6.04(e)).

7. Common conceptual confusions and how to avoid them

ConfusionClarification
“Psychotherapy = counselling”Overlapping; distinctions of emphasis; state the view you are quoting
“Confidentiality is absolute”It has legal and ethical limits; explain at the start; release minimum necessary
“Assent replaces consent”No: guardian consent is the legal default; assent is the child's agreement
“Guidelines are laws”APA guidelines are aspirational; statutes are binding
“Evidence-based = a manualised therapy on a list”EBPP integrates research, expertise and client characteristics
“Improvement on a test proves therapy worked”Regression to mean, remission, other influences, no control
“APA rules apply to Indian psychologists as law”They are a respected reference, not Indian law; RCI/MHCA bind in India
“HIPAA applies in India”HIPAA is a US law; used here only for comparison
“All therapies are equal for all problems”Broadly comparable overall, but specific treatments are better supported for specific disorders
“Boundary crossing is always wrong”Some are benign or helpful; violations are exploitative
