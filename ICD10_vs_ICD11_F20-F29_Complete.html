The F20–F29 Block — Overview

The ICD-10 block F20–F29 is titled "Schizophrenia, schizotypal and delusional disorders". It brings together the disorders in which psychosis (a loss of contact with reality, shown by delusions, hallucinations, or grossly disorganised thinking and behaviour) is the defining feature and is not caused by a physical brain/body disease or a substance (those are coded in F00–F09 and F10–F19). Schizophrenia is the most important and most studied disorder of the group. ICD-10

In ICD-11 the corresponding grouping is "Schizophrenia or other primary psychotic disorders", coded in the 6A2 range. The word "primary" (meaning the psychosis is the disorder itself, not a symptom of something else) signals that these psychoses are not secondary to a medical condition or substance. ICD-11

Two key definitions before we begin. A delusion (a fixed, false belief that is firmly held despite clear evidence to the contrary, and not shared by the person's culture) and a hallucination (a perception — hearing, seeing, feeling — that occurs without any real external stimulus) are the two building blocks of psychosis and appear throughout this block.

Schneider's first-rank symptoms (the classic building blocks of the ICD-10 criteria)

First-rank symptoms (a set of symptoms described by Kurt Schneider that were traditionally considered strongly suggestive of schizophrenia) are heavily represented in the ICD-10 groups (a)–(d). Kaplan & Sadock

  • Thought echo (hearing one's own thoughts spoken aloud), thought insertion or withdrawal (the feeling that thoughts are being put into, or taken out of, one's mind by an outside force), and thought broadcasting (the belief that one's thoughts are being transmitted so others can hear them).
  • Delusions of control, influence, or passivity (the belief that one's body, actions, or feelings are being controlled by an external force), and delusional perception (a real perception given a bizarre, self-referential delusional meaning).
  • Third-person auditory hallucinations (voices discussing the patient among themselves, or giving a running commentary on their actions).

Mnemonic for first-rank symptoms — "ABCD"

Auditory hallucinations (third-person, running commentary, thought echo) · Broadcasting of thought (and insertion/withdrawal) · Controlled feelings/impulses/acts (passivity) · Delusional perception.

F20 · Schizophrenia → ICD-11 6A20

ICD-10 F20 (.0 paranoid, .1 hebephrenic, .2 catatonic, .3 undifferentiated, .4 post-schizophrenic depression, .5 residual, .6 simple, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6A20 (first episode / multiple episodes / continuous; each with symptomatic, partial or full remission)
Core The prototypical primary psychotic disorder
Key change ICD-11 abolished the classic subtypes; uses symptom-domain and course specifiers instead

1. Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe, often chronic primary psychotic disorder (a disorder in which psychosis is the illness itself, not caused by another condition) characterised by fundamental distortions of thinking, perception, and affect (the outward expression of emotion). The core disturbance is a loss of the normal sense of self and of the boundary between the self and the outside world. Consciousness and, usually, intellectual capacity are preserved, although cognitive deficits accumulate over time. ICD-10

2. Etiology (causes)

Schizophrenia is best understood through the stress–vulnerability model (an interaction between an inherited/biological predisposition and environmental stressors that together trigger the illness). Kaplan & Sadock

  • Genetic: the strongest risk factor is family history; risk rises from about 1% in the general population to much higher in first-degree relatives and highest in the identical twin of an affected person. Many genes of small effect are involved (polygenic — many genes each contributing a little).
  • Neurochemical — the dopamine hypothesis: (the theory that psychotic symptoms arise from excess dopamine activity in certain brain pathways) overactivity of dopamine in the mesolimbic pathway is linked to positive symptoms; underactivity in the mesocortical pathway is linked to negative symptoms. Glutamate and serotonin systems are also implicated.
  • Neurodevelopmental: obstetric complications, prenatal infection or malnutrition, and winter birth; subtle brain changes (enlarged ventricles, reduced grey matter) suggest an early developmental origin.
  • Environmental: cannabis use in adolescence, urban birth/upbringing, migration, childhood adversity, and social stress.

3. Epidemiology

  • Lifetime prevalence is approximately 1% worldwide, remarkably consistent across cultures. Kaplan & Sadock
  • Sex: roughly equal overall; men have an earlier onset (late teens to mid-20s) than women (late 20s), and women show a second smaller peak after age 45.
  • Associated with substantially reduced life expectancy (cardiovascular disease, metabolic effects of treatment, and a high suicide rate — roughly 5–10% die by suicide).
  • Better long-term outcomes have historically been reported in some developing countries.

4. Clinical features — identifying symptoms

Positive symptoms

  • Delusions (often persecutory, of reference, or bizarre).
  • Hallucinations (classically auditory — third-person voices or running commentary).
  • Formal thought disorder (disorganised thinking shown in speech — loosening of associations, tangential replies, or neologisms, newly invented words).
  • Passivity phenomena and thought interference (first-rank symptoms).

Negative symptoms

  • Affective flattening/blunting; alogia (poverty of speech).
  • Avolition and apathy (loss of drive).
  • Anhedonia; social withdrawal; self-neglect.

Identifying clues — "recognise schizophrenia"

  • Onset in late adolescence or early adulthood, often with a prodrome (an early phase of subtle decline in functioning, odd ideas, and withdrawal before frank psychosis).
  • First-rank symptoms (thought insertion/withdrawal/broadcasting, passivity, third-person voices, delusional perception) — strongly suggestive.
  • Clear consciousness and preserved orientation (distinguishing it from delirium).
  • Progressive functional decline with accumulating negative symptoms and cognitive impairment.
  • Lack of insight is common.

Associated conditions and complications

  • High suicide risk, especially early in the illness and in post-schizophrenic depression (F20.4).
  • Comorbid substance use (especially nicotine, cannabis, and alcohol), which worsens outcome.
  • Metabolic syndrome, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease (partly from antipsychotics).
  • Tardive dyskinesia (late-onset, potentially irreversible involuntary movements caused by long-term antipsychotic use) and other movement side-effects.
  • Social complications: unemployment, homelessness, stigma.

5. Diagnostic criteria (strictly from ICD-10 and ICD-11)

ICD-10 Diagnostic guidelines

The symptoms are divided into groups of special diagnostic importance:

  1. Thought echo, thought insertion or withdrawal, and thought broadcasting.
  2. Delusions of control, influence, or passivity (referred to body movements, thoughts, actions, or sensations); delusional perception.
  3. Hallucinatory voices giving a running commentary, or discussing the patient among themselves, or other voices coming from a part of the body.
  4. Persistent delusions of other kinds that are culturally inappropriate and completely impossible (e.g. superhuman powers).
  5. Persistent hallucinations in any modality, accompanied by fleeting delusions or persistent over-valued ideas, or occurring daily for weeks/months.
  6. Breaks or interpolations in the train of thought, resulting in incoherence, irrelevant speech, or neologisms.
  7. Catatonic behaviour (excitement, posturing, waxy flexibility — limbs stay in whatever position they are placed, negativism, mutism, stupor).
  8. "Negative" symptoms (marked apathy, poverty of speech, blunting/incongruity of affect), not due to depression or medication.
  9. A significant, consistent change in overall quality of behaviour (loss of interest, aimlessness, social withdrawal).

The rule: a diagnosis requires at least one very clear symptom (two or more if less clear) from groups (a)–(d), OR symptoms from at least two of the groups (e)–(h), present for most of the time during a period of at least one month. If the same symptoms are present for less than one month, diagnose acute schizophrenia-like psychotic disorder (F23.2) unless/until they persist.

ICD-10 subtypes: F20.0 paranoid (dominated by delusions/hallucinations); F20.1 hebephrenic (disorganised affect and behaviour, early onset, poor prognosis); F20.2 catatonic (psychomotor disturbance); F20.3 undifferentiated; F20.4 post-schizophrenic depression; F20.5 residual (chronic negative symptoms); F20.6 simple (insidious negative decline without florid psychosis).

ICD-11 Essential (required) features (6A20)

At least two of the following symptoms must be present (by the individual's report or through observation), for most of the time for at least one month, and at least one must be from items 1–4 (the "core positive" symptoms):

  1. Persistent delusions;
  2. Persistent hallucinations (most commonly auditory);
  3. Disorganised thinking (formal thought disorder), e.g. tangential/incoherent speech;
  4. Experiences of influence, passivity, or control (the feeling that thoughts/feelings/actions are controlled by an external force);
  5. Negative symptoms (affective flattening, alogia, avolition, anhedonia, asociality);
  6. Grossly disorganised behaviour;
  7. Psychomotor disturbances (catatonic restlessness/agitation, posturing, waxy flexibility, mutism, or stupor).

The symptoms are not a manifestation of another medical condition or the effect of a substance. Subtypes are not used; instead the clinician records the course (first episode / multiple episodes / continuous, each as currently symptomatic, in partial remission, or in full remission) and rates the symptom domains (6A25: positive, negative, depressive, manic, psychomotor, cognitive).

6. Easy way to remember

Mnemonics

First-rank symptoms — "ABCD": Auditory hallucinations (3rd-person/commentary/thought echo), Broadcasting (+ insertion/withdrawal of thought), Control/passivity of feelings-impulses-acts, Delusional perception.

Negative symptoms — the "5 A's": Affective flattening, Alogia (poverty of speech), Avolition, Anhedonia, Asociality.

ICD-10 diagnostic rule — "1 from A–D, or 2 from E–H, for 1 month."

7. Treatment

Pharmacological Kaplan & Sadock

  • Antipsychotics (medications that reduce psychotic symptoms, mainly by blocking dopamine receptors) are the mainstay: first-generation ("typical") agents (e.g. haloperidol, chlorpromazine) and second-generation ("atypical") agents (e.g. risperidone, olanzapine, aripiprazole).
  • Clozapine (the most effective antipsychotic, reserved for treatment-resistant schizophrenia because it can lower white blood cells, requiring regular blood monitoring) for treatment resistance.
  • Long-acting injectable ("depot") antipsychotics improve adherence.
  • Watch for side-effects: extrapyramidal side-effects (movement problems such as stiffness, tremor, restlessness), tardive dyskinesia, metabolic syndrome, sedation, and (rarely) neuroleptic malignant syndrome (a life-threatening reaction with fever, rigidity, and confusion).

Psychological and psychosocial

  • CBT for psychosis (therapy that helps the person test and cope with delusional beliefs and voices); family therapy / psychoeducation to reduce expressed emotion (high levels of criticism or over-involvement in the family, which raise relapse risk).
  • Social skills training, supported employment, cognitive remediation, assertive community treatment, and relapse-prevention planning.

F21 · Schizotypal Disorder → ICD-11 6A22

ICD-10 F21
ICD-11 6A22
Type Chronic, schizophrenia-like but sub-threshold
Key point Odd but never frankly psychotic; no clear onset

1. Introduction

A disorder of eccentric behaviour and anomalies of thinking and affect that resemble schizophrenia but in which no definite and characteristic schizophrenic symptoms occur at any stage. There is no clear onset; it runs a chronic course fluctuating in intensity, and it is genetically related to schizophrenia (more common in relatives of people with schizophrenia). ICD-10

2–3. Etiology and epidemiology

Considered part of the schizophrenia spectrum (a family of related conditions sharing genetic vulnerability with schizophrenia); more frequent among biological relatives of people with schizophrenia. Kaplan & Sadock

4. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Inappropriate or constricted affect (appears cold and aloof).
  • Odd, eccentric behaviour and appearance; poor social rapport and social withdrawal.
  • Magical thinking (odd beliefs influencing behaviour, e.g. in telepathy or a "sixth sense") and ideas of reference (the feeling that ordinary events have special personal meaning).
  • Suspiciousness or paranoid ideas; unusual perceptual experiences; vague, circumstantial, or over-elaborate speech (without frank incoherence).
  • Occasional transient quasi-psychotic episodes with intense illusions or hallucinations, but these are brief and never sustained.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

This diagnostic rubric is not recommended for general use, but where used it requires three or four of the typical features (listed above) to have been present, continuously or episodically, for at least two years. The individual must never have met the criteria for schizophrenia itself.

ICD-11 (6A22)

An enduring pattern (several years) of eccentricities in behaviour, appearance, and speech, accompanied by cognitive and perceptual distortions, unusual beliefs, and discomfort with (and reduced capacity for) interpersonal relationships. Symptoms are not of sufficient intensity or duration to meet the requirements for schizophrenia.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — "ODD-MAP"

Odd/eccentric behaviour · Detached (constricted) affect · Distrust (paranoid ideas) · Magical thinking · Abnormal perceptions · Peculiar speech. Never fully psychotic, chronic, spectrum-related.

Treatment: supportive psychotherapy and social-skills support; low-dose antipsychotics only for transient psychotic-like symptoms; treat comorbid anxiety/depression. Kaplan & Sadock

F22 · Persistent Delusional Disorders → ICD-11 6A24

ICD-10 F22 (.0 delusional disorder, .8 other, .9 unspecified)
ICD-11 6A24 Delusional disorder
Type A single delusion or set of related delusions, otherwise normal
Key point No prominent hallucinations, no thought disorder, personality preserved

1. Introduction

A group of disorders in which a long-standing delusion (or set of related delusions) is the only, or the most conspicuous, clinical feature. The delusions are highly variable in content but are typically non-bizarre (they concern situations that could conceivably occur in real life — being followed, poisoned, deceived by a partner, loved from afar). Crucially, there are no persistent hallucinations, no schizophrenic thought disorder, and the personality and functioning outside the delusion remain largely intact. ICD-10

2–3. Etiology and epidemiology

Onset is usually in middle or late adult life. Less common than schizophrenia. Sensory impairment (deafness), social isolation, and migration are recognised risk factors. Kaplan & Sadock

4. Identifying symptoms and delusional themes

Classic delusional themes (identifying clues)

  • Persecutory (belief of being conspired against, cheated, or harmed) — the commonest type.
  • Grandiose (inflated belief in one's own worth, power, or special identity).
  • Jealous / Othello syndrome (the fixed false belief that a partner is unfaithful).
  • Erotomanic / de Clérambault syndrome (the belief that another person, often of higher status, is secretly in love with them).
  • Somatic (a false belief about one's body, e.g. of infestation or a foul odour).
  • Behaviour and emotion outside the delusion are normal; there is often good preservation of social function, which can make the disorder hard to detect.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

A delusion or set of delusions must be present for at least 3 months, and must be personal (not sub-cultural). Depressive symptoms or even a depressive episode may be present intermittently, provided the delusions persist at times when there is no mood disturbance. There must be no evidence of brain disease, no or only occasional auditory hallucinations, and no history of schizophrenic symptoms (delusions of control, thought broadcasting, blunted affect, etc.).

ICD-11 (6A24)

The presence of a delusion or set of related delusions, typically persisting for at least 3 months (usually much longer), occurring in the absence of other characteristic symptoms of schizophrenia (e.g. clear and persistent hallucinations, thought disorder, negative symptoms). Apart from actions and attitudes directly related to the delusion, affect, speech, and behaviour are unaffected.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic for delusional themes — "PGJES"

Persecutory · Grandiose · Jealous (Othello) · Erotomanic (de Clérambault) · Somatic. Key rule: delusion only, otherwise normal, for at least 3 months.

Treatment: antipsychotics (often with limited response and adherence problems because insight is poor); build a therapeutic alliance; treat comorbid depression; CBT can help. Kaplan & Sadock

F24 · Induced Delusional Disorder (Folie à Deux)

ICD-10 F24
ICD-11 No exact stand-alone equivalent
Type A shared delusion between two (or more) closely linked people
Also known as Folie à deux ("madness of two")

1. Introduction and identifying features

A delusional disorder shared by two or more people with close emotional links. Only one of them has a genuine psychotic disorder (the primary / inducer); the delusions are induced in the other(s) (the secondary / recipient), who is usually more suggestible, dependent, and socially isolated with the primary. ICD-10

Identifying clues

  • Two (or more) people share the same delusion.
  • They live in unusually close association and are relatively isolated from others.
  • The delusions of the passive partner(s) usually resolve when the pair is separated — a key diagnostic and therapeutic clue.

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Diagnosed only if: (1) two or more people share the same delusion or delusional system and support one another in it; (2) they have an unusually close relationship; and (3) there is temporal or contextual evidence that the delusion was induced in the passive member(s) by contact with the active partner.

ICD-11

There is no dedicated stand-alone category; such presentations are captured under delusional disorder or other specified primary psychotic disorder, taking into account the induced/shared context.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic

"Two, close, isolated, shared — separate to cure." Folie à deux = madness of two.

Treatment: separate the individuals (the recipient's delusions often fade); treat the primary person's psychotic disorder; support and monitor the recipient. Kaplan & Sadock

F23 · Acute and Transient Psychotic Disorders → ICD-11 6A23

ICD-10 F23 (.0 acute polymorphic without schizophrenic symptoms, .1 with schizophrenic symptoms, .2 acute schizophrenia-like, .3 other predominantly delusional, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6A23 Acute and transient psychotic disorder
Type Sudden-onset, short-lived psychosis
Key point Acute onset, rapidly changing ("polymorphic") picture, full recovery

1. Introduction

A group of disorders defined by their acute onset and brief, fluctuating course. They are especially common in developing countries and are often preceded by acute stress. The characteristic form is polymorphic (the type and intensity of symptoms change rapidly, even from day to day — a kaleidoscopic, shifting picture). ICD-10

2–3. Etiology and epidemiology

Frequently triggered by a stressful life event; more common in women and in lower-income countries. Prognosis is generally good, with full recovery within weeks to a few months, although some cases later evolve into schizophrenia or a mood disorder. Kaplan & Sadock

4. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Acute onset defined as within 2 weeks from a non-psychotic to a clearly psychotic state.
  • Rapidly changing ("polymorphic") delusions, hallucinations, and perceptual disturbances, often with emotional turmoil (rapidly shifting intense happiness, anxiety, and irritability).
  • Frequent association with acute stress (onset within about 2 weeks of a stressful event).
  • Rapid, complete recovery (usually within weeks; the diagnosis is revised if symptoms persist).

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Requires acute onset (within 2 weeks) as the defining feature; the presence of typical syndromes (polymorphic, or schizophrenia-like); and the presence or absence of associated acute stress (a fifth character marks this). Duration limits distinguish the subtypes — if schizophrenic symptoms persist beyond 1 month, the diagnosis is changed to schizophrenia (F20). Full recovery usually occurs within 2–3 months, often within weeks or days.

ICD-11 (6A23)

Acute onset of psychotic symptoms that reach their maximum severity within about two weeks. Symptoms typically change rapidly in nature and intensity from day to day, or within a day (polymorphic). Symptoms have not persisted for more than three months (most last days to a month). If the picture persists, an alternative diagnosis (e.g. schizophrenia) is considered.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic — "SPAR"

Sudden onset (within 2 weeks) · Polymorphic, rapidly changing symptoms · Associated acute stress often · Rapid, full recovery. If it lasts beyond a month with schizophrenic features → reconsider schizophrenia.

Treatment: short-term antipsychotics and management of any precipitating stress; benzodiazepines for agitation; most recover fully and treatment can often be withdrawn; follow up because a minority evolve into schizophrenia or a mood disorder. Kaplan & Sadock

F25 · Schizoaffective Disorders → ICD-11 6A21

ICD-10 F25 (.0 manic type, .1 depressive type, .2 mixed type, .8/.9)
ICD-11 6A21 Schizoaffective disorder
Type Schizophrenic and mood symptoms together in the same episode
Key point Both sets of symptoms are prominent and roughly simultaneous

1. Introduction

Episodic disorders in which both schizophrenic and affective (mood) symptoms are prominent within the same episode of illness, either simultaneously or within a few days of each other. The disorder sits "between" schizophrenia and the mood disorders. ICD-10

2–3. Etiology and epidemiology

Shares genetic risk with both schizophrenia and mood disorders; prognosis is generally intermediate — better than schizophrenia but worse than a pure mood disorder. Kaplan & Sadock

4. Identifying symptoms

Identifying clues

  • Simultaneously prominent schizophrenic symptoms (e.g. first-rank symptoms, bizarre delusions) and a full mood syndrome (manic or depressive) within the same episode.
  • Manic type (F25.0): elevated mood/grandiosity with schizophrenic symptoms — usually a good prognosis.
  • Depressive type (F25.1): depressive symptoms with schizophrenic symptoms — a more prolonged course.
  • Distinguishing feature: the psychotic symptoms are not confined to the periods of mood disturbance (that would make it a mood disorder with psychotic features).

5. Diagnostic criteria

ICD-10

Diagnosed only when both definite schizophrenic and definite affective symptoms are prominent simultaneously, or within a few days of each other, within the same episode, and the episode consequently meets neither the criteria for schizophrenia alone nor for a manic or depressive episode alone. Subtypes: F25.0 manic type, F25.1 depressive type, F25.2 mixed type.

ICD-11 (6A21)

All diagnostic requirements for schizophrenia are met concurrently with those for a moderate or severe mood episode (manic, depressive, or mixed), within the same episode of illness. The diagnosis requires that the two sets of symptoms occur together; if psychotic symptoms occur only during mood episodes, a mood disorder with psychotic features is diagnosed instead.

6–7. Remember and treatment

Mnemonic

"Schizophrenia + mood episode, together, same episode." If psychosis is ONLY during the mood episode → it is a mood disorder with psychotic features, not schizoaffective.

Treatment: combination of an antipsychotic with a mood stabiliser (e.g. lithium or valproate) or an antidepressant, according to the predominant mood pole; psychosocial support; ECT in severe or treatment-resistant cases. Kaplan & Sadock

F29 · Unspecified Nonorganic Psychosis → ICD-11 6A2Z

ICD-10 F29
ICD-11 6A2Z Schizophrenia or other primary psychotic disorder, unspecified
Type Residual "unspecified" category

A catch-all for psychosis with no organic cause where information is insufficient to make a more specific diagnosis, or where the features do not fit any defined category. It is a provisional label pending clarification. ICD-10 ICD-11

Practice point: "unspecified psychosis" is often used in emergency or first-contact settings before enough history is available; it should be revised to a specific diagnosis as the picture clarifies.

Two golden rules for the block

Rule 1 — sort the psychoses by duration: days–1 month = acute/transient; ≥1 month = schizophrenia; ≥3 months delusion-only = delusional disorder; ≥2 years odd-but-not-psychotic = schizotypal.

Rule 2 — always exclude an organic/substance cause first: clear consciousness + auditory hallucinations + no medical trigger points to a primary psychosis; clouded consciousness or visual hallucinations point to a secondary (organic/substance) cause.

PsychosisA loss of contact with reality, shown by delusions, hallucinations, or grossly disorganised thinking/behaviour.
Primary psychotic disorderA psychosis that is the illness itself, not caused by a medical condition or substance.
DelusionA fixed, false belief held despite clear contrary evidence and not shared by the person's culture.
HallucinationA perception (hearing, seeing, feeling) that occurs without any real external stimulus.
Positive symptomsAdded, abnormal experiences not normally present (delusions, hallucinations, disorganisation).
Negative symptomsNormal functions that are reduced or lost (flat affect, poor speech, low drive, anhedonia).
First-rank symptomsA set of symptoms (Schneider) traditionally considered strongly suggestive of schizophrenia.
Thought echoHearing one's own thoughts spoken aloud.
Thought insertion / withdrawalFeeling that thoughts are put into, or removed from, one's mind by an outside force.
Thought broadcastingBelief that one's thoughts are transmitted so others can hear them.
Passivity / delusion of controlBelief that one's actions, feelings, or body are controlled by an external force.
Delusional perceptionA real perception given a bizarre, self-referential delusional meaning.
Formal thought disorderDisorganised thinking shown in speech (loose associations, tangential replies, neologisms).
NeologismA newly invented word used by the patient.
Ideas of referenceThe feeling that ordinary events have a special personal meaning.
Affect / affective flatteningThe outward expression of emotion / a marked reduction in that expression.
AlogiaPoverty of speech (reduced amount or content of speech).
AvolitionLack of motivation or drive to start and continue activities.
AnhedoniaInability to feel pleasure.
CatatoniaA syndrome of marked motor disturbance: stupor/immobility or excitement, with posturing, mutism, negativism.
Waxy flexibilityLimbs remain in whatever position they are placed (a catatonic sign).
ProdromeAn early phase of subtle decline and odd experiences before frank psychosis.
PolymorphicRapidly changing in type and intensity of symptoms (a shifting picture).
Non-bizarre delusionA delusion about something that could conceivably happen in real life (being followed, deceived).
Othello syndromeThe delusion that one's partner is unfaithful (delusional jealousy).
De Clérambault syndrome (erotomania)The delusion that another person, often of higher status, is secretly in love with the patient.
Folie à deuxA delusion shared between two closely linked people ("madness of two").
Expressed emotionHigh levels of criticism or over-involvement in the family, which increase relapse risk.
Antipsychotic (neuroleptic)A medication that reduces psychotic symptoms, mainly by blocking dopamine.
ClozapineThe most effective antipsychotic, reserved for treatment resistance; requires blood monitoring.
Extrapyramidal side-effectsMovement side-effects of antipsychotics (stiffness, tremor, restlessness).
Tardive dyskinesiaLate-onset, potentially irreversible involuntary movements from long-term antipsychotic use.
Neuroleptic malignant syndromeA rare, life-threatening reaction to antipsychotics: fever, rigidity, confusion, autonomic instability.
