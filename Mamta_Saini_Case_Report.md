## 7. ASSESSMENTS ADMINISTERED

1. **Dissociative Experiences Scale (DES)**
2. **Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)**
3. **Bech–Rafaelsen Depression / Bipolar Depression Rating Scale (BDRS)**
4. **Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)**
5. **Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)**

### Rationale for Each Test

- **DES (Dissociative Experiences Scale):** Administered because the core clinical picture is dissociative — depersonalisation/derealisation, identity alteration ("possession"), amnesic gaps, and absorption. The DES is a well-validated self-report screen that quantifies the frequency and pervasiveness of dissociative experiences, helping to establish the severity of dissociation and support/rule against a dissociative (trance and possession) disorder.
- **BAI (Beck Anxiety Inventory):** Administered because prominent somatic anxiety features are reported (restlessness, inability to relax, tremors, heart pounding, breathlessness, numbness/tingling, dizziness). The BAI isolates the somatic and subjective severity of anxiety and helps differentiate anxiety-driven somatic symptoms from purely dissociative phenomena.
- **BDRS (depression rating):** Administered to quantify the depressive component reported in history (persistent low mood over episode periods, reduced energy, appetite/weight loss, social withdrawal). It helps clarify whether a comorbid or secondary depressive syndrome is present and its severity.
- **BPRS (Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale):** Administered as a broad-band clinician-rated measure to screen the overall level of psychopathology and, importantly, to evaluate for psychotic-spectrum features (given "suspiciousness" and "seeing dead females"), thereby helping to differentiate dissociative/trance phenomena from a primary psychotic disorder.
- **TAT (Thematic Apperception Test):** Administered as a projective measure to access underlying personality dynamics, dominant needs, perceived environmental pressures, characteristic conflicts, defences, and ego strength that are not readily accessible through direct interview — informing case conceptualisation and therapy planning.

### Test Scores

| Scale | Total Score | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| **BAI** (Beck Anxiety Inventory) | **36** | Severe / concerning level of anxiety |
| **BDRS** | **20** | Moderate depressive symptomatology |
| **BPRS** (Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale) | **22** | Mild overall psychopathology |
| **DES** (Dissociative Experiences Scale) | **125** | Elevated — clinically significant dissociation |

### Interpretation of Scales

- **BAI = 36:** Falls in the **severe anxiety** range. This is consistent with the reported somatic-autonomic arousal (tremors, palpitations, breathlessness, numbness, restlessness, inability to relax). Anxiety appears to be a significant maintaining and precipitating component around dissociative episodes.
- **BDRS = 20:** Indicates a **moderate level of depressive symptomatology**, consistent with the historical account of persistent low mood, reduced energy, appetite loss with ~10 kg weight loss, and social withdrawal during episode periods. Depression appears secondary/reactive to her chronic interpersonal stressors rather than a primary mood disorder (note: no independent history of pervasive anhedonia or elevated mood).
- **BPRS = 22:** A relatively **low total score** indicating only mild overall psychopathology and, in particular, **no strong evidence of an active primary psychotic process**. The "suspiciousness" and perceptual reports are better accounted for within the dissociative/trance-possession framework and the culturally embedded faith-healing context than by a psychotic disorder.
- **DES = 125:** **Markedly elevated**, indicating clinically significant and pervasive dissociative experiences. This strongly supports the primacy of dissociation in the clinical picture and is consistent with a dissociative (trance and possession) presentation. *(Note for supervision: DES is typically scored as a mean 0–100; a raw sum of 125 across items should be recorded transparently and, if required, converted to the standard mean score for norm comparison. ### Integrated Summary (TAT)

The analysis of the subject's TAT responses indicates that she was able to comprehend the task and generate coherent stories with a clear beginning, progression, and outcome. The narratives were adequately structured and meaningful, suggesting adequate comprehension and average general intellectual functioning. The stories were largely realistic and emotionally grounded, with strong personal involvement, indicating high self-referential thinking.

Across the cards, the heroes were frequently female characters closely resembling the subject herself, reflecting strong identification. The central figures were consistently portrayed as emotionally sensitive, burdened by interpersonal stress, and struggling with feelings of sadness, anger, and helplessness. A recurring emotional tone across the stories was emotional distress combined with internal conflict, particularly in the context of close relationships (marital conflict, infidelity, being disbelieved, and unfair burdening within the family).

A prominent pattern was the over-control and inward turning of aggression: in the domestic-conflict story, anger is suppressed and the heroine submits to unfair work; in the infidelity and illness stories, hurt and protest are followed by loss or belated validation rather than effective assertion. Emotional pain, anger, and disappointment are largely contained or directed inward rather than expressed constructively, and blame is at times projected onto burdening figures (e.g., the "lazy" mother-in-law).

Strong needs for **affiliation** and **nurturance** were evident, particularly in the mother–daughter, family, and blank-card stories, reflecting a deep concern for emotional connection, security, and responsibility toward others—especially children. However, these affiliative needs frequently appeared **unmet**, contributing to feelings of loneliness, rejection, and emotional deprivation (notably the woman-alone-in-the-forest story), with a recurrent **wish to be rescued or "taken along"** (n-Succorance) coexisting with a fear of abandonment.

Interpersonal relationships, especially with the husband and marital-family figures, were commonly depicted as emotionally unsupportive, dismissive, or invalidating (infidelity, "this is your usual drama"). Male figures were often perceived as dominant, self-focused, or emotionally detached, while the female protagonist appeared compliant, restrained, or silently distressed—reflecting a relational pattern of power imbalance, emotional dependency, and difficulty asserting personal needs.

Environmental press was largely **interpersonal** (criticism, rejection, unfair role expectations, imposed tasks) and, in places, an empty/threatening world; overt hostility was rarely enacted directly. In contrast, togetherness, family, and nurturing roles were idealised as safe and restorative, and the blank card produced a compensatory image of an intact, harmonious family—suggesting reliance on affiliation, idealisation, and role-based coping.

Overall, the TAT profile reflects significant unresolved emotional conflicts, with prominent themes of interpersonal stress, betrayal/abandonment, being disbelieved, anger turned inward, and unmet needs for closeness and validation. Despite emotional vulnerability, the subject shows **adequate ego strength, reality orientation, and moral awareness**. Her coping style, however, appears constrained by emotional inhibition, over-controlled aggression, compliance, self-blame, and a wish for rescue rather than active assertion. The narratives point to a strong longing for emotional safety, validation, and understanding, coupled with difficulty trusting others—or her own voice—to meet these needs. These dynamics are highly consistent with the presenting dissociative (trance/possession) picture, in which symptoms may serve a communicative and role-sanctioned function in an environment where direct expression of distress and anger is not permitted.

## 8. DIAGNOSTIC FORMULATION & CLINICAL IMPRESSION

Index patient **MS**, a 30-year-old married female, educated up to 8th grade, presented with chief complaints consistent with a dissociative presentation: she is convinced she has been "taken away" by a spirit/ghost, and experiences anger outbursts, trembling and shaking, deepened voice, heart pounding, difficulty breathing, dizziness, a feeling that objects are unreal, numbness, inability to relax, difficulty initiating sleep, and appetite disturbance.

These episodes are unwanted and troublesome, occur outside her subjective control, and represent a change in the normal sense of personal identity, with substitution by a new "identity" attributed to spirits. There is a **convincing temporal association between symptom onset and stressful interpersonal/life events** (chronic conflict with sister-in-law and mother-in-law; a non-consensual arranged marriage; emotional neglect by the husband; reinforcement by faith healers). There is **no evidence of a physical disorder** (normal CT, MRI, and medical profiling) or of substance use that could explain the symptoms, and standardized assessment (low BPRS, markedly elevated DES) does not support a primary psychotic disorder.

**Clinical impression:** The presentation is best understood as a **dissociative (trance and possession) disorder** arising in a psychologically vulnerable, emotionally inhibited woman within a culturally sanctioned faith-healing context, with significant secondary anxiety and reactive depressive features, maintained by secondary gains (increased spousal attention, reduced obligation to interact with conflictual family members).

---

## 9. PROVISIONAL DIAGNOSIS

**Trance and Possession Disorder — ICD-10 F44.3**
(Dissociative [conversion] disorders category)

With associated features:
- Prominent secondary anxiety (BAI = 36, severe range)
- Reactive/secondary depressive symptoms (BDRS = 20, moderate)

*Differentials considered and provisionally ruled out:* primary psychotic disorder (low BPRS; symptoms culturally patterned and dissociative in form), organic/neurological cause (normal CT/MRI/medical work-up), substance-related disorder (nil substance history), primary mood disorder (no independent pervasive mood syndrome).

## 10. POINTS IN FAVOUR

- Insidious onset with an **episodic, deteriorating course** temporally linked to psychosocial stressors.
- **Transient alteration of identity** attributed to external "spirits" (naming Sita, Iqbal, Sakeen), with subjectively involuntary "possession."
- Loss of personal control over behaviour and voice (deepened voice, head-rotating, falling), with **partial/absent recall** and perseveration on episodes.
- **No organic correlate** — normal CT, MRI, and medical profiling.
- **No evidence of primary psychosis** (low BPRS; no first-rank symptoms, no true hallucinations/thought alienation on negative history).
- **Markedly elevated DES (125)** indicating pervasive dissociation.
- **Culturally embedded** onset and course through faith-healing (possession explained and reinforced by healers).
- Clear **secondary gains** (spousal attention, reduced contact with conflictual in-laws) and **symptom-free at the new residence**, indicating context-dependence rather than a fixed organic/psychotic process.
- Premorbid vulnerability: emotionally inhibited, self-critical, over-controlled aggression, high fantasy life, over-religiosity, chronic interpersonal stress and disempowerment (non-consensual marriage, silenced opinions).

## 11. CASE CONCEPTUALISATION

**Predisposing:** Strict, controlling upbringing with suppression of autonomy and opinion; early conditioning into a submissive, self-critical, emotion-inhibited style; non-consensual arranged marriage at 18; high fantasy proneness and strong religiosity; chronic low position and lack of validation within the marital family.

**Precipitating:** Ongoing conflict with, and perceived favouritism towards, the sister-in-law and mother-in-law; emotional neglect and limited attention from the husband; exposure to and reinforcement by faith healers who provided a "possession" explanatory model; a triggering trip (Ujjain) followed by intensified symptoms.

**Perpetuating:** Secondary gains—significantly increased attention and care from the husband during episodes, and legitimate withdrawal from conflictual interactions with the in-laws; the family's and healers' possession framework validating and reinforcing the symptoms; high anxiety and sleep disruption feeding an arousal–dissociation cycle; symptom relief on relocation reinforcing avoidance of the "first house."

**Protective factors:** Adequate ego strength, reality orientation, and intelligence (TAT); preserved self-care and childcare even when unwell; supportive (if partially understanding) husband; open family communication; strong nurturant and affiliative capacities; symptom-free periods; no substance use, no suicidality.

**Cross-sectional mechanism (working model):** In a woman for whom direct expression of anger and distress is neither permitted nor validated, chronic interpersonal stress and emotional inhibition generate high autonomic arousal (severe anxiety) and low mood. Distress that cannot be expressed or "heard" is channelled, within a culturally available idiom, into dissociative/trance-possession episodes. These episodes communicate distress, temporarily discharge over-controlled affect, elicit care, and legitimise withdrawal from conflict—thereby being negatively and positively reinforced and recurring.

## 12. LONG-TERM AND SHORT-TERM GOALS

**Short-term goals**
1. Establish therapeutic rapport, safety, and a collaborative, non-stigmatising formulation.
2. Provide psychoeducation about dissociation, stress, and the mind–body link (gently bridging from the "spirit/stress" model).
3. Reduce acute anxiety and arousal (relaxation, breathing, grounding) and stabilise sleep.
4. Teach grounding and early-warning identification to reduce frequency/intensity of dissociative episodes.
5. Begin an emotion diary linking episodes to interpersonal triggers and feelings.

**Long-term goals**
1. Develop adaptive emotional expression and assertiveness (especially anger and needs) to replace dissociative expression.
2. Address maladaptive beliefs (self-blame, "my distress isn't valid/won't be believed," rigid role expectations).
3. Improve interpersonal functioning and boundaries with the marital family; increase healthy support and validation.
4. Undertake family/couple sessions to modify the family's explanatory model, reduce reinforcement of symptoms, and increase appropriate emotional support.
5. Relapse prevention: consolidate coping skills, reduce reliance on symptoms for secondary gains, and support graded, safe re-engagement with avoided situations.

---

## 13. GOAL OF THERAPY

To reduce the frequency, intensity, and functional impact of dissociative (trance/possession) episodes by (a) lowering the underlying anxiety and physiological arousal, (b) building direct, adaptive channels for expressing and regulating emotion (especially anger and unmet needs), (c) restructuring self-critical and invalidating beliefs, (d) improving interpersonal assertiveness and boundaries within the family, and (e) modifying the family/system factors that maintain the symptoms—so as to restore a stable sense of self-control and improve overall quality of life.

---

## 14. THERAPY PLANNING

An **integrative, stepped, and culturally sensitive plan**, primarily CBT-informed with stress-management, dissociation-focused (grounding), and systemic components:

1. **Engagement & psychoeducation** — collaborative formulation; validate her distress; introduce the stress–body–dissociation link respectfully alongside her existing belief framework.
2. **Arousal reduction & stabilisation** — diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscular relaxation, sleep hygiene; grounding techniques (5-4-3-2-1, orienting) for early episode signs.
3. **Self-monitoring** — diary of episodes, triggers, emotions, and consequences (to make secondary-gain and trigger patterns explicit and collaborative).
4. **Cognitive work** — identify and restructure self-blame, invalidation beliefs, and rigid role/should beliefs.
5. **Affect regulation & assertiveness training** — naming and safely expressing anger and needs; graded assertiveness with in-laws and husband; problem-solving.
6. **Systemic/family sessions** — psychoeducation for the husband/family; reduce reinforcement of symptoms; increase adaptive attention/validation; renegotiate roles and workload.
7. **Graded exposure/behavioural experiments** — as appropriate, to avoided cues (e.g., the "first house") once coping skills are established.
8. **Relapse prevention & review** — consolidate skills, coordinate with psychiatry for pharmacological support of anxiety/depression if indicated.

*Recommended: liaison with psychiatry for consideration of adjunctive pharmacotherapy for the severe anxiety and moderate depression; monitor with repeat DES/BAI/BDRS.*

## 16. GUIDANCE — HOW TO PRESENT THIS AS A CASE CONFERENCE (M.Phil. Clinical Psychology)

**A. Structure your presentation in this order (mirrors this report):**
1. Identifying data & informant (keep confidential — use initials "MS").
2. Chief complaints (verbatim, with duration).
3. HOPI (onset, course, progress; the 3 P's).
4. Family, personal, premorbid history (highlight the formulation-relevant threads: control, non-consensual marriage, in-law conflict, neglect, religiosity).
5. MSE.
6. Assessments — **state the rationale before the score**, then the interpretation (this is what examiners look for).
7. Diagnostic formulation → provisional diagnosis (ICD-10 F44.3) → points in favour → differentials ruled out.
8. Case conceptualisation (the 4 P's diagram is very effective on a slide).
9. Goals → therapy plan → session notes.

**B. Tips that impress a panel:**
- **Lead with a one-line case summary**: *"30-yr-old married female, 8th-grade educated housewife, with a 2-year episodic history of trance-possession phenomena, provisionally F44.3, on a background of chronic interpersonal stress and emotional inhibition."*
- **Always give rationale for every test** — never just report scores. Panels reward hypothesis-driven testing.
- **Show the biopsychosocial 4-P grid** as a single slide; this demonstrates formulation skill.
- **Be explicit about differentials** (psychosis, organic, substance, mood) and how you ruled each out with data (BPRS, CT/MRI, negative history).
- **Address culture directly** — trance-possession must be framed within cultural context; discuss the faith-healing explanatory model non-judgmentally. Panels value cultural formulation.
- For the **TAT**, present it as: task comprehension → recurring hero → dominant needs (affiliation, nurturance, succorance; inward/over-controlled aggression) → environmental press (interpersonal, rejecting) → conflicts/defences → ego strength — then the integrated summary. Link each finding back to the formulation.
- **Anticipate questions:** severity/scoring of DES; why not conversion vs. dissociative; risk assessment; role of secondary gain; family involvement; evidence base for your therapy plan.
- **Slides:** ~12–15 slides; one concept per slide; verbatim complaints in the patient's language add authenticity; end with formulation diagram, goals, and a short outcome-monitoring plan.
- **Ethics:** state consent for presentation, anonymisation, and supervisor oversight up front.

**C. How to "teach yourself" the therapy delivery (quick primer):**
- Master the **formulation first** — every intervention should trace back to a P (predisposing/precipitating/perpetuating).
- For dissociation specifically: **safety + arousal reduction + grounding come before** any exploratory/insight work.
- Use **collaborative empiricism** (CBT) — the diary/behavioural experiments generate the patient's own evidence.
- For trance-possession, **do not confront the belief head-on**; build a bridge from her model ("stress can affect the body") and let grounding successes create doubt in the possession attribution.
- **Involve the family** — systemic maintenance factors (secondary gain) often decide the outcome.
- Track outcomes with repeat DES/BAI/BDRS to demonstrate change.
