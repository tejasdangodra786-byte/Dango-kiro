1 Overview & the mind–heart link

Before the psychology, a quick map. The cardiovascular system (CVS) is simply the heart and the network of tubes (blood vessels) that carry blood around the body. When this system goes wrong — high blood pressure, a heart attack, blocked heart arteries — it is never only a plumbing problem. How a person thinks, feels, stresses and lives helps decide who gets heart disease, how severe it becomes, and how well they recover. That two-way traffic between the mind and the heart is what this whole unit is about.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Give a one-line picture of the system — say in plain words what the heart and blood vessels do (carry oxygen and food to every cell).
  2. Name the key conditions simply — what hypertension, coronary heart disease, angina, heart attack (MI), bypass surgery (CABG), arrhythmia and stroke actually are, one or two glossed lines each. Do not turn this into a medical chapter.
  3. Make the real point — heart disease is a biopsychosocial condition: body + mind + social world together.
  4. Say why psychology matters — the mind affects the heart directly (stress hormones) and indirectly (habits), and the heart event then affects the mind (fear, low mood). This two-way link sets up every later topic.
  5. Close with the frame — psychological help is always an add-on to medical care, never a replacement.
Characterisations by standard texts

definition Cardiovascular (heart) diseases are "a class of diseases that affect the heart or the blood vessels… any disease that affects the cardiovascular system." [ICD-10 classification of diseases, World Health Organization, 1994]

risk Beyond the classic physical risks (high blood pressure, diabetes, poor diet, inactivity, smoking), "stress and depression are the psychological factors that have also been added to the list more recently." [Rozanski, Blumenthal & Kaplan, 1999, Circulation]

Detailed notes

1. What the system does — one minute of biology, then on to the mind

The cardiovascular system (also called the circulatory system — the heart plus all the blood vessels) is a delivery network. The myocardium (the heart muscle) pumps blood so that oxygen and nutrients reach every cell and waste is carried away. The heart has four chambers (two atria on top, two ventricles below). One loop (the pulmonary circulation (the short loop to the lungs)) picks up oxygen; the other (the systemic circulation (the long loop to the rest of the body)) delivers it. The heart feeds itself through its own small arteries, the coronary arteries (the vessels that supply the heart muscle with blood) — and most serious heart disease is really a problem of those arteries. That is all the anatomy you need; now the psychology.

2. The key conditions in plain words

You will meet these names again and again, so here is each one in a glossed line or two. These are plain-language pictures, not a medical syllabus.

The cardiovascular conditions this unit keeps referring to
Name (and what it stands for)In the simplest words
Hypertension (HTN) — "high blood pressure"The blood pushes on the artery walls too hard, for too long (persistently at or above 140/90 mmHg). The heart has to work harder than it should. Mostly there is no single obvious cause (called "primary" or "essential").
Coronary heart disease (CHD) / coronary artery diseaseThe heart's own arteries get narrowed by fatty deposits (a process called atherosclerosis (hardening and furring-up of the arteries)), so the heart muscle gets less blood.
Angina (angina pectoris)Chest pain that comes when the narrowed arteries cannot deliver enough blood — a warning signal, especially on effort or stress.
Myocardial infarction (MI) — a "heart attack"An artery is completely blocked, so a patch of heart muscle is starved of blood and dies. ("Myocardial infarction" literally means death of heart-muscle tissue.)
CABG — coronary artery bypass graft (said "cabbage")Open-heart surgery that takes a healthy vessel and sews it in to bypass a blocked coronary artery, making a new path for blood.
ArrhythmiaAn irregular heartbeat — too fast, too slow, or uneven. Many are harmless; some are dangerous.
Heart failureNot the heart stopping, but the heart's pump growing weak, so the body is short of oxygen and fluid builds up.
StrokeBlood supply to part of the brain is cut off (often a downstream result of high blood pressure or blocked arteries).

Other procedures you may hear named: PCI / angioplasty (a non-surgical way to reopen a narrowed artery with a tiny balloon or a mesh "stent"), pacemaker / ICD (small implanted devices that steady a dangerous heartbeat), and heart transplant (replacing a failing heart in end-stage disease). Each of these is a stressful life event for the patient — which is exactly where psychology comes in.

3. Heart disease is a biopsychosocial condition

The old "biomedical" view treated a heart attack as a pure plumbing fault. The modern view is biopsychosocial (illness arises from body + mind + social world working together). Health psychologists use this guide to see wellness as the product not only of biological processes (such as hormones) but also of mental processes — a person's attitude to health and how they cope with stress — and of social factors such as income, culture and support. On this view, many of the "standard" heart risk factors (smoking, diet, inactivity) are really behaviours, and genuinely psychosocial risks (stress, hostility, depression, isolation) sit right beside them.

4. Why psychology matters to the heart — the two pathways

Psychological factors reach the heart by two routes, and keeping them apart makes every later topic clearer.

  • Direct (bodily) route: the mind acts straight on the body. Stress triggers the release of the hormone cortisol (a stress hormone) and switches the body into "alarm mode"; this raises resting heart rate and blood pressure, lowers heart rate variability (the healthy beat-to-beat flexibility of the heart), and can nudge the heart toward dangerous rhythms. Psychological distress has been shown to do exactly this.
  • Indirect (behavioural) route: the mind acts through choices — whether a person smokes, what they eat, whether they exercise, whether they take their tablets. A low mood or a sense of "losing control" can quietly sabotage all of these.

And the loop runs both ways: once heart disease strikes, it frightens and depresses the patient, which then feeds back onto the heart. Breaking that loop with psychological care is the job of Topic 5.

Common confusion

"Cardiac arrest" and "heart attack (MI)" are not the same. A heart attack is a blocked-artery problem where muscle dies; a cardiac arrest is an electrical problem where the heart suddenly stops pumping. A heart attack can trigger a cardiac arrest, but they are different events — keep the words apart in an answer.

2 Psychosocial, personality & lifestyle / health-practice issues

This is the heart of the "why" question: which psychological things raise the risk of heart disease? The answer comes in three bundles. Psychosocial factors — stress, work strain, poverty, loneliness. Personality — the famous Type A pattern and its "toxic core", hostility. And lifestyle / health practices — smoking, diet, inactivity, drinking, obesity and whether people stick to their treatment. All of these are, at bottom, about how people feel, relate and behave — which is why they belong to psychology, not just cardiology.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Open with the three bundles — psychosocial, personality, lifestyle — and say they act alongside the physical risks, with real evidence behind them.
  2. Do psychosocial first — depression, stress (acute and work-related), social isolation / low support, and low socioeconomic status. Give the striking numbers.
  3. Then personality — define Type A clearly, list its parts, single out hostility as the dangerous core, give the evidence and the criticisms; mention the "distressed" / Type D idea briefly.
  4. Then lifestyle & health practices — smoking, diet, inactivity, alcohol, obesity, adherence; stress that these are modifiable behaviours and that thoughts and feelings drive them.
  5. Close — these are the levers that psychological treatment (Topic 5) will later pull.
Characterisations by standard texts

Type A Type A behaviour was defined as "an action–emotion complex which is exhibited by those individuals who are engaged in relatively chronic struggle to obtain an unlimited number of poorly defined things from their environment in the shortest period of time and, if necessary, against the opposing efforts of other things or persons in this same environment." [Rosenman and colleagues, 1975; the Type A pattern was first described by Friedman & Rosenman, 1959, JAMA]

hostility A hostile person is described as "one who has little confidence in his fellow men… sees people as dishonest, unsocial, immoral, ugly and mean, and believes that they should suffer for their sins." [as summarised in the behavioural-medicine teaching literature]

Detailed notes

1. Psychosocial risk factors

These are factors from a person's mind and social world. The core claim is strong: psychological factors — depression, stress, anxiety and hostility — influence the development, the clinical expression and the prognosis (likely outcome) of heart disease. So clinicians are urged to spot clinically significant distress, both in hospital and after discharge, to find the patients who need psychological help on top of standard cardiac rehabilitation.

(a) Depression — the single most studied psychosocial risk

  • Depression is strikingly common in heart patients. While roughly 20% of the general population has a depressive episode in life, the figure rises to about 50% among people with heart disease.
  • It works both ways. In healthy people, clinical depression roughly doubles the chance of later developing coronary artery disease or having a heart attack (a relative risk around 1.5–2.0 for a first MI). And in people who already have heart disease, depression raises the risk of future bad cardiac events and death roughly three- to four-fold.
  • After a cardiac event it is almost expected: between 35% and 45% of patients after an MI or after CABG surgery have some level of depression, from a few symptoms up to major depressive disorder. Encouragingly, some of this "cardiac depression" lifts on its own within a few months of the event.
  • The bodily bridge most often named is autonomic dysfunction (the automatic nervous system that controls heart rate losing its healthy balance). [Ferketich et al., 2000; Glassman & Shapiro, 1998; Lett et al., 2004; Carney et al., 2001]

(b) Social isolation & low social support

  • Social support (the practical, informational and emotional help a person gets from family, friends and community) protects the heart; its absence harms it. Chronic isolation, low support and lack of social/economic resources all raise the risk of coronary artery disease.
  • Early studies counted the size of a person's social network; later work looked at its quality (how much emotional support is felt). A small network roughly doubles-to-triples the chance of developing heart disease, and low perceived emotional support raises the risk of future cardiac events more than three-fold. [Shumaker et al., 1994; Cohen & Wills, 1985; Blazer, 1982]

(c) Socioeconomic status (SES) — poverty gets into the arteries

  • Within rich countries, heart disease and death are highest among the poorest — measured by education, income or occupation. This was first blamed on worse medical care and more smoking, but those explain only part of it.
  • Low-SES men were more likely to face isolation and life stress and had a death rate about twice that of better-educated peers; low SES also clusters with more high-risk behaviours and more psychosocial risk. [Ruberman, Weinblatt, Goldberg & Chaudhary, 1984; Winkleby et al., 1990; Barefoot et al., 1991]

(d) Stress — acute triggers and chronic work strain

  • Acute stress and anger can act as triggers. Sudden shocks — the death of a spouse, natural disasters, personal trauma — cluster in the hours and days before sudden cardiac deaths. Proposed bodily mechanisms: narrowing (poor dilation) of coronary vessels, drops in plasma volume, and increased blood-clotting stickiness — all of which can tip the balance into cardiac ischaemia (the heart muscle starved of blood) or dangerous rhythms.
  • Chronic work stress is the most studied long-term stressor. The classic model is the job demand–control model (Karasek: jobs that pile on heavy demands while giving the worker little control or say are the most harmful) — high demand + low control (low "decision latitude") predicts more coronary disease. A rival model, the effort–reward imbalance model (Siegrist: high effort that is poorly rewarded is toxic), linked demand–reward imbalance to about a 2.15-fold rise in new coronary disease. [Krantz et al., 1996; Karasek & Theorell, 1990; Siegrist et al., 1990; Lown, 1987]

2. Personality — the Type A behaviour pattern

Here is the famous one. The Type A behaviour pattern (a hard-driving, time-pressured, competitive, easily-angered style of living) was described by cardiologists Friedman and Rosenman. It is an "action–emotion complex" — a bundle of behaviour and feeling, not just a mood.

  • The core features: time urgency and impatience (always racing the clock), competitive striving for achievement, aggressiveness, and easily aroused hostility.
  • The claim: at its height, Type A was called an independent risk factor for coronary disease "equivalent in power to smoking or hypertension," and its link to the severity of atherosclerosis (furring-up of the arteries) held even after adjusting for standard risks.
  • Not just "workaholism": Type A is more than being busy or job-involved — that is a common confusion. Its dangerous ingredient turned out to be narrower than the whole pattern.

Hostility — the "toxic core"

As research matured, attention narrowed from the whole Type A pattern down to one component: hostility (a settled attitude of mistrust, cynicism and readiness to anger). Hostility has received particular attention as a predictor of coronary heart disease. It is usually split into three parts:

  • Cynical thoughts — a mistrustful belief that other people are selfish and mean.
  • Angry feelings — the emotional heat.
  • Aggressive behaviour — acting the anger out.

In hostile people the autonomic (automatic nervous-system) response to social stress is altered — a shift toward sympathetic ("fight") dominance and reduced calming vagal (the "rest-and-digest" nerve that slows the heart) tone — which is the proposed bridge to artery disease.

The "distressed" personality (Type D)

A newer idea: there is good evidence that depression, anxiety and pathological anger — grouped together as negative affectivity (a general tendency to feel bad emotions) — raise the risk of cardiac events in people who already have coronary heart disease. When this negative affectivity is combined with social inhibition (holding those feelings in, not sharing them with others), it forms a lasting "distressed personality type" that predicts cardiac events over and above the standard medical risks. (In the wider literature this combination is often labelled the Type D — "distressed" — personality; the teaching material presents it under the "distressed personality" description above.) Psychological distress of this kind has been shown to raise resting heart rate and blood pressure, lower heart rate variability, and increase dangerous rhythms and ischaemia (blood-starvation of the heart muscle).

Anxiety deserves its own line. Generalised anxiety, pervasive fear, phobias and post-traumatic reactions are common in people who have faced the diagnosis and complications of heart disease. Importantly, chronic low-grade anxiety (as in generalised anxiety disorder) may act on the heart differently from brief, intense anxiety (as in panic). High phobic anxiety in particular has been linked to raised risk of sudden death, thought to work through reduced vagal "braking" on the heart.

3. Lifestyle & health practices — the modifiable behaviours

People often think heart disease is only about physical actions — not exercising, eating badly, smoking, drinking too much. But the teaching stresses something more psychological: attitudes, emotions and thoughts are equally important. Thought patterns can speed the onset of heart disease and block a person from taking concrete steps toward health.

  • The behaviours themselves: smoking, a fatty/high-salt diet, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol, and obesity are the classic modifiable risks. (Obesity matters especially for high blood pressure — see Topic 3 and 5.)
  • Why they are psychological: giving up a cigarette, making time to exercise, or dropping a favourite food can feel like a loss of control over one's life. A quick cigarette or a cheat on the diet satisfies an immediate urge but defeats the long-term goal — the classic battle of short-term reward versus long-term health.
  • Change is a skill, not a wish: building new habits "takes training" — it does not happen by willpower alone, which is exactly why behaviour-change methods (Topic 5) are needed.
  • Two routes again: psychological factors harm or protect health directly (e.g. stress releasing cortisol) and indirectly (through the behavioural choices above). The health psychologist reads all of this through the biopsychosocial lens, adding culture, ethnicity and SES to the picture when planning treatment.

The upside: cultivating a healthy lifestyle can cut the risk of heart disease or help manage it — even when some risk comes from things you cannot change (sex, family history, age).

3 Psychobehavioural responses & coping with illness and functional loss

A heart event is not only something that happens to the body — it is something that happens to a person. This topic is about the inner side of being a cardiac patient: the feelings that flood in (fear, anxiety, low mood, denial), the coping strategies people reach for, the painful adjustment to a smaller, more restricted life, and the specific psychological aftermath of a heart attack (MI), of bypass surgery (CABG) and of living with hypertension. How well a person copes shapes their recovery just as surely as the physical damage does.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Start with the emotions — the normal flood of fear, anxiety, depression, anger and denial after a cardiac event.
  2. Explain coping — problem-focused vs emotion-focused coping, and how "defences" such as denial can help early but hurt later.
  3. Trace the journey — acute event → hospital/ICU → going home → recovery → rehabilitation, naming the psychological task at each stage.
  4. Cover functional loss — adjusting to lifestyle restriction, giving up old powers, and the question of return to work and activity.
  5. Zoom into the specifics — the psychological issues that follow MI, follow CABG, and shadow hypertension (the "silent" condition).
  6. Close — good adjustment means realistic insight and accepting the new situation, which is where therapy (Topic 5) helps.

Detailed notes

1. The emotional responses — what patients feel

A cardiac event is existential: it is a brush with death, so the emotions it stirs are big ones. The teaching lists them clearly.

  • Fear and anxiety are the front-line reactions. Anxiety here "originates in one's own body" and is "generated by fear for life" — it is a response to a real biological threat, not an over-reaction. Generalised anxiety, pervasive fear, specific phobias and post-traumatic reactions are all "commonplace" in people who have faced a serious cardiac diagnosis. Agoraphobia (fear of situations where escape or help feels hard) can take hold, much as it does after panic attacks.
  • Depression and grief follow close behind — grief for the healthy body and the life that has been lost. (Recall from Topic 2 that 35–45% of post-MI and post-CABG patients show some depression.)
  • Anger — "why me?" — and the fear, anger, depression and grief triggered by separation and difficult relationships may be blocked from direct expression, so they leak out sideways and need a therapist's help to surface safely.

2. Coping styles & defence mechanisms

Coping (the mental and practical efforts a person makes to manage a threat) comes in two broad flavours, and cardiac patients use both.

  • Problem-focused coping — acting on the problem itself: taking the tablets, turning up to rehabilitation, changing the diet.
  • Emotion-focused coping — managing the feelings the problem stirs up: seeking comfort, reframing, relaxation.

The teaching puts special weight on defence mechanisms (automatic mental strategies that protect us from unbearable feelings), because they can be double-edged:

  • "Primitive" defences — chiefly denial and regression (slipping back to a more dependent, childlike state) — can actually be useful at the very start of a cardiac illness, protecting the patient from being overwhelmed, and so they "deserve support" early on. But in time they must be gently let go, because clinging to them works against rehabilitation.
  • "Mature" defences — developed suppression (choosing to set a worry aside for now), humour and sublimation (channelling the feeling into something constructive) — support good adjustment and grow stronger with individual capacity.
  • So therapeutic management of defences — supporting denial early, then helping the patient move past it — is treated as an important factor in good adaptation to a cardiac event.

3. The patient's journey & adjusting to functional loss

Adjustment is a process over time, not a single moment (see Figure 3.1). Across the acute event, the hospital/ICU stay, the return home and rehabilitation, two psychological threads run through:

  • Loss of control & restriction. Patients may "sense a loss of control over their life" once living revolves around medication, exercise slots and forbidden foods. This is the lived experience of functional loss (no longer being able to do the things one used to do) — not being able to lift, climb, work or be intimate as before.
  • Giving up old "powers". The teaching describes successful adaptation beautifully: the patient "develops a realistic insight into the limitations, gives up the powers he had had before the illness, and adjusts to the actual situation." Good coping is not pretending nothing has changed — it is seeing the new reality clearly and making peace with it.

Because the partner also "suffers considerable distress" that can shape the patient's recovery, adjustment is a family task, not a solo one.

4. Specific issues after MI and after CABG

Psychological aftermath of the two big cardiac events
After a heart attack (MI)After bypass surgery (CABG)
A sudden, frightening brush with death. High rates of depression and anxiety (35–45% show some depression). Fear of a second attack can make patients avoid effort, sex and activity. Much of the depression may remit on its own within a few months — but the patients who are psychologically predisposed to poor adjustment need to be identified early. A planned but major operation. Patients face post-operative mood changes (low mood, tearfulness), anxiety about the surgery and its results, and worries about recovery and functional loss. Like MI survivors, a large share (again around 35–45% in the post-CABG group) experience some depression. Partners too carry distress through the convalescence.

For both groups the teaching lands on one point: the outcome after a coronary event depends "not only on the physical characteristics of the event, but also on the type of person they are and how well they adjust psychologically." Mood states (depression, anxiety) can shift with treatment; personality is far more stable and harder to change — a crucial note for planning realistic help.

5. Living with hypertension — the "silent" condition

Hypertension poses a different psychological problem from MI or CABG. It is usually symptomless — the person feels perfectly well — yet they are asked to take daily tablets, cut salt, lose weight and attend check-ups for a danger they cannot feel. This mismatch is a classic cause of poor adherence (sticking to the treatment plan): the immediate "cost" (pills, restrictions, loss of favourite foods) is felt now, while the benefit (avoiding a future stroke or heart attack) is invisible and far off. Understanding this gap is central to helping hypertensive patients psychologically. (Behavioural methods such as weight loss, salt reduction, relaxation and biofeedback are covered in Topic 5.)

Pitfall to avoid in an answer

Do not treat denial as simply "bad". The nuance the examiner wants is that early denial can be protective and worth supporting at first, and only becomes harmful if it persists and stops the patient engaging with rehabilitation. Showing you grasp the timing is the mark.

Topic 3 at a glance
  • Emotions: fear, anxiety (existential — "fear for life"), depression, grief, anger, denial.
  • Coping: problem-focused vs emotion-focused; defences — denial/regression help early but must be released; suppression, humour, sublimation are mature.
  • Journey: acute event → hospital/ICU → home recovery → rehabilitation; each stage has its own task.
  • Functional loss: good adjustment = realistic insight + "giving up old powers" + adjusting to the new situation; it is a family task.
  • MI & CABG: high depression/anxiety, fear of recurrence, return-to-work worries; outcome depends on the person and their adjustment, not just the damage.
  • Hypertension: "silent" → adherence is the key psychological challenge.

4 Quality of life & well-being in cardiac patients

Keeping a heart patient alive is only half the goal. The other half is helping them live a life that feels worth living — able to work, love, move, sleep and enjoy things. That is what quality of life and well-being mean, and in modern cardiac care they are treated as real outcomes, measured and aimed at, not soft extras. The biggest threats to a cardiac patient's quality of life are the very things we met earlier: depression and anxiety — which also predict a worse cardiac future.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define the two terms simply — what quality of life (QoL) means and what well-being means, in plain words.
  2. List the domains affected — physical, emotional, social, occupational and sexual life.
  3. Name the biggest threats — depression and anxiety, which lower QoL and worsen the cardiac outcome.
  4. Bring in social support — being connected lifts well-being; isolation drags it down.
  5. Close — QoL and well-being are key goals and outcomes of cardiac care, which is why psychology is built into it.

Note: this topic is framed from the syllabus and the standard behavioural-medicine/health-psychology handbooks. It is written in plain language and does not put words into the mouth of any single source.

Detailed notes

1. What quality of life and well-being mean

  • Quality of life (QoL — how good and satisfying a person's life feels to them, across body, mind and social life) is a subjective, whole-person idea. In health care we usually mean health-related quality of life (the part of QoL that illness and its treatment actually touch) — energy, freedom from pain, being able to do daily tasks, mood, and social and working life.
  • Well-being (a settled sense of feeling good and functioning well — emotionally, socially and in daily life) is the broader felt state of being "all right in oneself". It includes both feeling good (positive emotion, life satisfaction) and functioning well (coping, relationships, purpose).
  • The key point for an answer: these are patient-defined. Two people with the same test results can have very different quality of life, because QoL is about the person's own experience, not just the numbers on a chart.

2. The domains affected in heart disease

Heart disease and its treatment reach into several areas of life at once (Figure 4.1):

  • Physical — tiredness, breathlessness, chest pain or fear of it, reduced stamina, and the restrictions of a careful cardiac lifestyle.
  • Emotional — the fear, anxiety, low mood and grief described in Topic 3; worry about dying or about a repeat event.
  • Social — changed roles in the family, dependence on others, withdrawal from friends, strain on the partner.
  • Occupational — whether and when to return to work; loss of earnings, identity and routine (a major worry after MI and CABG).
  • Sexual & intimacy — fear that sex is dangerous, loss of confidence, and effects of illness or medication; often unspoken but important to QoL.

3. Depression and anxiety — the biggest threats to QoL and to the heart

Of everything that can drag down a cardiac patient's quality of life, depression and anxiety are the heaviest — and they are the hinge that connects this topic to the rest of the unit.

  • They directly worsen quality of life: a depressed or anxious patient enjoys less, does less, sleeps worse and withdraws more, whatever the state of their arteries.
  • They also predict a worse cardiac outcome. As Topic 2 showed, depression raises the risk of future cardiac events and death roughly three- to four-fold in people who already have heart disease, and high phobic anxiety is linked to raised risk of sudden death. So mood is not a "side issue" — it is part of the prognosis.
  • This two-for-one is exactly why treating depression and anxiety (Topic 5) is argued to improve both how the patient feels and, potentially, how the heart fares.

4. Social support & well-being

  • Connection lifts well-being. The same social support that protects against getting heart disease (Topic 2) also protects quality of life after it — emotional support cushions distress, practical support eases the burdens of treatment, and feeling understood reduces the sense of isolation.
  • Isolation drags it down. Loneliness, a small network and low perceived support leave the patient facing a frightening illness alone, worsening mood and well-being and (as we saw) cardiac risk itself.
  • This is why support-building interventions and group and family approaches (Topic 5) are valued: they repair the social fabric that well-being rests on.

5. Quality of life as a goal and outcome of cardiac care

Pulling it together: modern cardiac care is judged not only by survival and symptom control but by quality of life and well-being as outcomes in their own right. One explicit aim of cardiac rehabilitation (Topic 5) is to "reintegrate heart disease patients into successful functional status in their families and in society" — which is quality of life, stated as a treatment goal. Because QoL is partly psychological, psychological care is a built-in part of good cardiac treatment, not an optional extra.

Topic 4 at a glance
  • QoL = how good and satisfying life feels (patient-defined); well-being = feeling good + functioning well.
  • Domains: physical, emotional, social, occupational, sexual — all can be hit at once.
  • Depression & anxiety are the biggest threats — they lower QoL and predict worse cardiac outcome.
  • Social support raises well-being; isolation lowers it.
  • QoL and well-being are genuine goals and outcomes of cardiac care — which is why psychology belongs in it.

5 Empirically-proven methods of psychological management of CVS disease

This is the pay-off of the whole unit: the evidence-based toolkit for helping the heart through the mind. Every method here has been tested in real trials, and every one is an add-on to medical cardiac care, never a substitute for it. The toolkit runs from cardiac rehabilitation and stress management through Type A / hostility modification and social-support programmes to lifestyle change, CBT and other therapies for mood, and biofeedback and relaxation for blood pressure. We also keep the evidence honest — including where results have been modest or disappointing.

Detailed notes

The golden rule — read this before anything else

Every method below is an adjunct — an add-on — to proper medical cardiac care (drugs, surgery, PCI). Psychological treatment supports and complements medicine; it never replaces it. The evidence is genuinely positive, but short-term psychological support works best "in conjunction with" coronary risk-behaviour change and exercise.

1. Cardiac rehabilitation — the umbrella programme

Cardiac rehabilitation (a structured programme of exercise, education, counselling and risk-factor change after a cardiac event) is the big container that holds most of the other methods. Its aim is to reverse the physical and psychological damage of a cardiac event. Its stated goals read as a checklist:

  • Curtail the pathophysiological and psychosocial effects of heart disease.
  • Limit the risk of re-infarction (a second attack) or sudden death.
  • Relieve cardiac symptoms.
  • Retard or reverse atherosclerosis through exercise training, education, counselling and risk-factor change.
  • Reintegrate patients into successful functional status in their families and society (the quality-of-life goal of Topic 4).

Candidates include the roughly 1 million MI survivors and 300,000+ bypass-surgery patients each year (US figures) — a huge population for whom the psychological component (needs assessment, stress management, counselling) is as much a part of rehabilitation as the treadmill.

2. Stress management & relaxation training

Because stress and arousal feed heart disease (Topic 2), lowering them is a core tool. Psychologists are urged to deliver generic stress management and relaxation techniques for all patients, stepping up to specific therapies for those in severe distress.

  • Relaxation training (teaching the body to switch out of "alarm mode" on purpose), including progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) (tensing then releasing muscle groups one by one until the whole body is calm), slowed breathing, meditation and yoga, is used to decrease physiological arousal — the raised heart rate and blood pressure that distress produces.
  • In the lifestyle and Type A programmes below, stress management and relaxation appear as key ingredients, which is part of why those programmes worked.

3. Modifying Type A behaviour & hostility

Since Type A — and especially its hostility core — is a risk factor (Topic 2), can it be changed? The evidence says yes, in motivated people.

  • The landmark trial — the Recurrent Coronary Prevention Project (RCPP). This was the first and most ambitious trial built solely to test whether Type A behaviour can be modified and whether that cuts cardiac risk. Over 1,000 post-MI patients were split into three groups: cardiology counselling alone, cardiology counselling + Type A behaviour modification, or no-treatment control. The Type A arm got drills to change hurried, angry, aggressive habits, discussion of the beliefs behind them, and relaxation / stress-reduction training. After 4.5 years, the Type A counselling group showed a bigger drop in Type A behaviour and a significantly lower rate of recurrent MI than the other two groups. [Friedman et al., 1986]
  • Honest caveat. Later analysis suggested some of that benefit may owe to more treatment contact and increased social support rather than Type A change alone — a good example of keeping the evidence honest. [Mendes de Leon, Powell & Kaplan, 1991]
  • Hostility reduction. A separate trial randomised highly hostile male coronary patients to a hostility-reduction programme or an information control; the intervention group were less hostile at follow-up and had significantly lower diastolic blood pressure. The anger component can be modified through practice: patients learn to notice their anger and express it "in a soft, slow voice," or use other coping and relaxation strategies to stop anger escalating into fury. [Gidron, Davidson & Bata, 1999]

4. Social-support & life-stress monitoring programmes

Since low support and life stress raise risk (Topic 2), adding support and watching stress is a logical treatment.

  • The Ischemic Heart Disease Life Stress Monitoring Program. Post-MI patients were either given life-stress monitoring + intervention or routine care. The treatment group were phoned monthly and rated 20 distress symptoms (insomnia, low mood, etc.); if stress crossed a set level, a nurse made a home visit to help the patient understand and cope with the stressor, with counselling, heart-disease education and emotional support. Over one year there was a 50% reduction in cardiac deaths, and over seven years fewer repeat MIs in the treated group. Its success is credited largely to the social and emotional support that eased depression and distress, lowering harmful arousal. [Frasure-Smith & Prince, 1987, 1989]

5. Long-term lifestyle change & exercise

  • The Lifestyle Heart Trial. One of the most important intervention studies, it tested whether coronary patients could make and keep big lifestyle changes. Patients with moderate-to-severe coronary disease were randomised to an intensive lifestyle change group or usual care. The intensive package had four parts: a very-low-fat vegetarian diet; stress-management training and group support (including yoga and meditation); smoking cessation; and moderate aerobic exercise. After one year the intervention group showed a 37% fall in LDL ("bad") cholesterol, a 91% fall in angina episodes, and even slight regression (shrinking) of artery blockages — while controls got worse. Strikingly, the more a patient stuck to the programme, the more their disease improved, showing the power of adherence. [Ornish et al., 1990]

6. Psychotherapy & therapies for comorbid anxiety and depression

Because depression and anxiety are both common and dangerous in heart patients (Topics 2 & 4), treating them is central. The teaching describes a whole family of talking therapies.

  • Supportive psychotherapy. The primary goal is to make patients "more psychologically comfortable" by lowering stress and creating a safe relationship where feelings can be expressed and the patient feels accepted — which supports the realistic adjustment described in Topic 3.
  • Cognitive-behaviour therapy (CBT). CBT (a therapy that works on unhelpful thoughts and behaviours together) is called "highly effective" for anxiety and depression. Its cognitive side helps patients see how their thoughts feed anxiety and correct irrational negative thinking; its behavioural side uses relaxation, deep breathing and graded, safe exposure to feared situations.
  • Behavioural therapy. Techniques to reduce or stop unwanted behaviours — for example, training in relaxation and deep breathing to counter the agitation and rapid shallow breathing of anxiety.
  • Interpersonal therapy (IPT). Focuses on the patient's relationships — with family, friends, co-workers — to improve communication and self-esteem over 3–4 months; it works well for depression tied to loss and grief, relationship conflict, major life events, isolation or role change. For cardiac depression it targets a "focal area of interpersonal difficulty" — role transitions, grief or role disputes. Focal brief psychotherapy is offered as a logical treatment where depression arises in reaction to the cardiac disease.
  • Group & family therapy. In rehabilitation settings, education and family groups let patients and relatives share experiences, ease isolation and stigma, and learn from each other with a special empathy born of the shared ordeal. Offering help to partners matters, since their distress shapes the patient's recovery.
  • Timing & targeting. Interventions should start as soon after the cardiac event as possible, and effort should go to identifying the patients psychologically predisposed to poor adjustment so they (and their partners) get targeted help.

7. Managing hypertension — behavioural methods & biofeedback (with an honest verdict)

  • Weight loss is an important behavioural method for high blood pressure: studies showed that weight loss even without salt restriction significantly lowered blood pressure. Salt (sodium) reduction helps in some populations. Both are behaviour-change tasks.
  • Relaxation, meditation and biofeedback (using live signals of the body — e.g. a monitor of muscle tension or heart rate — to learn to calm the body on purpose) have all been tried to lower blood pressure.
  • The honest verdict. This is where we keep the evidence truthful: the teaching concludes that the effects of behavioural stress-reducing techniques — relaxation training, biofeedback and meditation — in lowering blood pressure have been "modest" and, overall, "disappointing." Hypertension can be modified somewhat by behavioural means, but drug treatment is often still necessary. The anger component of hostility, by contrast, can be reduced through training, which usefully cuts cardiac risk in anger-prone patients.

8. A balanced closing verdict

Many environmental, behavioural and physiological variables interact to produce heart disease, and many standard risk factors have strong behavioural components. The identification of psychosocial risks (work stress, hostility, reactivity, depression, isolation, low resources) has led to several promising behavioural and psychosocial interventions for treating and preventing coronary disease in high-risk people. The honest overall picture: psychological methods genuinely help — easing distress, improving quality of life, and in several trials improving cardiac outcomes — while some effects (notably on blood pressure) are modest. They are, in every case, a complement to medical treatment.

Topic 5 at a glance
  • All methods are adjuncts to medical care — support, never replace.
  • Cardiac rehabilitation = the umbrella (curtail, limit, relieve, reverse, reintegrate) with a built-in psychological component.
  • Stress management & relaxation (PMR, breathing, meditation, yoga) lower harmful arousal.
  • Type A / hostility modification: RCPP (fewer recurrent MIs); hostility-reduction (less anger, lower BP); anger can be trained down.
  • Social support / life-stress monitoring (Frasure-Smith & Prince) → ~50% fewer cardiac deaths.
  • Lifestyle change (Ornish) → big falls in cholesterol & angina, with a dose–response to adherence.
  • For mood: CBT, behavioural therapy, IPT, supportive, group & family therapy — start early, target high-risk patients.
  • Hypertension: weight loss & salt help; relaxation/biofeedback effects are honestly modest.
