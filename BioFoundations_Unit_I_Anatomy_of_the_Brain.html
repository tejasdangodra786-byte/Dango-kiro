How to read these notes

These are concise, exam-ready revision notes for Unit I of the Biological Foundations of Behavior paper (M.Phil. Clinical Psychology). They cover the anatomy of the brain topic by topic, from its major sub-divisions and the surface of the cortex to its interior structures, connectivity, blood supply, fluid system and microscopic organization, so a whole 5-mark answer can be revised from a single topic.

What the sections are

  • Each numbered section is one Unit I syllabus topic, written as a 5-mark exam answer in full sentences rather than terse fragments, so you can revise straight from the point and reproduce it under exam conditions.
  • Every topic opens with a one-line summary, carries a flowchart figure that pictures the core idea, gives a short definition box drawn from standard neuroanatomy texts, and closes with a mnemonic that packs the key points into a single memorable word.
  • A short source note and a references list sit at the end; the references name only the books (full title and author) and the online sources that were actually used.

1Major anatomical sub-divisions of the human brain

The human brain develops from a simple neural tube into three primary divisions, forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain, which further form five vesicles whose adult derivatives are the cerebrum, diencephalon, midbrain, pons with cerebellum, and medulla, all set within the central nervous system.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Standard neuroanatomy describes the brain as part of the central nervous system (the brain and spinal cord together), which with the peripheral nerves forms the whole nervous system, and divides it embryologically into forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain. [John Nolte, The Human Brain: An Introduction to Its Functional Anatomy]
  • Standard text Standard descriptions note that the brain forms from five regions, telencephalon, diencephalon, mesencephalon, metencephalon and myelencephalon, arranged from rostral to caudal during development. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Standard text The three primary vesicles are named prosencephalon (the forebrain vesicle), mesencephalon (the midbrain vesicle) and rhombencephalon (the hindbrain vesicle), and each gives rise to defined adult structures. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]

Exam points

  • The brain begins in the embryo as a hollow neural tube, whose front end swells into three primary vesicles, the forebrain, the midbrain and the hindbrain, from which every adult region is ultimately derived. [John Nolte, The Human Brain: An Introduction to Its Functional Anatomy]
  • The forebrain or prosencephalon (the most rostral, front-most brain vesicle) later splits into the telencephalon and diencephalon, making it the source of the cerebrum and of the deep structures around the third ventricle. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The telencephalon becomes the cerebrum (the large, folded cerebral hemispheres), the largest part of the brain, which carries the cortex, the underlying white matter and the basal ganglia within each hemisphere. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The diencephalon forms the central core of the forebrain and gives rise to the thalamus, hypothalamus and epithalamus, the last of which carries the pineal gland and encloses the third ventricle. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The midbrain or mesencephalon (the short middle brain segment) stays undivided, becoming a short segment between the diencephalon and the pons that carries reflex and relay centres. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The hindbrain or rhombencephalon (the most caudal brain vesicle, nearest the spinal cord) divides into the metencephalon and myelencephalon, so it is the source of the pons, the cerebellum and the medulla. [John Nolte, The Human Brain: An Introduction to Its Functional Anatomy]
  • The metencephalon develops into the pons and the cerebellum, placing the great coordinating cerebellum and the bridge-like pons together in the upper hindbrain just above the medulla. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The myelencephalon becomes the medulla oblongata, the lowest part of the brainstem, which continues directly into the spinal cord and houses vital cardiovascular and respiratory centres. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • By function the brain is grouped into the brainstem (midbrain, pons and medulla), the diencephalon, the cerebrum and the cerebellum, which is a convenient adult map laid over the embryonic scheme. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The brainstem is the oldest part of the brain and runs automatic survival functions, and its net-like reticular formation (a diffuse network of neurons through the brainstem core) filters incoming stimuli and governs arousal. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The cerebellum, the 'little brain', sits behind the brainstem and fine-tunes balance and posture, smoothing movement, working on the same side of the body that it helps to control. [Neil R. Carlson, Physiology of Behavior]
  • Anatomical orientation uses paired terms, so rostral means toward the front and caudal toward the tail, dorsal means toward the back and ventral toward the belly, letting any structure be located precisely. [John Nolte, The Human Brain: An Introduction to Its Functional Anatomy]
  • The whole brain is bilaterally symmetrical and weighs roughly 1250 to 1450 grams in adults, sitting protected inside the bony cranium and wrapped in the three meningeal membranes. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The brain and the spinal cord together make up the central nervous system, while the cranial and spinal nerves branching out to the body form the peripheral nervous system that connects it to the periphery. [Neil R. Carlson, Physiology of Behavior]
  • A deep interhemispheric fissure splits the cerebrum into left and right hemispheres, and the two are joined underneath by the corpus callosum so that they can share information. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Historically, Franz Josef Gall tried to localise mental faculties to brain regions and Paul Broca in the 1860s located a speech region, early steps that founded the discipline of modern neuroanatomy. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Clinically the scheme matters, because damage at different levels produces different pictures, so a brainstem lesion threatens vital functions while a cerebral lesion affects thought, movement or sensation instead. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
Sub-divisions at a glance
  • Neural tube → three primary vesicles: forebrain, midbrain, hindbrain.
  • Five secondary vesicles: telencephalon, diencephalon, mesencephalon, metencephalon, myelencephalon.
  • Adult derivatives: cerebrum; thalamus and hypothalamus; midbrain; pons and cerebellum; medulla.
  • Functional map: brainstem, diencephalon, cerebrum, cerebellum; CNS = brain + spinal cord.
  • Orientation: rostral–caudal, dorsal–ventral, medial–lateral locate every structure.
Mnemonic
TDMMM
Telencephalon, Diencephalon, Mesencephalon, Metencephalon and Myelencephalon — the five vesicles from front to back; remember they group under forebrain, midbrain and hindbrain, giving cerebrum, diencephalon, midbrain, pons-plus-cerebellum and medulla.

2Surface anatomy of cortical regions

The surface of the cerebral cortex is thrown into folds and grooves and divided by the central sulcus and lateral fissure into four lobes, frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital, plus the hidden insula, each carrying primary motor, sensory, visual or auditory areas among wide zones of association cortex.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text The cerebral cortex is characterised as the outermost layer of grey matter, a wrinkled sheet of tightly packed neurons responsible for higher processes such as thought, language, decision-making and problem-solving. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Standard text Standard texts divide the cortex into four lobes, frontal, parietal, temporal and occipital, separated by the central sulcus and the lateral fissure, with a fifth lobe, the insula, buried deep within the lateral fissure. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Classical account Hitzig and Fritsch (1870) showed by electrically stimulating the motor cortex of dogs that distinct body muscles map to distinct cortical points, early evidence for the localisation of function. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]

Exam points

  • The cortex is folded to pack a large surface into the skull, so its raised ridges are called gyri (the raised folds of the cortical surface) and its grooves are called sulci (the valleys between the folds), and deeper grooves are termed fissures. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Two landmark grooves organise the surface: the central sulcus runs down and forward dividing frontal from parietal lobe, while the lateral (Sylvian) fissure runs backward separating the temporal lobe below. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The frontal lobe lies in front of the central sulcus and governs voluntary movement and planning, which is why damage here, as in the classic case of Phineas Gage, changes behaviour. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The primary motor cortex sits on the precentral gyrus (the fold just in front of the central sulcus), and its parts are arranged in orderly fashion so that finger, hand and arm controls lie next to one another. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The parietal lobe lies behind the central sulcus and handles touch, pressure, pain and temperature, integrating bodily sensation with spatial awareness of where the body is in its surroundings. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The primary somatosensory cortex occupies the postcentral gyrus (the fold just behind the central sulcus) and is highly adaptable, so skilled users such as Braille readers develop larger fingertip representations here. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The temporal lobe lies below the lateral fissure and processes hearing, language comprehension and memory, receiving auditory input mostly from the opposite ear through its primary auditory cortex. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The primary auditory cortex sits on the upper temporal lobe at Heschl's gyrus (the transverse temporal gyrus that first receives sound) and analyses pitch, loudness and pattern of incoming acoustic information. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The occipital lobe forms the back of the brain and is devoted to vision, with its primary visual cortex around the calcarine sulcus processing colour, shape and movement from the opposite visual field. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Visual input crosses over, so the right visual field is processed in the left hemisphere and the left field in the right, a crossing pattern that recurs for many sensory and motor pathways. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The insula is a fifth lobe tucked deep inside the lateral fissure and is involved in taste, visceral sensation and emotional awareness, hidden from the brain's outer surface. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Most of the cortex is not primary but association cortex (cortex that integrates and interprets rather than simply receiving), which binds and interprets sensory inputs into meaningful experience and action. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Localisation of function means that specific abilities such as language or vision have specific cortical locations, a principle supported first by lesion case studies and now by modern neuroimaging. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Lateralisation means the two hemispheres differ in emphasis, so in most people the left hemisphere leads for language and the right for spatial and holistic processing, though both cooperate closely. [Neil R. Carlson, Physiology of Behavior]
  • Language has two classic surface regions, Broca's area in the frontal lobe for speech production and Wernicke's area in the temporal lobe for comprehension, linked by an association fibre bundle. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The cortex is only a few millimetres thick but its heavy folding greatly enlarges the surface area, and small increases in thickness and folding are associated with greater processing capacity. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Clinically the surface map guides diagnosis, because a stroke or tumour in a given lobe produces predictable deficits, such as weakness from motor-strip damage or visual loss from occipital damage. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
Cortical surface at a glance
  • Folds: gyri (ridges) and sulci (grooves); central sulcus and lateral fissure are the key landmarks.
  • Four lobes plus the hidden insula: frontal (motor), parietal (sensory), temporal (hearing), occipital (vision).
  • Primary areas: precentral (motor), postcentral (somatosensory), Heschl's (auditory), calcarine (visual).
  • Association cortex integrates; Broca (speech) and Wernicke (comprehension) serve language.
  • Localisation + lateralisation: functions have places, and the hemispheres specialise, especially for language.
Mnemonic
FPTO-I
Frontal, Parietal, Temporal and Occipital are the four lobes, plus the Insula hidden in the lateral fissure — pair each with its primary job: Front moves, Parietal feels, Temporal hears, Occipital sees, Insula tastes and senses the viscera.

3Interior structures of cortical and sub-cortical regions

Beneath the cortex lie the white-matter tracts and a set of deep grey-matter structures, the basal ganglia, the thalamus and hypothalamus, the limbic system with its hippocampus and amygdala, the brainstem and cerebellum, all arranged around the fluid-filled ventricles.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Standard texts distinguish grey matter (tissue rich in neuron cell bodies), found in the surface cortex and the deep nuclei, from white matter (tissue made of myelinated axons), the connecting fibres that lie beneath. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Standard text The basal ganglia are characterised as a group of deep forebrain nuclei, the striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus and substantia nigra, which tune voluntary movement. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Basal Ganglia (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • Standard text Standard texts describe the limbic system as a doughnut-shaped ring of structures at the border of the brainstem and cerebrum, including the hippocampus, amygdala and hypothalamus, serving emotion and drive. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]

Exam points

  • Directly under the cortex lies the cerebral white matter, a dense mass of myelinated fibres that carries signals between cortical areas and between the cortex and the deeper and lower centres of the brain. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • The basal ganglia are deep grey-matter nuclei that fine-tune voluntary movement, receiving plans from the cortex, processing them and returning instructions to the cortex by way of the thalamus. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Basal Ganglia (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The main basal-ganglia parts are the striatum (the caudate nucleus and putamen together), the globus pallidus, the subthalamic nucleus and the substantia nigra, whose loss of dopamine cells causes Parkinson's disease. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Basal Ganglia (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The thalamus is a pair of egg-shaped grey masses forming the walls of the third ventricle, and it acts as the brain's great relay station through which nearly all sensory input passes on its way to the cortex. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Because of this gateway role the thalamus is central to sensation, motor control, arousal and memory, so that damage to it can blunt or distort incoming experience. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The hypothalamus sits just below the thalamus and is the master controller of the autonomic and endocrine systems, regulating hunger, thirst, temperature, sleep and the drives for sex and survival. [Neil R. Carlson, Physiology of Behavior]
  • The limbic system is the emotional core of the brain, a border ring that links the cortex with deeper structures and underlies emotion, motivation, memory and drive for food and sex. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The hippocampus (a curved limbic structure deep in the temporal lobe) is essential for forming new long-term memories, consolidating experiences for weeks before they are stored more permanently in the cortex. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The amygdala (an almond-shaped limbic nucleus) processes fear and emotional significance, tagging events with feeling and triggering the body's rapid defensive responses to danger. [Neil R. Carlson, Physiology of Behavior]
  • The brainstem contains the midbrain, pons and medulla, and runs the automatic survival functions of heartbeat, breathing and consciousness, acting as the junction through which most ascending and descending tracts pass. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The midbrain carries the superior and inferior colliculi, which serve as visual and auditory reflex centres, together with the substantia nigra that links functionally to the basal ganglia. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The pons bridges the two cerebellar hemispheres and helps coordinate movement, sleep, waking and dreaming, while the medulla below it controls the force and rate of the heartbeat and of breathing. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The cerebellum behind the brainstem has two folded hemispheres and smooths and coordinates movement, posture and balance, correcting errors so that actions are accurate and well timed. [Neil R. Carlson, Physiology of Behavior]
  • The ventricles (fluid-filled cavities deep within the brain) are an interconnected web of spaces holding cerebrospinal fluid, comprising two lateral ventricles, a third and a fourth linked by narrow channels. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Grey and white matter are arranged in a clear pattern, with grey matter on the surface and in the deep nuclei and the connecting white matter sandwiched between them, a layout repeated through the brain. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The whole interior is modular yet connected, so each deep structure does a specialised job within defined pathways, and damage to one, such as the basal ganglia or hippocampus, produces a characteristic deficit. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Clinically these interior structures matter greatly, since Parkinson's disease and movement disorders each trace to specific deep structures such as the substantia nigra or the hippocampus. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Basal Ganglia (NCBI Bookshelf)]
Interior structures at a glance
  • White matter = connecting fibres beneath the cortex; grey matter = cell bodies in cortex and deep nuclei.
  • Basal ganglia (striatum, globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra) tune movement.
  • Thalamus relays sensation; hypothalamus runs the autonomic and endocrine systems and basic drives.
  • Limbic system (hippocampus, amygdala) drives memory and emotion; brainstem runs vital functions.
  • Cerebellum coordinates movement and balance; ventricles hold the cerebrospinal fluid.
Mnemonic
BLT-HeavenC
Basal ganglia, Limbic system and Thalamus/hypothalamus, with the Hippocampus and amygdala inside the limbic ring, plus the Brainstem and Cerebellum — the deep structures under the cortex, all set around the fluid-filled ventricles.

4Anatomical connectivity among regions

The regions of the brain are wired together by white-matter fibres of three kinds, association fibres that link areas within one hemisphere, commissural fibres such as the corpus callosum that join the two hemispheres, and projection fibres such as the internal capsule that connect the cortex to lower centres.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Standard texts classify cerebral white-matter tracts into three kinds, association, commissural and projection fibres, according to the direction and reach of the connections they make. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Standard text The corpus callosum is characterised as the largest white-matter structure and the major commissure, carrying on the order of two hundred million axons between the two hemispheres. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Standard text Association fibres (fibres linking regions inside one hemisphere) include the arcuate and superior longitudinal fasciculi, the cingulum and the uncinate fasciculus, each joining defined cortical areas. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]

Exam points

  • White matter is made of myelinated axons, called tracts or fasciculi, which carry nerve impulses between grey-matter regions and so coordinate communication across the whole brain. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Association fibres connect different areas within the same hemisphere, allowing one lobe to talk to another, for example linking the visual cortex to the areas that interpret what is seen. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Short association fibres called U-fibres (short arching fibres joining neighbouring gyri) loop between adjacent gyri, while long bundles span whole lobes within a hemisphere. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • The superior longitudinal (arcuate) fasciculus is a key long association bundle that links the frontal lobe with the other lobes, famously joining Broca's and Wernicke's language areas. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Other named association bundles include the cingulum, which runs within the limbic lobe, and the uncinate fasciculus, which hooks the frontal lobe to the anterior temporal lobe. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Commissural fibres cross the midline to connect matching areas in the two hemispheres, so that the left and right sides of the brain can share and integrate their information. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The corpus callosum is the great commissure and the largest white-matter structure in the brain, a broad arched band of around two hundred million fibres joining the hemispheres front to back. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • A smaller commissure, the anterior commissure, links the two temporal lobes, and cutting the callosum, as in split-brain surgery, leaves the hemispheres unable to share information directly. [Neil R. Carlson, Physiology of Behavior]
  • Projection fibres run vertically between the cortex and lower centres, carrying motor commands down to the brainstem and spinal cord and sensory signals up from the thalamus to the cortex. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • The great projection gateway is the internal capsule (a compact band of projection fibres between the basal ganglia), a dense band that funnels most ascending and descending fibres past the basal ganglia and thalamus. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Above the internal capsule the fibres fan out into the corona radiata (the fanned-out crown of projection fibres), which spreads the tracts widely to and from the whole cortical surface. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • The chief descending projection tract is the corticospinal (pyramidal) tract, which carries voluntary motor commands from the motor cortex and crosses to the opposite side in the medulla's pyramidal decussation. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Because of this crossing, the left hemisphere controls the right side of the body and the right hemisphere the left, a decussation that explains why a one-sided stroke weakens the opposite limbs. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Sensory projection fibres carry touch and pain up through the brainstem and thalamus to the cortex, for example the medial lemniscus relaying fine touch and position sense. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Between the forebrain and the cerebellum run great fibre bundles called the cerebellar peduncles, through which the cerebellum receives and returns the signals that coordinate movement. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Together the three fibre classes make the brain a single connected network, not isolated parts, so that complex behaviour depends on intact wiring as much as on intact regions. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Clinically, broken connections cause disconnection syndromes, so cutting the arcuate fasciculus disrupts repetition of speech and damaging the callosum stops the hemispheres from coordinating. [Neil R. Carlson, Physiology of Behavior]
Connectivity at a glance
  • Three fibre classes: association (within a hemisphere), commissural (between hemispheres), projection (cortex ↔ below).
  • Association: U-fibres, arcuate/superior longitudinal fasciculus, cingulum, uncinate fasciculus.
  • Commissural: corpus callosum (the largest) and the anterior commissure.
  • Projection: internal capsule and corona radiata; corticospinal tract crosses in the medulla.
  • Intact wiring is essential; broken tracts cause disconnection syndromes.
Mnemonic
ACP
Association (within a hemisphere), Commissural (between hemispheres) and Projection (up and down to lower centres) — the three fibre types; anchor each to its star tract: Arcuate fasciculus, Corpus callosum and the internal-capsule Projection fibres.

5Blood supply to the brain

The brain is fed by two arterial systems, the internal carotid arteries in front and the vertebrobasilar system behind, which join at the base of the brain in the circle of Willis, a protective ring from which the major cerebral arteries arise, while venous blood drains through the dural sinuses to the internal jugular veins.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text The circle of Willis (an arterial ring at the base of the brain) is characterised as an anastomotic ring joining the anterior and posterior circulations and providing collateral flow if one vessel is blocked. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Circle of Willis (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • Standard text Standard references describe the brain's supply as two paired systems, the internal carotid arteries in front and the vertebral arteries behind that fuse into the basilar artery. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • Standard text Standard physiology notes that the brain uses about a quarter of the body's glucose and much of its oxygen, so any fall in blood supply quickly threatens it and can cause a stroke. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]

Exam points

  • The brain has a very high metabolic demand and receives its blood from two paired arterial systems, the internal carotid arteries supplying the front and the vertebral arteries supplying the back and base. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • The internal carotid arteries form the anterior circulation (the front blood supply from the carotid arteries), supplying most of the cerebral hemispheres through the anterior and middle cerebral arteries. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Circle of Willis (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The two vertebral arteries climb through the neck and fuse into a single basilar artery (the midline artery formed by the two vertebral arteries), together forming the posterior circulation. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Circle of Willis (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The vertebrobasilar system supplies the brainstem, cerebellum and the back of the cerebrum, including the occipital lobe, through the posterior cerebral arteries that branch from the basilar artery. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • At the base of the brain these two systems meet in the circle of Willis, a polygonal arterial ring that encircles the pituitary stalk and connects the anterior and posterior circulations. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Circle of Willis (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The ring is completed in front by the two anterior cerebral arteries joined by the single anterior communicating artery (the short bridge joining the two front arteries), closing the front of the circle. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Circle of Willis (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • On each side a posterior communicating artery (a side bridge joining front to back circulation) links the carotid to the posterior cerebral artery, joining the two circulations laterally. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Circle of Willis (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The circle's protective value is its collateral flow, so if one vessel is blocked, blood is rerouted around the ring to keep the brain tissue perfused and reduce the damage. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Circle of Willis (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The three major cerebral arteries arise around this system, each watering a defined territory of the cortex and the deep structures beneath of the cortex and the deep structures beneath it. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • The middle cerebral artery is the largest and supplies much of the lateral cortex including the language areas, so its blockage causes a severe, common stroke. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • The anterior cerebral artery supplies the medial frontal and parietal lobes, the region for the leg, so its occlusion typically weakens the opposite leg. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • The posterior cerebral artery supplies the occipital lobe and lower temporal lobe, so its occlusion typically causes loss of vision in the opposite field. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • The circle of Willis shows many normal variants, since in many people one or more segments is thin or absent, which can change how well collateral flow protects them. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Circle of Willis (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • Guarding the supply is the blood-brain barrier (a selective filter between blood and brain tissue), a tight capillary lining that lets nutrients pass while shielding the brain from blood-borne toxins. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Venous blood is collected into large channels called the dural venous sinuses, such as the superior sagittal sinus, which run between the dural layers of the dura mater. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • From these sinuses the blood drains mainly into the internal jugular veins to return to the heart, and the same sinuses also receive the cerebrospinal fluid absorbed from around the brain. [Gray’s Anatomy (standard edition), the arteries and meninges of the brain]
  • Clinically this anatomy is central, because a stroke follows interrupted blood supply or bleeding, and the deficit maps onto the artery that failed. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
Blood supply at a glance
  • Two inflow systems: internal carotid (anterior) and vertebral → basilar (posterior) arteries.
  • They meet in the circle of Willis, closed by anterior and posterior communicating arteries.
  • Three cerebral arteries: anterior (medial/leg), middle (lateral/language, largest), posterior (occipital/vision).
  • The ring gives collateral flow; it has many normal variants; the blood-brain barrier protects the tissue.
  • Venous drainage: dural venous sinuses → internal jugular veins.
Mnemonic
CoW-CAMP
Circle of Willis joins the Carotid and vertebrobasilar systems; its branches are the Anterior, Middle and Posterior cerebral arteries — remember CoW = CAMP, and that the communicating arteries close the ring to give collateral flow.

6The cerebrospinal-fluid (CSF) system

The brain is wrapped in three meninges and bathed in cerebrospinal fluid, which is made by the choroid plexus in the ventricles, flows from the lateral ventricles through the third and fourth ventricles into the subarachnoid space, and is absorbed back into the venous blood at the arachnoid granulations, cushioning and nourishing the brain.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Cerebrospinal fluid is characterised as a clear, colourless, watery fluid similar to blood plasma but with less protein, which cushions the brain, gives it buoyancy and carries away waste. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Standard text The meninges (the three protective membranes around the brain) are the tough dura mater, the web-like arachnoid mater and the delicate pia mater that clings to the brain surface. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Standard text Standard references describe the CSF route from the lateral ventricles through the third and fourth ventricles, out of the apertures of Magendie and Luschka into the subarachnoid space. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Cerebrospinal Fluid (NCBI Bookshelf)]

Exam points

  • The brain is protected by three membranes called the meninges, which lie between the skull and the brain, enclose the blood vessels and form the sheath around the venous sinuses. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The outer dura mater is a strong, leathery, two-layered membrane whose layers separate to enclose the dural venous sinuses (venous channels within the dura) that drain blood from the brain. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The middle arachnoid mater is a loose, web-like membrane, and beneath it the subarachnoid space (the fluid-filled gap beneath the arachnoid membrane) is filled with cerebrospinal fluid and the larger blood vessels. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The inner pia mater is a delicate membrane that clings tightly to the brain's folded surface, following every gyrus and sulcus and carrying small blood vessels into the tissue. [Richard S. Snell, Clinical Neuroanatomy]
  • Cerebrospinal fluid is a clear fluid, roughly 80 to 150 millilitres in volume, resembling blood plasma but with less protein, that surrounds the brain and spinal cord and fills the ventricles. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The fluid is produced by the choroid plexus (tufts of capillaries that make CSF in the ventricles), clusters of specialised capillaries lined by ependymal cells that hang from the roof of each ventricle. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The ventricular system is an interconnected set of cavities comprising two lateral ventricles, a third ventricle between the thalamic halves and a fourth ventricle between the brainstem and cerebellum. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • From each lateral ventricle the fluid passes through the foramen of Monro (the channel from a lateral ventricle to the third) into the midline third ventricle, beginning its one-way journey through the brain. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Cerebrospinal Fluid (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • From the third ventricle the fluid flows down the narrow cerebral aqueduct (the thin channel of Sylvius between third and fourth ventricles) into the fourth ventricle lying behind the pons and medulla. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Cerebrospinal Fluid (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • From the fourth ventricle the fluid escapes into the subarachnoid space through three openings, the median aperture of Magendie and the two lateral apertures of Luschka, to bathe the whole brain and cord. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Cerebrospinal Fluid (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • Once in the subarachnoid space the fluid circulates over the brain and down around the spinal cord, forming a continuous liquid cushion between the delicate tissue and the bone. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Cerebrospinal Fluid (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The fluid is finally reabsorbed into the venous blood at the arachnoid granulations (small projections that return CSF to the venous sinuses), tiny tufts of arachnoid that push into the dural venous sinuses. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Cerebrospinal Fluid (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • Production, circulation and absorption are kept in balance, so the adult makes half a litre a day while holding only a small volume, the surplus being continually absorbed. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Cerebrospinal Fluid (NCBI Bookshelf)]
  • The first function of the fluid is mechanical protection, giving the brain buoyancy so that it floats and does not crush under its own weight, and cushioning it against blows. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Further functions are nourishment and waste removal, since the fluid carries nutrients to the brain and washes away metabolic waste, helping to keep the brain's environment stable. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The choroid plexus also helps regulate the chemical environment, using ion pumps to adjust the composition of the fluid and so of the central nervous system, assisting in cleaning and homeostasis. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Clinically it matters, because a blockage of the narrow channels backs up the fluid and raises pressure in a condition called hydrocephalus (a build-up of CSF that enlarges the ventricles), which can damage the brain. [StatPearls, Neuroanatomy: Cerebrospinal Fluid (NCBI Bookshelf)]
The CSF system at a glance
  • Meninges: dura (tough, holds the sinuses), arachnoid (web-like, over the subarachnoid space), pia (clings to brain).
  • Made by the choroid plexus in the ventricles; ~80–150 ml held, ~500 ml made per day.
  • Route: lateral ventricles → foramen of Monro → third → aqueduct → fourth → Magendie & Luschka → subarachnoid space.
  • Absorbed at the arachnoid granulations into the dural venous sinuses.
  • Functions: cushion and buoyancy, nourishment, waste removal, chemical stability; blockage causes hydrocephalus.
Mnemonic
DAP-MALM
Dura, Arachnoid and Pia are the meninges; then the flow runs Monro, Aqueduct, Luschka and Magendie out to the subarachnoid space — DAP then M-A-L-M — made by choroid plexus, absorbed at the arachnoid granulations.

7Cytoarchitecture and modular organization

At the microscopic level the cortex is built from neurons and glial cells arranged in six horizontal layers of the neocortex, organised into vertical columns or modules, and mapped by Brodmann into about fifty-two numbered areas whose differing layer patterns match differing functions.

Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Cytoarchitecture (the microscopic arrangement of cells in the cortex) is characterised as the pattern of neuronal cell types and layering by which the cortex is divided into structurally distinct areas. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Classical account Korbinian Brodmann in 1909 mapped the cortex into roughly fifty-two numbered areas based on cell structure, and these Brodmann areas are still used to name functional regions. [Korbinian Brodmann, Localisation in the Cerebral Cortex (cytoarchitectonic map)]
  • Standard text The six neocortical layers are named, from the surface inward, the molecular, external granular, external pyramidal, internal granular, internal pyramidal and multiform layers. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]

Exam points

  • The cortex is built from two cell classes, the information-carrying neurons and the supporting glia (non-neuronal support cells of the nervous system), which outnumber neurons and maintain, feed and insulate them. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • The chief excitatory neuron of the cortex is the pyramidal cell, a triangular cell whose long axon carries the cortex's main output, while small stellate (granule) cells are mostly local interneurons. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • The main glial types are the star-shaped astrocytes (star-shaped glia that support neurons and the blood-brain barrier), the myelin-making oligodendrocytes, the immune microglia and the ependymal cells lining the ventricles. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Most of the cortex is neocortex, the youngest, six-layered type that makes up the great bulk of the human cortex and carries out the highest cognitive functions. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Older allocortex, such as that of the hippocampus, has only three layers, and the six-layered neocortex evolved from this simpler cortex. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • Counting from the pial surface, layer I is the molecular layer, a cell-poor layer of dendrites and axons that allows horizontal connections across the top of the cortex. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Layer II, the external granular layer, is packed with small pyramidal and stellate cells, and together with layer III it supplies many of the fibres linking one cortical area to another. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Layer III, the external pyramidal layer, holds medium pyramidal cells sending cortico-cortical and commissural output, including fibres that cross in the corpus callosum. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Layer IV, the internal granular layer, is rich in stellate cells and is the main input layer that receives sensory fibres arriving from the thalamus, so it is thick in sensory cortex. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Layer V, the internal pyramidal layer, contains the largest pyramidal cells, including the giant Betz cells of the motor cortex, and provides the major output to the brainstem and spinal cord. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Layer VI, the multiform layer, holds cells of varied shape and sends much of its output back to the thalamus, closing the loop between the cortex and its main relay station. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • The relative thickness of the layers varies with function, so sensory cortex has a thick layer IV (granular, input-heavy) while motor cortex has a thick layer V (pyramidal, output-heavy). [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • The cortex is organised not only in layers but in cortical columns (vertical modules of cells running through all six layers), narrow vertical modules running from surface to white matter whose cells share a similar response. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • These columns act as the basic processing modules of the cortex, repeated many times across the sheet, so that the cortex works as a mosaic of similar computing units. [Eric R. Kandel, James H. Schwartz & Thomas M. Jessell, Principles of Neural Science]
  • Brodmann's map divided the cortex into about fifty-two areas by their cell structure, and the numbers remain a standard shorthand, so that specific areas correspond to specific functions. [Korbinian Brodmann, Localisation in the Cerebral Cortex (cytoarchitectonic map)]
  • Familiar examples link a Brodmann number to a function, for instance area 4 is the primary motor cortex, areas 3, 1 and 2 are the somatosensory cortex, and area 17 is the visual cortex. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
  • This microscopic organisation underpins the whole brain's working, because the 'minimal wiring' principle places co-active neurons close to one another to save energy and reduce delay. [Barre Vijaya Prasad, Examining Biological Foundations of Human Behavior]
Cytoarchitecture at a glance
  • Cells: pyramidal (output) and stellate (local) neurons; astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia, ependyma.
  • Neocortex has six layers; older allocortex (hippocampus) has three.
  • Six layers: I molecular, II external granular, III external pyramidal, IV internal granular, V internal pyramidal, VI multiform.
  • Layer IV = sensory input (thick in sensory cortex); layer V = output with Betz cells (thick in motor cortex).
  • Vertical columns = processing modules; Brodmann mapped ~52 areas (4 motor, 3-1-2 sensory, 17 visual).
Mnemonic
MEE-IIM
Molecular, External granular, External pyramidal, Internal granular, Internal pyramidal and Multiform — the six cortical layers top to bottom (MEE-IIM); remember input lands in IV and output leaves from V, and that vertical columns are the modules Brodmann mapped.
