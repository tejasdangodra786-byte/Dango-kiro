# CHILDREN'S APPERCEPTION TEST — HUMAN VERSION (CAT-H)
## Psychodiagnostic Report — Bellak Analysis-Sheet Method

**Instrument:** The Children's Apperception Test — Human Version (CAT-H; Bellak & Bellak, 1965)
**Interpretive system:** Bellak, L. & Abrams, D. M., *The T.A.T., the C.A.T., and the S.A.T. in Clinical Use* — the Bellak C.A.T. Blank & Analysis Sheet (10-variable scheme; descriptive → interpretive → diagnostic levels)
**Cards administered:** Card 1 to Card 10
**Language of production:** Hinglish (transcribed verbatim by the examiner)
**Patient:** AP, 16-year-old male adolescent

---

### A NOTE ON METHOD (per the manual)

Bellak instructs the interpreter to read each story on three levels: the **descriptive** (what literally happens), the **interpretive** (a generalised conditional clause, usually framed as *"If one…"*), and the **diagnostic** (a definite statement about the person). The interpreter then condenses each record onto the Analysis Sheet's ten variables. Throughout, Bellak warns that fantasy material is "prima facie only the fantasy needs of the subject," that the relation of fantasy needs to behaviour must be checked against clinical/biographical data, and that single signs are never diagnostic in isolation — consistency *across* stories is what carries weight.

The CAT-H employs human figures in the same stimulus situations as the original animal C.A.T., designed for children and adolescents aged 7–10+ and those with higher intellectual functioning who respond more productively to human stimuli. The interpretation framework remains identical.

## CARD 1 — Children at a table with food; adult figure in background

**1. Main theme**
- *Descriptive:* Three children and their father. The father, traumatised by his wife's death (she died while cooking), develops serious mental issues. He kills the children one by one and makes the surviving ones eat them. Initially there were 5–6 siblings. The child sitting in the corner escapes, calls the police, and saves himself. The father is arrested.
- *Interpretive:* *If* one lives under a traumatised, violent authority figure, survival depends on being overlooked and marginalized; only external institutional help can provide rescue.
- *Diagnostic:* Profound fear of a destructive, cannibalistic paternal figure. Oral-aggressive themes dominate. The family is perceived as a site of annihilation. Survival requires invisibility and external intervention — personal agency is insufficient.

**2. Main hero** (✓)
- a) Age: 6–8 years (corner child); father also nominated ~ b) Sex: male ~ c) Vocation: child/dependent
- d) Abilities: escape, calling for help ~ e) Interests: survival ~ f) Traits: small, quiet, overlooked
- g) Body/self-image: **vulnerable, threatened** — the body is conceived as potential food, an object to be consumed; survival depends on insignificance
- h) Adequacy: **minimally adequate** — can only escape and call police; cannot actively fight or protect

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**
- a) Behavioural needs: **n Harm Avoidance** (primary — escape/survival); **n Succorance** (seeking help from police). Father's needs: extreme **n Aggression**, **n Dominance** (absolute control through violence). *Dynamic inference:* the massive aggression attributed to the father likely represents projected aggressive impulses of the subject.
- b) Figures/objects/circumstances introduced: **police** (external rescue — significant introduction); multiple siblings (5–6) systematically eliminated; **cannibalism** (children forced to eat siblings — highly significant oral-aggressive content); mother dying while cooking; trauma and mental illness as causal explanations; **movie reference** as distancing device.
- c) Figures/objects/circumstances omitted: **no nurturing figure** after mother's death; no sibling alliance; no internal coping mechanism.

**4. Conception of environment (world) as:** **extremely dangerous, violent, unpredictable.** The home is a place of death. Authority figures are mentally unstable and destructive. No natural protection exists — safety requires external institutional intervention. Trauma begets endless cycles of violence.

**5. Figures seen as**
- a) Parental figures: **father — traumatised, mentally ill, cannibalistic, murderous.** His aggression is attributed to trauma (wife's death) and anger issues. He is simultaneously provider and destroyer. **Mother — absent; died in the act of nurturing (cooking).** Idealised through absence. Her death catalyses father's deterioration.
- b) Contemporary/sibling figures: **victims, helpless, consumed** by paternal aggression.
- c) Authority figures: **police — only source of justice and rescue.** External, impersonal authority more trustworthy than family.

**6. Significant conflicts:** survival vs. annihilation; trust vs. fear of authority (the protector is the destroyer); oral incorporation vs. oral aggression (food = both nurturance and destruction); dependence vs. autonomy.

**7. Nature of anxieties** (✓)
- (✓) **physical harm/annihilation** (primary, overwhelming)
- (✓) **being consumed/incorporated** (oral-cannibalistic)
- (✓) **loss of nurturing object** (mother's death)
- (✓) **helplessness/abandonment** (no reliable caretaker)

**8. Main defenses against conflicts and fears** (✓)
- (✓) **Projection** — all aggression placed entirely in the father figure
- (✓) **Externalization** — solution comes from outside (police)
- (✓) **Rationalization** — father's behaviour explained through "trauma" and "mental issues"
- (✓) **Distancing** — story framed through movie reference ("maine esi movie dekhi thi")
- (✓) **Splitting** — clear good (children/police) vs. evil (father), no ambivalence

**9. Adequacy of superego** (✓)
- (✓) Punishment is **proportionate** — father caught by police. However, punishment is external and imposed rather than self-generated. Father shows no remorse. Suggests a superego relying on **external enforcement** rather than internalized moral standards.

**10. Integration of the ego** (✓)
- a) Hero: minimally adequate (escapes only)
- b) Outcome: **mixed** — child survives but siblings are dead; no restoration
- c) Drive control: **poor** — aggressive content overwhelms the narrative
- d) Thought processes: **structured, coherent, logically sequenced** (beginning/middle/end present); reality testing preserved (police intervention is realistic); however, oral-cannibalistic content indicates significant anxiety breakthrough
- Intelligence: above average (elaborate story, causal reasoning) | Maturation level: regressed content despite adequate cognitive structure | Organic signs: none

## CARD 2 — Three figures in a rope-pulling scene; one alone, two together

**2. Main hero** (✓)

- h) Adequacy: **paradoxically adequate** (saves others through technique/sacrifice) yet **profoundly inadequate** (cannot find a reason to live; adequacy is expressed only through self-destruction)

**3. Main needs and drives of hero**

**5. Figures seen as**

- b) Sibling figures: Younger = favourite, receives unconditional love by being young. Older/arrogant = academically excellent, redeemable. Self (middle) = invisible, unvalued, dispensable.

**6. Significant conflicts:** self-worth vs. perceived worthlessness; need for love vs. acceptance of rejection; aggression (toward parents) vs. self-directed destruction (retroflection); life vs. death — existence itself conditional on "winning."

**7. Nature of anxieties** (✓)
- (✓) **being unloved/unvalued** (primary)
- (✓) **insignificance** — not mattering to anyone
- (✓) **abandonment** — even in death parents don't fully mourn
- (✓) **competitive inadequacy** — compared and found wanting
- (✓) **existential** — questioning purpose of existence

**8. Main defenses** (✓)
- (✓) **Turning against the self (retroflection)** — all aggression directed inward as self-sacrifice
- (✓) **Rationalization** — "mere marne se loss kam hai" — logical justification for self-destruction
- (✓) **Reaction Formation** — hostility toward family converted to noble sacrifice
- (✓) **Intellectualization** — uses logic to arrive at self-destruction
- (✓) **Altruistic Surrender** — gives up life so "more valued" others can live
- (✓) **Denial** — denies anger; presents acceptance rather than rage

**9. Adequacy of superego** (✓)
- (✓) **Extremely harsh, primitive superego.** The "crime" is merely existing without being exceptional — punishment is death. Massively disproportionate. No actual crime committed, yet death is the consequence. Consistent with **depressive dynamics and passive suicidal ideation.**

**10. Integration of the ego** (✓)
- a) Hero: paradoxically adequate (saves others) yet profoundly self-destructive
- b) Outcome: **unhappy** — hero dies; others reformed but he is gone
- c) Drive control: **poor** — ego overwhelmed by internalized aggression; "solution" is self-annihilation
- d) Thought processes: **structured, coherent, highly elaborated** (detailed, emotionally invested); reality testing partially maintained (story internally consistent but the "logic" of self-sacrifice reveals depressive distortion)

- c) Drive control: adequate; affect managed through action

- Intelligence: above average | Maturation: adequate | Organic signs: none

The index patient, AP, a 16-year-old male adolescent, presents a CAT-H profile characterised by significant personality disturbance centred around themes of emotional deprivation, parental inadequacy, sibling-based worthlessness, and pervasive aggressive content with inadequate containment.
