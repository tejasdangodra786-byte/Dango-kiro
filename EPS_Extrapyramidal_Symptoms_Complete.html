What EPS are

Extrapyramidal symptoms (EPS) are drug-induced disturbances of movement caused mainly by medicines that block dopamine in the brain's motor control system. The extrapyramidal system is the network (chiefly the basal ganglia and their connections) that runs alongside the “pyramidal” (direct voluntary) motor pathway and fine-tunes movement — smoothing it, setting muscle tone, and suppressing unwanted movements. When drugs interfere with it, movement becomes either too little and stiff (parkinsonism), fixed in abnormal postures (dystonia), restless (akathisia), or excessive and involuntary (dyskinesia).

EPS are the classic and most troublesome side effects of antipsychotic (neuroleptic) drugs, but they can be caused by any dopamine-blocking drug — including common anti-sickness (antiemetic) medicines. They matter clinically because they are distressing, stigmatising, a major cause of patients stopping treatment, sometimes dangerous (airway dystonia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome) and, in the tardive form, sometimes permanent.

Terms in plain language. Extrapyramidal = the movement-regulating system outside the main voluntary motor pathway; dopamine (D2) blockade = the drug plugs the dopamine receptor so dopamine can't act; dystonia = sustained muscle contraction pulling the body into an abnormal posture; akathisia = an inner restlessness with a need to move; parkinsonism = slowness + stiffness + tremor like Parkinson's disease; dyskinesia = involuntary repetitive movements; tardive = late-appearing; acute = early/sudden; anticholinergic = a drug that blocks acetylcholine (relieves most acute EPS).

Mechanism — why EPS happen

The dopamine–acetylcholine balance in the striatum

Kaplan & Sadock The key pathway is the nigrostriatal dopamine pathway (from the substantia nigra to the striatum). Normally dopamine and acetylcholine are held in balance there. Antipsychotics block striatal dopamine D2 receptors; this removes dopamine's braking effect and leaves a relative cholinergic excess, which produces the acute EPS. This is why anticholinergic drugs (benztropine, procyclidine, trihexyphenidyl) relieve acute dystonia and parkinsonism — they restore the balance.

  • The ~80% threshold. Clinically significant EPS typically appear once striatal D2 receptor occupancy exceeds roughly 80%. Drugs and doses that push occupancy above this line cause EPS; the therapeutic “window” for antipsychotic effect sits just below it (~65–80%).
  • Four dopamine pathways — why antipsychotics have these effects. Blocking the mesolimbic pathway gives the antipsychotic benefit; blocking the nigrostriatal pathway causes EPS; blocking the tuberoinfundibular pathway raises prolactin; the mesocortical pathway relates to negative/cognitive symptoms.
  • Why atypicals cause fewer EPS. Second-generation (atypical) antipsychotics combine D2 blockade with strong serotonin 5-HT2A blockade (and, for some, looser/“fast-off” D2 binding or partial agonism). 5-HT2A blockade disinhibits dopamine release in the nigrostriatal pathway, protecting motor function — hence lower EPS risk.
  • Why tardive dyskinesia is different. The leading theory is dopamine-receptor supersensitivity / up-regulation: after months to years of blockade, striatal D2 receptors increase in number and sensitivity, so dopamine (or a fall in blockade) now produces excess movement. This explains why TD may worsen when the drug is reduced and why anticholinergics worsen it.

The single most useful rule

Acute EPS = too much acetylcholine relative to dopamine → anticholinergics help. Tardive dyskinesia = dopamine supersensitivity → anticholinergics HARM; use a VMAT2 inhibitor. Getting this the wrong way round is a classic and dangerous error.

Classification & timeline of EPS

EPS are grouped into acute/early (dose-related, usually reversible if caught) and tardive/late (delayed, potentially persistent). A widely used memory aid is the “4 hours – 4 days – 4 weeks – 4 months” sequence.

Two families of movement. Hypokinetic (too little movement) = parkinsonism. Hyperkinetic (too much/abnormal movement) = dystonia, akathisia (subjective + motor), dyskinesia. Recognising which family you are looking at points quickly to the diagnosis.

1. Acute dystonia (acute dystonic reaction)

Acute dystonia is a sudden, sustained, involuntary contraction of muscle groups that pulls part of the body into an abnormal, often twisted, fixed posture. It is frequently dramatic, painful and frightening for the patient and family, and can be mistaken for a seizure, tetany, hysteria, or even “attention-seeking” — but it is a genuine, treatable drug reaction that usually reverses within minutes of the right injection.

OnsetHours to ~5 days; often within 24–48 h of starting or increasing the drug, or after a depot injection
Highest riskYoung males; high-potency typicals; high dose / rapid loading; dystonia-prone individuals; antipsychotic-naïve patients
MechanismAcute striatal D2 blockade → relative cholinergic excess
TreatmentIM/IV anticholinergic (benztropine, procyclidine) or antihistamine (diphenhydramine, promethazine) — rapid relief
Why young men? Acute dystonia is most common in young males (and in the antipsychotic-naïve, and after rapid high-potency dosing). Always warn such patients and consider prophylactic anticholinergic cover when starting a high-potency typical.

Acute dystonia can affect almost any voluntary muscle group. The named forms below are all variants of the same reaction — a patient may show one or several at once.

· Oculogyric crisis (the “eyes rolled up to the ceiling” dystonia)

Type: Acute dystonia affecting the extraocular muscles — the classic and most-asked-about EPS presentation.

What it looks like

  • A forced, sustained, involuntary deviation of both eyes — classically upward and to one side, so the person appears to be staring at the ceiling and cannot bring the eyes back down voluntarily.
  • The eyes may instead deviate laterally or, less often, downward; the head is often tilted back (retrocollis) to accompany the upward gaze.
  • The episode is painful, distressing and terrifying; the patient is fully conscious and aware throughout (which distinguishes it from a seizure).
  • Often accompanied by blepharospasm (forced eyelid closure), widened palpebral fissures/staring, tongue protrusion, jaw or neck dystonia, and marked anxiety/agitation.
  • May be episodic and recurrent, sometimes precipitated or worsened by stress or fatigue; between episodes eye movements can be normal.
OnsetEarly — hours to days after starting/increasing a dopamine blocker; can recur if the drug continues
Due toHigh-potency typicals (haloperidol, fluphenazine, trifluoperazine); antiemetics (metoclopramide, prochlorperazine); occasionally atypicals
NoteOculogyric crises also occurred in post-encephalitic parkinsonism historically — but today the commonest cause is medication
TreatmentIM/IV anticholinergic (benztropine/procyclidine) or diphenhydramine — eyes usually normalise within minutes
Differential diagnosis

From an epileptic seizure / gaze deviation of a seizure (there consciousness is impaired, with post-ictal confusion — oculogyric crisis has preserved awareness); from a tonic gaze from a stroke/brainstem lesion (other neurological signs); from conversion/functional presentations; from tics (brief, suppressible, not sustained). The key clue: recent start of a dopamine-blocking drug + sustained forced upgaze + full awareness.

Remember

“Eyes forced up to the ceiling, can't look down, fully awake, just started an antipsychotic/metoclopramide” — oculogyric crisis (acute ocular dystonia). Give an anticholinergic injection.

· Fixed staring, gaze deviation and eyelid dystonias

Type: Acute (and sometimes tardive) dystonia of the eye and eyelid muscles — the “staring / eyes-fixed” presentations.

Forms of abnormal gaze/eyelid posture

  • Sustained lateral or upward gaze deviation — the eyes are pulled and held to one side or up, giving a fixed “staring” appearance the person cannot overcome.
  • Blepharospasm — involuntary forced closure/squeezing of the eyelids.
  • Widened palpebral fissures with a fixed stare — a “wide-eyed staring” look, sometimes with reduced blinking; also seen as part of drug-induced parkinsonism (the “reptilian” stare with reduced blink rate).
  • A general reduced blink rate and masked, staring face is a feature of drug-induced parkinsonism (see below) rather than dystonia — useful to distinguish the two “staring” pictures.
Differential diagnosis

The staring in parkinsonism (reduced blinking, masked face, slowness — hypokinetic) versus the staring/forced gaze of ocular dystonia (sustained forced eye deviation, often painful — hyperkinetic). Also from thyroid eye disease and neurological gaze palsies.

Remember

“Fixed forced gaze/blink-squeeze” = dystonia; “wide unblinking mask-like stare + slowness” = parkinsonism.

· Neck dystonias — torticollis, retrocollis, anterocollis, laterocollis

Type: Acute dystonia of the neck muscles — among the commonest acute dystonic presentations.

Forms

  • Torticollis — the neck twists so the head turns/tilts to one side (“wry neck”).
  • Retrocollis — the head is pulled backward (often accompanying an oculogyric crisis).
  • Anterocollis — the head is pulled forward/down onto the chest.
  • Laterocollis — the head is pulled sideways toward the shoulder.

These are sustained, often painful, and the patient cannot straighten the neck voluntarily.

Differential diagnosis

From musculoskeletal “crick” / spasmodic torticollis (no drug trigger, no other dystonic features); from tardive dystonia (late onset, more fixed and treatment-resistant); from meningism/neck stiffness (fever, infection signs).

Remember

-collis = neck. Torti (twist) / Retro (back) / Antero (forward) / Latero (side). Sudden, drug-triggered, painful → acute dystonia.

· Truncal dystonias — opisthotonus and axial arching

Type: Acute dystonia of the trunk/axial muscles — the most dramatic-looking form.

Forms

  • Opisthotonus — a strong backward arching of the whole spine and neck, so the body bows backward (can resemble the arching seen in tetanus or severe seizures).
  • Axial/truncal twisting and scoliotic posturing — the trunk is pulled and held to one side or into a twisted posture.
  • Pisa syndrome (pleurothotonus) — a sustained lateral bending of the trunk (leaning like the Leaning Tower of Pisa), which may be acute or tardive.
Differential diagnosis

From a tonic seizure or tetanus (opisthotonus with trismus, but with a wound/infection history) and from strychnine poisoning; the drug history and preserved awareness point to acute dystonia.

Remember

“Whole-body backward arch after an antipsychotic” = opisthotonic acute dystonia. Pisa = leaning to one side.

· Orofacial dystonias — tongue, jaw and mouth

Type: Acute dystonia of the tongue, jaw and facial muscles.

Forms

  • Tongue protrusion / buccolingual dystonia — the tongue is forced out or twisted; difficulty speaking or swallowing.
  • Trismus — forced jaw clenching (lockjaw), or the opposite, a forced-open jaw dislocation.
  • Grimacing and facial contortion — sustained abnormal facial postures.
  • Dysarthria / dysphagia — speech and swallowing impaired by the tongue/jaw contraction.
Watch for airway involvement. Tongue and jaw dystonia can shade into pharyngeal/laryngeal dystonia — see the next section — which is an emergency.

Differential diagnosis

From the orofacial movements of tardive dyskinesia (late, repetitive lip-smacking/chewing rather than a sustained fixed posture); from a focal seizure; from a temporomandibular joint problem.

Remember

“Tongue forced out / jaw locked, just started a dopamine blocker” = acute orofacial dystonia (not tardive dyskinesia, which is later and repetitive).

· Laryngospasm / pharyngeal dystonia — the airway EMERGENCY

Type: Acute dystonia of the laryngeal/pharyngeal muscles — rare but life-threatening.

Medical emergency. Dystonic contraction of the larynx and pharynx can cause stridor, difficulty breathing and airway obstruction, with risk of asphyxiation. It requires immediate parenteral anticholinergic/antihistamine (IM or IV), airway support, and urgent medical attention. Never dismiss breathing difficulty in a patient on antipsychotics as anxiety.

What it looks like

  • Sudden throat tightness, difficulty swallowing, a choking sensation, stridor (noisy breathing) and respiratory distress.
  • May accompany tongue/jaw dystonia; the patient is frightened and struggling to breathe.
Differential diagnosis

From anaphylaxis (urticaria, hypotension, allergen exposure), from a foreign body, and from a panic attack (no true stridor/obstruction). The drug history and rapid response to IV anticholinergic confirm dystonia.

Remember

“Can't breathe/stridor after an antipsychotic” = laryngeal dystonia — treat immediately with parenteral anticholinergic and secure the airway.

2. Akathisia

Akathisia (from the Greek for “inability to sit”) is a distressing subjective inner restlessness coupled with a compelling urge to move. It is felt as much as it is seen — and this is what makes it dangerous, because the inner torment can be mistaken for worsening psychosis, anxiety or agitation, leading to the harmful error of increasing the antipsychotic (which worsens the akathisia).

What it looks like & feels like

  • Subjective: a feeling of inner restlessness, tension or unease, an inability to relax, “jumping out of my skin”, being “driven to move”.
  • Objective (motor): pacing, inability to sit or stand still, rocking from foot to foot, shifting weight, marching on the spot, crossing/uncrossing legs, shuffling, rubbing the face or scalp.
  • Often worse when required to be still (waiting rooms, meals, at night).
OnsetEarly to intermediate — days to weeks of starting/increasing (acute); a persistent tardive form can appear later
Due toHigh-potency typicals; aripiprazole and other atypicals; metoclopramide; also SSRIs/SNRIs (akathisia-like restlessness)
DangerStrongly linked to distress, dysphoria, aggression and increased SUICIDALITY; often misread as worsening illness
TreatmentReduce dose / switch; PROPRANOLOL (beta-blocker) first-line; benzodiazepines; anticholinergics work poorly here
The critical trap. Akathisia is frequently mistaken for agitation, anxiety, or a psychotic relapse. If the antipsychotic dose is raised in response, the akathisia (and the risk) gets worse. Always ask specifically about inner restlessness after starting/increasing an antipsychotic.

Differential diagnosis

From psychotic agitation / psychomotor agitation (driven by psychiatric symptoms, not a subjective urge to move the legs); from an anxiety disorder; from drug-induced parkinsonism with restlessness; from restless legs syndrome (worse at night/rest, relieved by movement, often with dysaesthesia); from stimulant/withdrawal states. The clue: onset closely tied to starting/increasing a dopamine-blocking drug, with an intolerable urge to move.

Remember

“Can't sit still, feels tormented inside, just started/increased an antipsychotic” — akathisia. Propranolol, not anticholinergic. Don't raise the antipsychotic.

3. Drug-induced parkinsonism (pseudoparkinsonism)

Drug-induced parkinsonism is a reversible mimic of Parkinson's disease caused by dopamine blockade. It is the hypokinetic EPS — too little movement — and typically comes on more gradually than dystonia or akathisia.

Core features (the parkinsonian picture)

  • Bradykinesia / akinesia — slowness and poverty of movement; the cardinal feature.
  • Rigidity — increased muscle tone: “cogwheel” (ratchety) and “lead-pipe” (smooth, sustained) rigidity.
  • Tremor — a resting, ~4–6 Hz “pill-rolling” tremor.
  • Postural instability, shuffling festinant gait, reduced arm swing, stooped posture.
  • Masked, expressionless face (hypomimia) with a reduced blink rate and fixed stare, hypersalivation/drooling, soft monotonous speech, and small handwriting (micrographia).
  • Rabbit syndrome — a fine, rapid, rhythmic perioral (lip) tremor, a specific late variant of drug-induced parkinsonism (responds to anticholinergics, unlike tardive dyskinesia).
OnsetIntermediate — usually days to weeks, up to ~3 months after starting/increasing
Highest riskElderly, female, higher doses, high-potency typicals; also higher-dose risperidone/olanzapine; metoclopramide
MechanismNigrostriatal D2 blockade mimicking the dopamine deficiency of Parkinson's disease
TreatmentReduce dose / switch to lower-potency or atypical (quetiapine, clozapine); anticholinergics (benztropine, trihexyphenidyl); amantadine
Bilateral & symptom-linked. Drug-induced parkinsonism is typically more symmetrical and clearly time-linked to the drug, whereas idiopathic Parkinson's disease usually starts asymmetrically. But the two can be hard to separate, especially in the elderly, and a drug can unmask early true Parkinson's disease.

Differential diagnosis

From idiopathic Parkinson's disease (asymmetrical onset, progressive, not drug-linked, may respond to levodopa); from depression / negative symptoms of schizophrenia (which also produce slowing and reduced expression — but without rigidity/cogwheeling); from catatonia; from hypothyroidism; from other causes of tremor (essential tremor is postural/action, not resting).

Remember — parkinsonism “TRAP”

Tremor (resting, pill-rolling) · Rigidity (cogwheel/lead-pipe) · Akinesia/bradykinesia · Postural instability. Plus masked face, drooling, shuffling gait, micrographia. Reversible; anticholinergics/amantadine help.

4. Tardive dyskinesia (TD)

Tardive dyskinesia is the late-onset, involuntary, repetitive movement disorder caused by long-term dopamine-blocking drugs. It is the most feared EPS because it may be persistent or irreversible. “Tardive” means late — it appears after prolonged exposure, not at the start.

What it looks like

  • Orofacial / buccolingual-masticatory (classic): lip-smacking, puckering and pursing, chewing movements, tongue-thrusting and rolling (the “fly-catcher” tongue), grimacing, and cheek-puffing.
  • Limbs: choreoathetoid (dance-like, writhing) finger, hand and foot movements; piano-playing finger movements.
  • Trunk: rocking, twisting, pelvic thrusting, shoulder shrugging.
  • Respiratory dyskinesia: irregular breathing, grunting.
  • Movements lessen or disappear during sleep, worsen with stress, and can be briefly suppressed voluntarily — but return.
OnsetLate — after months to years (usually ≥3–6 months) of dopamine-blocker exposure
Highest riskELDERLY WOMEN; longer duration & higher cumulative dose; typicals >> atypicals; diabetes; early EPS; affective disorder
MechanismDopamine-receptor supersensitivity / up-regulation after chronic blockade
PrognosisMay be PERSISTENT or IRREVERSIBLE, especially if not caught early
The paradoxes that give TD away. TD worsens transiently when the drug is reduced or stopped (“withdrawal-emergent dyskinesia”) and may be temporarily masked by raising the dose — the opposite of acute EPS. And crucially: anticholinergics WORSEN tardive dyskinesia (whereas they help acute dystonia/parkinsonism). Withdraw anticholinergics if TD appears.

Differential diagnosis

From acute orofacial dystonia (early, sustained fixed posture — TD is late and repetitive); from spontaneous dyskinesias of elderly/edentulous patients and long-standing schizophrenia; from Huntington's disease and other choreas (family history, cognitive decline); from ill-fitting dentures; from stereotypies. Establish the long dopamine-blocker exposure and the classic orofacial pattern.

Remember

“Late, repetitive lip-smacking / chewing / tongue-rolling / choreoathetoid limbs after long-term antipsychotics” = tardive dyskinesia. Stop anticholinergics; use a VMAT2 inhibitor.

Treatment

  • Prevent: use the lowest effective antipsychotic dose, prefer atypicals, review regularly (e.g. AIMS — Abnormal Involuntary Movement Scale).
  • Reduce/stop or switch the offending drug where clinically possible; consider switching toward clozapine (lowest TD liability).
  • Specific treatment: VMAT2 inhibitors — valbenazine and deutetrabenazine (also tetrabenazine) are the evidence-based drugs for TD.
  • Stop anticholinergics (they worsen TD). Ginkgo biloba and clonazepam have limited supporting evidence.

5. Tardive dystonia and other tardive syndromes

Type: The broader “tardive” spectrum — late-onset, often persistent movement disorders beyond classic TD.

Tardive dystonia

Sustained twisting/posturing appearing late in treatment (unlike the acute dystonia of the first days). It is often more fixed, more disabling and more treatment-resistant than acute dystonia. Retrocollis, truncal arching (opisthotonic posturing) and Pisa syndrome are characteristic. Can coexist with tardive dyskinesia.

Other tardive syndromes

  • Tardive akathisia — late-onset, persistent restlessness (the tardive counterpart of acute akathisia).
  • Tardive tremor — a late, often postural/action tremor.
  • Tardive tics / tardive myoclonus — less common late movement phenomena.
  • Tardive pain / oral (burning mouth) syndromes — occasionally described.
Differential diagnosis

Tardive dystonia from acute dystonia (timing: late vs first days; fixedness) and from primary/idiopathic dystonias; tardive akathisia from acute akathisia and from an agitated depression.

Remember

“Tardive = late & stubborn.” Tardive dystonia (fixed twisting, retrocollis/arching), tardive akathisia (persistent restlessness), plus tardive tremor/tics. Treat as for TD: reduce/switch drug, consider VMAT2 inhibitor; anticholinergics may help tardive dystonia (unlike TD).

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome (NMS) — the life-threatening extreme

Type: A rare, life-threatening emergency from dopamine blockade. Discussed with EPS because of the shared mechanism, but it is a distinct medical emergency, not a simple EPS.

Emergency. NMS is fatal if unrecognised. Suspect it in any patient on a dopamine blocker who develops fever + rigidity + confusion + autonomic instability. Stop the drug and get urgent medical/critical-care help.

The NMS tetrad

  • Hyperthermia — high fever.
  • Severe “lead-pipe” muscular rigidity (generalised).
  • Autonomic instability — labile/high blood pressure, tachycardia, profuse sweating, tachypnoea.
  • Altered mental status — fluctuating consciousness, confusion, stupor.

Investigations: markedly raised creatine kinase (CK) (from muscle breakdown), leukocytosis, deranged electrolytes; risk of rhabdomyolysis and acute renal failure.

OnsetAny time, but classically within the first 1–2 weeks of starting/increasing, or with rapid escalation
Due toAny dopamine blocker (high-potency typicals highest); also ABRUPT WITHDRAWAL of dopaminergic (anti-Parkinson) drugs; dehydration a risk factor
MechanismProfound central dopamine blockade → thermoregulatory & motor failure
TreatmentSTOP the antipsychotic; supportive care + cooling + fluids; DANTROLENE; bromocriptine/amantadine (dopamine agonists); benzodiazepines; ICU; ECT if refractory
Differential diagnosis

From serotonin syndrome (serotonergic drugs, with hyperreflexia, clonus, myoclonus — NMS has lead-pipe rigidity and hyporeflexia, and evolves more slowly); from malignant catatonia (overlapping; may precede NMS); from malignant hyperthermia (anaesthetic trigger); from heat stroke, CNS infection (meningitis/encephalitis), and anticholinergic toxicity.

Remember — NMS “FEVER”

Fever · Encephalopathy (altered mind) · Vitals unstable (autonomic) · Elevated CK/enzymes · Rigidity (lead-pipe). Stop the drug; dantrolene + dopamine agonist; ICU.

Which drugs cause EPS (and why some cause more than others)

Any drug that blocks dopamine D2 receptors can cause EPS. Risk tracks with D2 potency, dose, and speed of titration. The general rule: typical (first-generation) >> atypical (second-generation); high-potency >> low-potency.

Non-drug and reversible causes to keep in mind

EPS-like pictures can also arise from abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic drugs (parkinsonism/NMS-like states in Parkinson's disease), dehydration and high fever (NMS risk), and rapid IM/depot loading. Always review the full drug list — including antiemetics — before attributing movements to the primary psychiatric illness.

In which disorders / situations EPS occur

EPS are a drug side effect, so they occur in any condition treated with dopamine-blocking drugs. The commonest settings:

  • Schizophrenia, schizoaffective and other psychotic disorders — the largest group, on long-term antipsychotics (highest cumulative TD risk).
  • Bipolar disorder — antipsychotics for mania and maintenance.
  • Severe / psychotic depression — antipsychotic augmentation.
  • Delirium and agitation, and behavioural symptoms of dementia — antipsychotics in the elderly (high parkinsonism/TD risk). Avoid antipsychotics in Lewy body dementia and Parkinson's disease dementia, which show extreme “neuroleptic sensitivity” (severe, sometimes fatal reactions).
  • Tourette syndrome and tic disorders — antipsychotics (haloperidol, aripiprazole, risperidone).
  • Autism spectrum disorder and intellectual disability with severe behavioural disturbance — risperidone, aripiprazole.
  • Nausea, vomiting, gastroparesis, migraine — metoclopramide, prochlorperazine: a very common, frequently missed cause of EPS (including acute dystonia in young patients and TD with chronic use), often outside psychiatry.
  • Postoperative / palliative and general-medical settings — antiemetic and sedative dopamine blockers.
Special-population cautions. Young men → acute dystonia; elderly, especially women → parkinsonism and tardive dyskinesia; Parkinson's/Lewy body → avoid dopamine blockers; use quetiapine or clozapine if antipsychotic is unavoidable.

Treatment principles across EPS

Acute EPS (dystonia, parkinsonism)

  • Acute dystonia: parenteral anticholinergic (IM/IV benztropine or procyclidine) or antihistamine (diphenhydramine, promethazine) — rapid relief; secure the airway if laryngeal.
  • Parkinsonism: reduce dose / switch to lower-potency or atypical (quetiapine, clozapine); anticholinergics (benztropine, trihexyphenidyl); amantadine.
  • Prophylaxis: consider anticholinergic cover when starting a high-potency typical in a high-risk (young, naïve) patient.

Akathisia

  • Reduce dose / switch to a lower-liability drug.
  • Propranolol (beta-blocker) first-line; benzodiazepines (e.g. clonazepam); mirtazapine has some evidence.
  • Anticholinergics are relatively ineffective for akathisia.

Tardive dyskinesia

  • Prevent (lowest dose, prefer atypicals, monitor with AIMS).
  • Reduce/stop or switch (toward clozapine); VMAT2 inhibitors: valbenazine, deutetrabenazine.
  • STOP anticholinergics (they worsen TD).

Neuroleptic malignant syndrome

  • Stop the antipsychotic; supportive care, cooling, IV fluids; monitor CK/renal function.
  • Dantrolene; bromocriptine/amantadine (dopamine agonists); benzodiazepines; ICU; ECT if refractory.
The anticholinergic response rule (memorise this). Anticholinergics help acute dystonia and drug-induced parkinsonism; are relatively unhelpful for akathisia (use propranolol); and worsen tardive dyskinesia. NMS is treated with dantrolene + dopamine agonists, not anticholinergics.

Mnemonics & quick recall

Timeline — “4 hours, 4 days, 4 weeks, 4 months”

  • ~4 hours–days → acute Dystonia (incl. oculogyric crisis)
  • ~4 days–weeks → Akathisia
  • ~4 weeks–months → Parkinsonism
  • ~4 months–years → Tardive dyskinesia

Recognise each at a glance

  • Dystonia = fixed abnormal posture (eyes to ceiling / neck twist / arched back).
  • Akathisia = can't sit still, inner torment.
  • Parkinsonism = slow, stiff, tremulous, masked staring face.
  • Tardive dyskinesia = late, repetitive lip-smacking / chewing / tongue-rolling.
  • NMS = fever + lead-pipe rigidity + autonomic + confusion + raised CK (emergency).

Treatment at a glance

Dystonia → anticholinergic injection. Akathisia → propranolol. Parkinsonism → anticholinergic/amantadine + dose cut. Tardive dyskinesia → VMAT2 inhibitor, stop anticholinergics. NMS → stop drug + dantrolene + dopamine agonist + ICU.
