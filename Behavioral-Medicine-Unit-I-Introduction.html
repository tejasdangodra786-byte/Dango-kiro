1 Definition, boundary & scope of Behavioral Medicine

Behavioral medicine is the meeting-point of behavioral science and medicine. It asks how the way people act, think, feel and live shapes who becomes ill, how ill they become, and how well they recover — and then turns that knowledge into real techniques for prevention and treatment. A defining idea of the field is the professional boundary: the understood limit of the clinician–patient relationship that keeps it safe and trustworthy.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Start with a clear definition — give the 1977 Yale / Schwartz & Weiss definition first, then the wider ISBM charter definition.
  2. Tell the short history — how thinking moved from "body only" (the biomedical view) to "body + mind + society" (the biopsychosocial model).
  3. Explain the scope — who works in it, which sciences it borrows from, and which fields sit next to it (health psychology, psychosomatic medicine, medical psychology).
  4. Define health and illness — the WHO definition of health, and why illness is partly a personal judgement, not just a lab result.
  5. Cover boundaries in full — what a boundary is, why it matters, the types of boundaries, crossing versus violation, high-risk situations, the three boundary challenges, warning signs and guidelines.
  6. End with significance and a one-line summary — why the field matters for prevention and the mind–body link.

The detailed notes below are grouped to follow exactly this order, so you can read top-to-bottom and write the answer in the same sequence.

Characterisations by standard texts

definition "The interdisciplinary field concerned with the development and integration of behavioral and biomedical science knowledge and techniques relevant to health and illness and the application of this knowledge and these techniques to prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation." [Yale Conference, 1977; Schwartz, G. E., & Weiss, S. M. (1978)]

charter "The interdisciplinary field concerned with the development and integration of socio-cultural, psychosocial, behavioral and biomedical knowledge relevant to health and illness, and the application of this knowledge to disease prevention, health promotion, etiology, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation." [International Society of Behavioral Medicine (ISBM) charter, 2008]

model The biopsychosocial model reframed the focus away from the aberrations that lead to illness and toward the conditions that promote health. [Engel, G. L. (1977); BM Notes (Senior)]

Detailed notes

1. Definition — what behavioral medicine is

  • Behavioral medicine is the interdisciplinary field that develops and integrates behavioral and biomedical knowledge about health and illness and then applies it to prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation. The word "interdisciplinary" is the hook: it is not one profession but many working together. [Schwartz & Weiss, 1978; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The later ISBM charter widens the definition to include socio-cultural and psychosocial (society, culture, mind and relationships) knowledge, and it adds health promotion and etiology (the study of what causes disease) to the earlier aims. [ISBM charter, 2008; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The definition deliberately puts application at the centre: knowledge is not gathered for its own sake, it is turned into real techniques for preventing and treating illness. [Schwartz & Weiss, 1978; Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]
  • In teaching notes, behavioral medicine is described as combining medicine and psychology and drawing on epidemiology, anthropology, sociology, physiology, pharmacology, neuroanatomy, endocrinology and immunology — a genuinely multi-science field. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Its practice is broader than health psychology alone: it also uses applied psychophysiological therapies (treatments that work on body-and-mind processes, e.g. biofeedback, hypnosis), occupational therapy, rehabilitation medicine, preventive medicine, and it stresses the remediation and healing of illness. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Practitioners act as behavioural change agents and include qualified nurses, social workers, psychologists and physicians (including medical students and residents). [BM Notes (Senior)]

2. History and emergence — how the field grew

  • Behavioral medicine has deep roots but only recently became a named field; its boundaries later blurred, a problem teachers call "drift." [BM Notes (Senior); Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Before the 1960s, the dominant view was reductionist (breaking the body into parts) and strictly biomedical, looking at organs and organ systems rather than the whole person. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]
  • Ancient Greek physicians held an interactionist (mind and body influence each other) view; in the 19th century, physicians such as Benjamin Rush reintroduced the idea that psychological and behavioural factors matter for health and disease. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • In the 20th century, psychosomatic medicine (the Flanders Dunbar era), psychophysiology, and conditioning research showed that autonomic responses such as heart rate and temperature can be conditioned (learned). Cohen and Ader even showed immune function could be conditioned. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; Cohen & Ader, 1988]
  • The field was first formulated as a reaction to psychoanalytic psychosomatic medicine and was strengthened by the more experimentally grounded behaviourist movement. [Pomerleau & Brady, 1979; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • The first behavioral medicine research unit was set up by Ovide F. Pomerleau and John Paul Brady at the University of Pennsylvania (1973); another early laboratory was founded by W. Stewart Agras at Stanford. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The 1977 Yale Conference on Behavioral Medicine is the field's formal birthplace; Schwartz and Weiss modelled the term and stressed its interdisciplinary scope. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • "Drift" happened because psychiatry sometimes used the term as a synonym for psychopathology, and complementary/alternative medicine also borrowed behavioural approaches, widening the boundaries of the field. [BM Notes (Senior)]

3. Biomedical versus biopsychosocial model

  • The biomedical model governed thinking for about 300 years. It explains all illness by aberrant somatic processes (faults in the body's physical workings), assumes mind–body dualism (mind and body are separate), stresses illness over health, and treats psychological processes as largely independent of disease. [Engel, G. L. (1977); BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977) says biological, psychological and social factors are all determinants of health. Macro-level (large-scale, e.g. social support, depression) and micro-level (cellular and chemical) processes interact, so mind and body cannot be cleanly separated. [Engel, G. L. (1977); BM Notes (Senior)]
  • It rests on systems theory: every level of organisation is linked in a hierarchy, so a change at one level affects all the others. This is why a social stress can show up as a chemical change, and vice versa. [Engel, G. L. (1977); BM Notes (Senior)]
  • The key shift is of focus: from the aberrations that cause illness toward the conditions that promote health. [Engel, G. L. (1977); BM Notes (Senior)]

4. Health and illness — the key concepts

  • The World Health Organization defines health as "a complete state of physical, mental and social well-being; it is not simply the absence of illness or disease." This matches the field's whole-person stance. [WHO, 1948; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The WHO Ottawa Charter (1986) adds that health is "a resource for everyday life, not the objective of living" — a positive idea that stresses social and personal resources as well as physical capacity. [WHO Ottawa Charter, 1986; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Illness is a pronounced deviation from the normal healthy state and is subjective (judged from the person's own experience), depending on how the individual evaluates their internal state and ability to function. [Dorland, 1965; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Illness is not the same as disease: one can have a disease without feeling ill (asymptomatic, e.g. early high blood pressure), and feel ill without disease (a normal experience that gets medicalised). [Emson & McWhinney, 1987; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Sickness behaviour (the body's evolved "being-ill" response) — lethargy, low mood, loss of appetite, sleepiness, hyperalgesia (feeling pain more strongly) and poor concentration — is useful because it helps the body rest and clear infection. [BM Notes (Senior); Hart, 1988]

5. Scope and related fields

  • Health psychology (APA Division 38) studies the behavioral, cognitive, psychosocial and physiological factors that shape responses to health and illness. Its three working areas are Research, Public Policy, and Clinical work. [APA Division 38; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Its stated aims are the promotion of good health, the prevention of poor health, and maintaining quality of life and well-being even within illness. [APA Division 38; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Psychosomatic medicine is the older, psychoanalytic-rooted tradition that behavioral medicine partly reacted against. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Medical psychology: in some countries a clinical health psychologist with extra training becomes a medical psychologist and can even gain prescription privileges. [BM Notes (Senior)]

6. Boundaries — what they are and why they matter

  • A boundary (the understood limit of a professional relationship) is defined as the "mutually understood, unspoken physical and emotional limits of the professional relationship between the patient and the physician or student, or supervisor and student." [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The essence of the relationship is trust, and trust needs clear boundaries. When the limits change, conduct becomes ambiguous, and unethical or unprofessional behaviour can follow. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Gabbard & Nadelson, 1995]
  • Put simply, "boundaries are the lines we draw between and around things." They tell everyone what is appropriate inside this particular relationship. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Boundaries must be flexible, not extreme: too rigid and the relationship feels cold and inhibiting; too porous or unclear and it becomes unsafe and full of conflict. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Confusion arises when boundaries are unclear, when the two people disagree about them, when there are multiple relationships with the same person (e.g. being both physician and friend, each needing different limits), or when people simply do not know how to create and keep healthy boundaries. [BM Notes (Senior)]

7. Types of boundaries

This is the part most often missed, so cover it fully. Boundaries come in three broad kinds, and then in several specific relationship classes that cause trouble in practice.

  • Physical boundaries — these define where one thing ends and another begins, like the line that marks where one country ends and another starts. In care, this is personal space and physical contact. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Biological boundaries — these differentiate cells, organs and whole organisms from one another; the body itself keeps a boundary between "self" and "not-self." [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Social boundaries — these set the norms and rules for what is appropriate in a particular relationship, conversation or piece of behaviour. The clinician–patient limit is a social boundary. [BM Notes (Senior)]

Within the clinical relationship, certain relationship-boundary classes are known trouble spots (each is a "high-risk situation"):

8. Boundary crossing versus boundary violation

  • A boundary crossing is a minor, sometimes harmless step outside the usual professional frame — for example, a brief, mutual acknowledgement of feelings. It is not automatically wrong, but it should be noticed. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • A boundary violation occurs any time the professional relationship becomes something other than patient–doctor or supervisor–student — for example, a student using the professional role for a tangible or intangible benefit. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The danger is that crossings can slide into violations if they are left unchecked, which is why early awareness matters. [BM Notes (Senior); Gabbard & Nadelson, 1995]

9. The three boundary challenges

  • The limits of caring — boundaries create a "safe space" that is partly physical and partly felt. Too much over-involvement leads to burnout (emotional exhaustion from overwork); walls that are too rigid cut the clinician off from the rewards of caring. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Sexuality and professionalism — sexual feelings are normal and not shameful, but if they are acted on they become exploitative and a serious violation. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Medical advice for family members — treating family means holding two relationships at once, which creates boundary confusion and divided judgment. [BM Notes (Senior); AMA Code of Medical Ethics E-8.19]

10. Warning signs and guidelines

  • Warning signs of boundary confusion include clouded judgment; a request that "doesn't feel right"; a change in the patient's behaviour (more calls, no-shows, personal questions, provocativeness); a change in the clinician's usual behaviour; and fear of hurting or angering the patient. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Guidelines to keep boundaries healthy: recognise the warning signs early; be aware of your own feelings and needs; use a professional support network or Balint group (a peer group where clinicians discuss difficult cases); keep practising the skill; and seek professional counselling if needed. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Self-help tips add: build a support network, know the relevant ethical and legal statutes, listen to your emotional response, and care for your own well-being. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

11. Significance and one-line summary

  • The field's significance is that it brings prevention and health promotion to the centre of medicine, so illness is forestalled and recovery supported, not only diagnosed. [Engel, G. L. (1977); Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • By integrating two traditions it gave scientific credibility to the mind–body link, replacing vague intuition with testable, applicable knowledge. [Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]
  • In one line: behavioral medicine is the interdisciplinary field that integrates behavioral and biomedical science and applies it across prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation, with clear professional boundaries protecting the trust it depends on. [Schwartz & Weiss, 1978]
Common exam pitfall

Do not stop at "a boundary is a limit." The marks are in the types (physical, biological, social, plus the relationship classes), the crossing-vs-violation distinction, and the three challenges with their warning signs.

Definition, boundary & scope at a glance
  • What: interdisciplinary integration of behavioral + biomedical science applied to health and illness.
  • When: defined at the 1977 Yale Conference (Schwartz & Weiss, 1978); broadened by the ISBM charter.
  • Frame: biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) + systems theory, replacing the reductionist biomedical view.
  • Health/illness: WHO 1948 + Ottawa 1986; illness is subjective and not the same as disease.
  • Boundary: the understood limit of the relationship, resting on trust; types = physical, biological, social (+ relationship classes); crossing vs violation.
  • Aim: prevention, promotion, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation.
Mnemonic BRIDGE Behavioral + biomedical · Rehabilitation · Interdisciplinary · Diagnosis · General prevention & promotion · Engel's biopsychosocial frame — behavioral medicine bridges mind and body, held together by clear boundaries.

2 Psychological & behavioral influences on health and illness

What people habitually do — how they eat, move, sleep, smoke and drink, and how they handle stress — pushes their risk of illness up or down. Behaviour acts on the body in two ways: directly (through physiology) and indirectly (through risky habits). A central lesson is that information alone rarely changes behaviour.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Frame it simply — behaviour and mental state are powerful "modifiers" of health, able to protect or harm the body.
  2. Give the background — healthy habits are linked to better health and a longer life.
  3. Explain health behaviour — what it is, and the two assumptions that make it worth studying.
  4. Show the two pathways — the direct (body) route and the indirect (habit) route from stress to illness.
  5. Give the evidence — smoking, alcohol, exercise, diet, sleep, and what stress does to immunity.
  6. Finish with significance — why change is hard, why information alone is not enough, and a one-line summary.

The detailed notes below follow exactly this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

definition Health behaviour is any activity undertaken for the purpose of preventing or detecting disease or improving health and well-being — from using medical services and following a regimen to self-directed behaviours such as diet, exercise and not smoking. [Conner & Norman, 1996; BM Notes (Senior)]

assumptions Interest in health behaviour rests on two assumptions: a large share of mortality is caused by individual behaviour, and that behaviour is modifiable. [Stroebe & Stroebe, 1995; BM Notes (Senior)]

Detailed notes

1. Framing — behaviour and mind as modifiers of health

  • Health is not fixed by biology alone; everyday behaviour and psychological state are powerful modifiers that can protect the body or steadily wear it down. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • People who eat regular meals, keep a healthy weight, do not smoke, drink little alcohol, sleep adequately and exercise are in measurably better health and live longer. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • These habits matter so much because they are largely modifiable (able to be changed), so the same behaviours that raise risk are also the realistic targets of prevention. [Stroebe & Stroebe, 1995; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Psychological stability is health-relevant too: under overwhelming stress, even a previously stable person may develop transient psychological problems. [Berenbaum & Connelly, 1993; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

2. Health behaviour — what it is and what shapes it

  • Health behaviour covers three things: using medical services, following medical advice (compliance), and self-directed behaviours such as diet, exercise, smoking and drinking. [Conner & Norman, 1996; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Its determinants (what shapes it) include demographic, social and emotional factors, perceived symptoms, access to care, personality, and cognitive factors such as beliefs. This is why two people with the same facts behave differently. [BM Notes (Senior)]

3. The two pathways from stress to illness

  • The indirect pathway: stress raises risk behaviour such as smoking and drinking, which then damages the body over time. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The direct pathway: physiological mechanisms (bodily processes) such as raised blood pressure and immune suppression injure health without any change in behaviour, and stress can also trigger or worsen disease in a vulnerable person. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Separating the two routes matters in the exam because it explains why some stressed people fall ill through lifestyle and others through bodily reactions alone. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

4. Evidence — specific behaviours

  • Smoking carries a dose-related (the more you smoke, the higher the risk) rise in lung and other cancers. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Smoking also raises cardiovascular disease and chronic respiratory disease, including emphysema (air sacs in the lungs are damaged and over-stretched, so breathing becomes hard) and chronic bronchitis (long-term inflammation of the airways with cough and mucus). [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Alcohol contributes to accidents (especially driving), liver damage, and harmful effects on the brain, so its cost runs across injury, organ disease and cognition. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Exercise benefits both physical and psychological health, reduces physical problems in the elderly, and even aids recovery after a heart attack — showing behaviour affects both the onset and the course of illness. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • But adherence to exercise is hard, and information alone rarely changes it: beliefs and social networks must also be addressed. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

5. Stress, the body and the immune system

  • During stress the brain's releasing factors drive the pituitary to release ACTH, which makes the adrenal cortex release corticosteroids (stress hormones such as cortisol); adrenaline and noradrenaline also rise, glucose is released, and natural painkillers (endorphins) appear. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • These steroids affect the immune system. Both acute stress (such as exams) and chronic stress (such as long-term caregiving) produce harmful immune changes. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Chronic stress slows wound healing and raises susceptibility to infection — concrete proof that mental state reaches the body. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

6. Personality, symptom-perception and the roles of health psychology

  • People high in negative emotion are not necessarily more disease-prone; crucially they notice bodily changes more and seek help more often, which can look like more illness. [Wiebe & Smith; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Health psychology (APA Division 38) works through three roles that recur across the unit: Research, Public Policy work, and Clinical work. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

7. Significance — why change is hard, and summary

  • Adherence (sticking to the advised behaviour) is genuinely hard, so simply knowing what is healthy does not reliably lead to doing it. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Information alone rarely changes behaviour — a finding central to the whole field, because it justifies structured psychological interventions rather than mere health leaflets. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • In one line: psychological state and daily behaviour shape health both directly (physiology) and indirectly (risk habits), yet information alone seldom changes them. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
Common exam pitfall

Do not reduce this topic to "smoking is bad." The marks are in the two-pathway model (direct vs indirect) and the point that information alone rarely changes behaviour.

Influences on health at a glance
  • Protective habits: regular meals, healthy weight, no smoking, little alcohol, adequate sleep, exercise.
  • Health behaviour: service use + compliance + self-directed behaviours; modifiable and behaviour-driven mortality.
  • Two pathways: indirect (risk behaviour) and direct (physiology — BP, immunity).
  • Evidence: smoking → cancer/CVD/respiratory; alcohol → accidents/liver/brain; exercise → prevention + recovery.
  • Key caveat: adherence is hard; information alone rarely changes behaviour.
Mnemonic SEALS Smoking · Exercise · Alcohol · Lifestyle habits (sleep, weight, meals) · Stress — the behavioral SEALS that guard or breach health.

3 Neuroendocrine, neurotransmitter & neuroimmune responses to stress

Stress is first read by the brain and then broadcast to the body along three systems — a fast nervous route, a slower hormonal route, and an immune route. Together they explain how a psychological threat becomes a real physical change, and how useful short-term adaptation turns into long-term disease.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define stress simply — Selye's idea that stress is the body's general response to any demand.
  2. Give the background — Cannon's fight-or-flight and the two "limbs" of the stress system.
  3. Explain the fast route (SAM) — the nervous, adrenaline route.
  4. Explain the slow route (HPA axis) — the hormonal, cortisol route, step by step.
  5. Name the brain centres — the parts of the brain that switch the stress response on and off.
  6. Explain the immune route (PNI) — how mind and nerves change immunity.
  7. Finish with the disease pathway — homeostasis → allostasis → allostatic load → overload, plus a one-line summary.

The detailed notes below follow this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

definition Stress is the nonspecific response of the body to any demand made upon it, first set out as "a syndrome produced by diverse noxious agents." [Selye, H. (1936), Nature]

model The fight-or-flight / emergency reaction: the adrenal medulla, driven by the splanchnic nerves, pours catecholamines straight into the blood to ready the body for action. [Cannon, W. B. (1914)]

system The HPA axis and the sympathetic-adrenomedullary system together form the two peripheral limbs of the stress system. [Stratakis & Chrousos, 1995]

Detailed notes

1. Definition and the nature of the response

  • Selye defined stress as the nonspecific response (the same general reaction whatever the trigger) of the body to any demand, which is why very different stressors can produce similar bodily effects. [Selye, H. (1936); Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • But the neuroendocrine response is not perfectly uniform: it depends on the stimulus (its nature and intensity), the species, time of day, temperature, prior stress history, age, health and emotion. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The general pathway: a stress signal is recognised by neural centres → the hypothalamus releases releasing/inhibiting hormones → the pituitary responds → peripheral stress hormones are secreted → cardiovascular, metabolic and immune changes follow. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

2. The fast route — the sympathetic-adrenomedullary (SAM) system

  • Cannon's fight-or-flight response works through the adrenal medulla, driven by the splanchnic nerves (nerves supplying the abdominal organs), which pours catecholamines (adrenaline-type hormones, i.e. epinephrine and norepinephrine) straight into the blood. [Cannon, W. B. (1914); Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • These catecholamines raise heart rate and force of contraction, dilate the coronary and skeletal-muscle arteries (more blood to the heart and muscles), reduce blood supply to skin and gut, and speed blood clotting — all preparing the body for sudden effort. [Cannon, W. B. (1914); Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

3. The slow route — the HPA axis, step by step

  • In the HPA axis (hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal chain), the hypothalamus releases CRF/CRH (corticotropin-releasing factor/hormone) plus vasopressin. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • CRH makes the anterior pituitary release ACTH, which drives the adrenal cortex to release cortisol (the main stress steroid). [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Cortisol produces gluconeogenesis (making new glucose for energy) and modulates the immune system, then feeds back to switch the axis off — a self-regulating loop. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The HPA axis (hormonal, slower) and the SAM system (neural, faster) are the two peripheral limbs of the stress system. [Stratakis & Chrousos, 1995]

4. The brain centres that run the stress response

  • Reticular Activating System (RAS) — drives general arousal and anxiety, and modulates limbic and cortical processing. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Locus Coeruleus (LC) (a brainstem alarm centre) — governs threat appraisal, arousal, vigilance, attention, startle and sleep; acute stress raises its activity. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Hippocampus — turns short-term into long-term memory. It is a target of cortisol, and repeated stress can inhibit new neurons and even atrophy (shrink) it, a change linked to PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder — lasting distress, flashbacks and arousal after trauma). [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Amygdala — stores learned fear and sets the emotional value of input, projecting to motor, autonomic and neuroendocrine areas. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Cortex — its appraisal sets the quality and intensity of emotion. Damage here matters: Kluver-Bucy syndrome (after removal of both temporal lobes, the person or animal loses normal fear and reacts abnormally to things that should be threatening) shows loss of fear for threatening cues. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

5. Other hormonal axes affected by stress

  • The HP-gonadal axis is suppressed by stress (reduced testosterone), which is why chronic stress harms reproduction. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Prolactin and oxytocin are rapidly activated by stress; the HP-thyroid axis and growth hormone (with somatostatin inhibiting it) are also modulated. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The vasopressin and oxytocin systems also act as neurotransmitters in social behaviour, circadian rhythm and ACTH release. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

6. The immune route — psychoneuroimmunology (PNI)

  • Psychoneuroimmunology (the study of mind, nerves and immunity together) examines how psychological processes and the nervous and immune systems interact, with the CNS regulating immune activity. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Moynihan & Ader, 1996]
  • Stress hormones (corticosteroids and catecholamines) modulate leukocytes; on balance they suppress immunity, so chronic stress is immunosuppressive. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The immune system has two arms: innate immunity (the fast, general first line — macrophages, neutrophils, recognising common microbe patterns) and adaptive immunity (the slower, microbe-specific response that learns and remembers). [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Direct effects of stress include acute activation then chronic suppression of NK cells (natural-killer cells that fight viruses and tumours), increased latent viral activity, decreased lymphocyte proliferation, and altered cytokine production. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 1994]
  • Classic work showed conditioned immunosuppression — the immune response can literally be learned, proving a mind–immune link. [Ader & Cohen, 1975; Cohen & Ader, 1988]
  • Cytokines (immune signalling chemicals) can activate the HPA axis and cause fever and sickness behaviour, so the immune system also "talks back" to the brain. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

7. Homeostasis → allostasis → load → overload (the disease pathway)

  • Homeostasis (keeping internal variables steady) holds values such as body temperature near 36–37°C using vasoconstriction (narrowing vessels to conserve heat) and vasodilation (widening them to lose heat). [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Allostasis (stability through change) is the body actively adjusting its set-points to meet demand — adaptive in the short term. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; McEwen, 2017]
  • Allostatic load (cumulative wear and tear) builds when mediators (cortisol, adrenaline, cytokines) stay elevated; allostatic overload is the damage stage — atherosclerosis (fatty plaques narrowing the arteries), hypertension (persistently high blood pressure), bone-density loss, cell loss and neural degeneration. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; McEwen, 2017]
  • A hyperactive HPA gives too much cortisol → insulin resistance, visceral fat and immune suppression; a hypoactive HPA allows chronic inflammation and autoimmunity. When one system responds weakly, another over-compensates and raises pro-inflammatory cytokines. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; McEwen, 2017]
  • In one line: stress is relayed by the fast SAM route, the slow HPA axis and immune modulation, which adapt the body short-term but cause disease when overloaded. [Selye, 1936; Stratakis & Chrousos, 1995]

4 Negative affectivity, behavioral patterns & coping styles

Who falls ill is shaped not only by what people do but by their emotional tone, their enduring behaviour patterns (such as Type A and Type D), and the coping styles they use when a stressor appears. These three ideas link personality and emotion to real disease, especially of the heart.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Start with negative affectivity — explain what it is and the key nuance (noticing symptoms vs truly being more ill).
  2. Describe the behaviour patterns — Type A vs Type B, and the newer Type D.
  3. Give the evidence — depression, anxiety and hostility linked to heart disease, plus the famous criticism.
  4. Explain coping — Lazarus's appraisal, then the three coping styles and when each works.
  5. Finish with significance and a one-line summary.

The detailed notes below follow this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

pattern Type A behaviour is marked by ambition, time-urgency, competitiveness, impatience and free-floating hostility; Type B is steadier and under lower stress. [Friedman, M., & Rosenman, R. (1959), JAMA; Friedman (1996)]

process Coping has two components: appraisal — perceiving the stressor and analysing one's ability to deal with it — and the coping effort itself. [Lazarus, R. S. (1966); Lazarus & Folkman (1984)]

Detailed notes

1. Negative affectivity and the key nuance

  • Negative affectivity (a stable tendency to feel unpleasant emotions such as worry, sadness and anger) is associated with poorer health, making emotional tone a genuine variable in behavioral medicine. [Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 2002; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The crucial nuance: people high in negative affect are not necessarily more disease-prone; they tend to notice bodily changes more and seek help more often. This separates true physiological risk from a reporting bias. [Wiebe & Smith; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

2. Behaviour patterns — Type A and Type B

  • The Type A behaviour pattern (Friedman and Rosenman) describes people who are ambitious, rigidly organised, status-conscious, impatient, time-urgent, competitive, "workaholic", and carry free-floating hostility (anger ready to attach to almost anything). [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The Type B pattern is the opposite: steadier, under lower stress, not upset by losing, and more reflective. [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Friedman later distilled Type A into three symptoms: free-floating hostility (often covert), time urgency/impatience, and competitive drive. [Friedman, 1996; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The original 8.5-year study of men aged 35–59 found Type A roughly doubled the risk of coronary heart disease. [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959, JAMA]

3. Hostility and the newer Type D pattern

  • The toxic core of Type A is thought to be hostility, which has three parts: cynical thoughts, angry feelings, and aggressive behaviour; it goes with altered autonomic responses (reduced vagal "brake", sympathetic over-drive). [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Type D ("distressed") personality (negative affectivity combined with social inhibition — feeling bad and holding it in) predicts cardiac events independently of the usual medical risk factors, and is linked to raised resting heart rate and blood pressure, reduced heart-rate variability, and more arrhythmia and ischemia. [BM Notes (Senior); Behavioral Medicine: An Evidence-Based Biobehavioral Approach]

4. Evidence linking emotion to disease

  • Depression is linked to immune changes, raised mortality in medical inpatients, heightened osteoporosis (thinning, fragile bones) risk in women, and decline in muscle strength in men. [Robles et al., 2005; Herrmann-Lingen et al., 2001; Michelson et al., 1996; Rantanen et al., 2000]
  • Anxiety is associated with the development of coronary heart disease, and chronic anger and hostility are risk factors for heart disease and death. [Miller et al., 1996; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • However, later analysis questioned whether Type A is a true mortality risk factor (it was tied to CHD incidence but not clearly to death), so present it as contested, not settled. [Ragland & Brand, 1988, NEJM; Eysenck, 1990]

5. Coping — appraisal first

  • Lazarus said coping has two components: appraisal (sizing up the threat and one's ability to handle it) and the coping effort itself. The same event therefore stresses people differently depending on how they read it. [Lazarus, R. S. (1966); Lazarus & Folkman, 1984]
  • Appraisal is made when we have experienced or anticipate a stressor, or face a chance for mastery or gain; the coping style we pick depends on whether we believe we have the resources to cope. [Lazarus, R. S. (1966)]

6. The three coping styles

  • Problem-focused coping targets the stressor itself — gathering information, planning and decision-making — aiming to change or remove the source of difficulty. It suits controllable stressors. [Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Park et al., 2004]
  • Emotion-focused coping manages the feelings the stressor produces — seeking social support, acceptance, venting. It suits uncontrollable stressors that cannot be changed. [Carver et al., 1989; Park et al., 2004]
  • Avoidant coping (escaping or disengaging — minimising, denying or ignoring the problem) is passive and is often linked to poorer long-term adjustment. [Holahan et al., 2005; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • A related distinction is approach vs avoidance: approach coping engages the stressor, while avoidance/disengagement coping withdraws (wishful thinking, fantasy, distancing, denial) — building a boundary between reality and experience. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

7. Significance and one-line summary

  • Coping style carries clinical weight: flexible, problem-focused coping tends to protect, while rigid avoidance tends to prolong distress. [Holahan et al., 2005; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • In one line: emotional tone (negative affectivity, Type D), enduring patterns (Type A/B, hostility) and coping style together shape both who reports illness and who develops it, especially heart disease. [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984]

  • Type A: hostility + time-urgency + competitiveness → roughly double CHD risk (contested for mortality).

  • Fit: problem-focused for controllable, emotion-focused for uncontrollable stressors.

5 Psychophysiological models of disease

These models explain the exact routes by which stress becomes disease. They range from Selye's three-stage General Adaptation Syndrome, through the diathesis–stress and transactional models, to a sharp four-way taxonomy of how bodily reactivity can cause, worsen or trigger illness.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Open with Selye's GAS — the three stages (alarm, resistance, exhaustion).
  2. Add diathesis–stress — stress triggers illness in someone already vulnerable.
  3. Explain the transactional model — stress as a "deal" between the person and the situation.
  4. Give the four mechanisms — the four ways reactivity turns into disease.
  5. Show the physical pathway — how repeated reactions reset the body toward hypertension and allostatic damage.
  6. Finish with specific disorders and a one-line summary.

The detailed notes below follow this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

model The General Adaptation Syndrome unfolds in three stages — alarm, resistance and exhaustion — as the body first mobilises, then copes, then wears down. [Selye, H. (1936); Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

model In the transactional view, stress exists when a person confronts circumstances that tax or exceed their ability to manage; it is a transaction between the person and the context. [Lazarus, R. S., & Folkman, S. (1984)]

Detailed notes

1. Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)

  • Alarm reaction — the body recognises the threat and the SAM system fires for fight-or-flight (an initial shock, then a counter-shock as defences mobilise). [Selye, 1936; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Resistance — the body adapts and copes with the continuing demand; arousal stays elevated and resources are steadily drawn down. [Selye, 1936; Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]
  • Exhaustion — prolonged or chronic stress depletes resources, leaving the person vulnerable to illness, and in extreme cases this can lead to death. [Selye, 1936; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

2. The diathesis–stress model

  • Diathesis–stress (a pre-existing vulnerability plus a stress trigger) says stress exacerbates or triggers disease in a person who is already predisposed, whether by genes, early experience or prior illness. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • This is why the same stressor makes one person ill and leaves another unharmed — the vulnerability is the difference. [BM Notes (Senior)]

3. The transactional / appraisal model

  • Stress is a transaction between person and situation: it exists when demands tax or exceed the person's ability to manage. [Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Primary appraisal asks "is this a threat?" — an event may be seen as threat (novel/harmful, bad consequences expected), harm/loss (damage already done), or challenge (a chance for gain or mastery). [Lazarus & Folkman, 1984]
  • Secondary appraisal asks "can I cope?" — the person weighs their resources. If resources are adequate → challenge and a better state; if the threat outweighs resources → stress. [Lazarus & Folkman, 1984]

4. The four psychophysiological mechanisms (key taxonomy)

This four-way split is the heart of the topic: it names the four ways bodily reactivity (how strongly the body responds) can turn into disease.

  • (1) Reactivity as a CAUSAL factor — heightened reactivity (blood pressure, gut motility, muscle tension) under repeated psychosocial demand gravitates from acute reactions to sustained pathology, for example persistent hypertension. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • (2) Reactivity as an INHIBITOR of host resistance — stress-induced immune impairment lowers the body's defences and raises susceptibility to invading organisms. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • (3) Reactivity as a DISRUPTIVE factor — in people already diseased (pain syndromes, asthma, diabetes), daily hassles can shift day-to-day clinical status for the worse. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • (4) Reactivity as a TRIGGER of acute events — an intense response can trigger a serious event such as myocardial infarction (a heart attack — blood flow to part of the heart is blocked) or sudden cardiac death in an already-vulnerable person. [BM Notes (Senior)]

5. The physical pathway — reactivity, hypertension and allostatic load

  • Repeated situational blood-pressure elevations can, over time, reset the regulatory set-point toward hypertension (persistently high blood pressure) — a "baseline-reset" model. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Over months, repeated ANS and HPA activation raises blood pressure and drives atherosclerosis (fatty plaques narrowing the arteries), bone-density loss, immune dysregulation and raised pro-inflammatory cytokines — the allostatic-load disease pathway. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; McEwen, 2017]

6. Specific psychophysiological disorders and summary

  • Classic psychosomatic theories include Alexander's specificity hypothesis (specific conflicts produce specific illnesses), accounts of asthma as psychosomatic (now contested), and conditioning explanations. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • In one line: psychophysiological models trace how stress becomes disease — through Selye's three stages, diathesis–stress vulnerability, the transactional appraisal, and the four ways reactivity causes, inhibits defence, disrupts or triggers illness. [Selye, 1936; Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; BM Notes (Senior)]
Common exam pitfall

Students often give only Selye's GAS. The high-mark content is the four psychophysiological mechanisms (causal, inhibitor, disruptive, trigger) and the transactional appraisal model.

Psychophysiological models at a glance
  • GAS (Selye): alarm → resistance → exhaustion.
  • Diathesis–stress: stress triggers illness in the already-vulnerable.
  • Transactional (Lazarus & Folkman): primary (threat/harm/challenge) + secondary (coping) appraisal.
  • Four mechanisms: reactivity as causal, inhibitor of defence, disruptive, trigger of acute events.
  • Physical pathway: repeated reactivity → BP set-point reset → atherosclerosis/hypertension (allostatic load).
Mnemonic CIDT Causal · Inhibitor of host resistance · Disruptive (in the already ill) · Trigger of acute events — the four ways reactivity makes disease. (Over GAS's alarm-resistance-exhaustion backbone.)

6 Theoretical models of health behavior

These models try to predict and explain why people do (or do not) healthy things. They are mostly social cognition models — they say behaviour follows from a person's beliefs, expectations and intentions. The key ones are HBM, TRA/TPB, SCT with Self-Efficacy, HLOC, PMT, the Transtheoretical Model, and HAPA.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Frame it — these are social cognition models that predict health behaviour from beliefs.
  2. Health Belief Model — the six beliefs that push a person to act.
  3. Reasoned Action / Planned Behaviour — how attitudes and intentions lead to behaviour.
  4. Social Cognitive Theory & Self-Efficacy — learning from others, and belief in oneself.
  5. HLOC and PMT — control beliefs, and fear-plus-coping.
  6. Transtheoretical Model & HAPA — the stages of change, and the motivation-then-action split.
  7. Finish with significance and a one-line summary.

The detailed notes below follow this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

model The Health Belief Model predicts health behaviour from perceived susceptibility, perceived severity, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, cues to action and self-efficacy. [Rosenstock, 1966; Becker, M. H. (1974); Janz, N. K., & Becker, M. H. (1984)]

model In the Theory of Planned Behaviour, intention — shaped by attitudes, subjective norms and perceived behavioural control — is the main predictor of behaviour. [Ajzen, I., & Fishbein, M. (1980); Ajzen, 1991]

Detailed notes

1. Framing — social cognition models

  • Most theories here are social cognition models (models that explain behaviour through a person's beliefs and thinking), split into attribution models and prediction models. [Conner, 1993; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Reviews found the most-used theories are the HBM, TRA/TPB, SCT and the Transtheoretical Model, yet only about 45% of studies actually used a named theory. For prediction, the common ones are HBM, TPB, HLOC, PMT and Self-Efficacy. [Glanz et al., 1997; BM Notes (Senior)]

2. Health Belief Model (HBM)

  • Perceived susceptibility — how likely the person thinks they are to get the problem. [Hochbaum, 1958; Rosenstock, 1966]
  • Perceived severity — how serious they believe it would be. Susceptibility + severity together form the perceived threat. [Becker, 1974]
  • Perceived benefits — how effective the healthy action seems; and perceived barriers/costs — the downsides. Benefits minus barriers form the outcome expectation. [Janz & Becker, 1984]
  • Cues to action — triggers that prompt the behaviour, either internal (a symptom) or external (media, a doctor's advice). [Rosenstock, 1966]
  • Self-efficacy and health motivation were added later, because believing you can do it, and wanting to be healthy, both matter. [Becker, 1974; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Criticism: the model is seen as incomplete and can be improved, because beliefs alone do not always predict action. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

3. Theory of Reasoned Action / Planned Behaviour (TRA/TPB)

  • The core idea: intention predicts behaviour. TRA assumes behaviour is under volitional control (we choose it) and that people are rational. [Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Intention is set by three things: attitudes, subjective norms, and (in TPB) perceived behavioural control (PBC) (how easy or hard the person feels the behaviour is). [Ajzen, 1991]
  • Attitudes follow an expectancy-value (likelihood of an outcome × how much you value it) rule from behavioural beliefs; subjective norms = normative beliefs × motivation to comply; PBC = control beliefs × perceived power (skills, emotions, opportunities, barriers). [Ajzen, 1991; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • PBC can also influence behaviour directly, to the extent that it reflects actual control. [Ajzen, 1988, 1991]

4. Social Cognitive Theory and Self-Efficacy

  • Bandura's Social Cognitive Theory explains behaviour through person constructs: behavioural capability, expectations, self-regulation (goal-setting, self-monitoring, self-reinforcement), observational learning (modeling), and reinforcement — all within reciprocal determinism (person, behaviour and environment all influence each other). [Bandura, A. (1986); Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Self-efficacy (belief that you can carry out a given action) is often the strongest predictor; it is described by three expectancy kinds (situation-outcome, outcome, self-efficacy) and measured by magnitude, strength and generality. High self-efficacy means stronger intentions, more effort and more persistence. [Bandura, A. (1977, 1991); Wallston, 1992; BM Notes (Senior)]

5. Health Locus of Control (HLOC) and Protection Motivation Theory (PMT)

  • HLOC (from Rotter's social learning theory) asks where a person feels control lies: internal (in themselves), external (chance), or with powerful others (e.g. doctors). "Internals" are more likely to take health-promoting action. [Rotter, 1954; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • PMT (Rogers) says protection motivation comes from two appraisals: threat appraisal (perceived severity + vulnerability, minus the rewards of the bad habit) and coping appraisal (response efficacy + self-efficacy, minus response costs). Together they steer the person toward an adaptive or maladaptive response. [Rogers, 1983; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

6. Transtheoretical Model (TTM) and HAPA

  • The Transtheoretical Model (Prochaska & DiClemente) describes five stages of change: precontemplation (not yet thinking of changing), contemplation, preparation, action, and maintenance (with 6-month and 30-day markers). Change is a spiral, not a straight line — relapse is normal. [Prochaska/DiClemente; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • It also includes processes of change (e.g. consciousness raising, counter-conditioning, stimulus control, reinforcement management, helping relationships), decisional balance (weighing pros against cons), and self-efficacy/temptation. [Prochaska/DiClemente; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The Health Action Process Approach (HAPA) splits behaviour change into a motivation phase (risk perception, outcome expectancies, self-efficacy → forming a goal) and a volitional phase (planning → initiative → maintenance, dealing with barriers and resources). It stresses that behaviour is governed by the perception of reality, not objective reality. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; Fiske & Taylor, 1991]

7. Significance and one-line summary

  • These models matter because they tell interventions which belief to target — threat, benefit, control, self-efficacy, or the stage a person is in — instead of just handing out information. [Glanz et al., 1997; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • In one line: health-behaviour theories are mostly social cognition models (HBM, TRA/TPB, SCT/self-efficacy, HLOC, PMT, TTM, HAPA) that predict behaviour from beliefs, intentions and stage of change. [Conner, 1993; Ajzen, 1991; Bandura, 1986]

7 Scope & application of psychological principles in health, illness & health care

This topic shows where psychology is actually used in health care: in assessment and intervention, in the branches of health psychology, in doctor–patient communication, in adherence and pain, and in the stresses of hospitalisation and medical procedures. The thread throughout is that psychological input measurably improves recovery and satisfaction.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. State the professional scope — what a health/clinical psychologist actually does.
  2. List the branches — clinical, public-health, community and critical health psychology.
  3. Give the applications — communication, adherence and pain management.
  4. Cover hospitalisation — why admission is a stressor, with the evidence.
  5. Cover specific settings and procedures — ICU/dialysis, sensory deprivation, stressful tests and surgery.
  6. Finish with significance and a one-line summary.

The detailed notes below follow this order.

Detailed notes

1. Professional scope

  • The professional scope covers assessing, diagnosing and intervening in psychological distress and psychopathology; identifying psychiatric disorders; delivering evidence-based interventions; referral; policy advice; designing, managing and evaluating programmes; training and supervising clinical psychologists; ethical research; and giving expert evidence. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

2. Branches of health psychology

  • Clinical Health Psychology (ClHP) — applies health-psychology science to clinical questions and contributes to behavioural health and behavioral medicine; with extra training the psychologist can become a medical psychologist with prescription privileges. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Public Health Psychology (PHP) — works at the population level, allied to epidemiology, nutrition, genetics and biostatistics, targeting at-risk groups. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Community Health Psychology (CoHP) — delivers community-level interventions. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Critical Health Psychology (CrHP) — examines power, social justice and health inequalities. [BM Notes (Senior)]

3. Applications — communication, adherence and pain

  • Doctor–patient communication can be patient-centred (built around the patient's views and concerns) or doctor-centred; patients often misunderstand medical and anatomical terms, so clear language matters. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Adherence improves when advice is tailored to daily life rather than given as a generic instruction. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Pain management uses patient-administered analgesia, acupuncture, biofeedback (learning to control body signals with live feedback) and CBT. Pain anomalies to know: episodic analgesia (feeling no pain despite injury), causalgia, neuralgia, and phantom-limb pain (pain felt in a limb that has been amputated). [BM Notes (Senior)]

4. Hospitalisation as a stressor

  • Admission to hospital is itself a stressor, and the physical and social environment affects recovery — in the classic window-view study, patients with a tree view recovered better than those facing a brick wall. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Hospitalisation forces lifestyle change, measured by tools such as the Hospital Stress Index and the Hospital Stress Rating Scale. [Koenig et al., 1995; Volicer & Bohannon, 1975]
  • Communication is a known weak point: dissatisfaction with communication is common, but an informational visit raised patient satisfaction to about 80%, versus roughly half without it. [Ley et al., 1972; Ley, Bradshaw, Kincey & Atherton, 1976]

5. Specific settings and procedures

  • Intensive and coronary care units (ICU/CCU) and haemodialysis (a machine filtering the blood when kidneys fail) are major stressors; in such settings sensory deprivation or overload (too little or too much stimulation) can cause wakefulness, disorientation and even hallucination. [Goldberger, 1982; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Patients' self-regulatory beliefs about a heart attack in the CCU (its cause, timeline, consequences and controllability) shape how they adjust. [Petrie et al., 1996]
  • Stressful investigations such as the barium X-ray and endoscopy (a camera-tube examination inside the body) cause distress that psychology can ease, and the threat of uncovering a serious condition adds its own anxiety. [Allan & Armstrong, 1984; Johnson & Leventhal, 1973; Weinman & Johnston, 1988]
  • Crucially, a patient's psychological reaction before surgery influences their subsequent surgical recovery, tying mental state directly to physical outcome. [Johnston, 1986]

6. Roles, methods, significance and summary

  • Health psychologists work as consultant, principal, research or in-training practitioners, using diverse methods — RCTs, quasi-experiments, longitudinal, cross-sectional, case-control, qualitative and action research. [BM Notes (Senior)]
  • These findings matter because they show psychological input is not an optional extra but a measurable determinant of recovery, satisfaction and distress in care. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Johnston, 1986]
  • In one line: psychological principles apply across assessment, the hospital environment, communication, adherence, pain and procedure preparation, improving recovery and satisfaction throughout care. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
Scope in health care at a glance
  • Clinical scope: assessment, diagnosis, intervention, referral, programme design, research, expert evidence.
  • Branches: clinical, public-health, community and critical health psychology.
  • Applications: communication (patient- vs doctor-centred), adherence, pain management.
  • Environment: hospitalisation is a stressor; tree-view patients recovered better; informational visit raised satisfaction to ~80%.
  • Settings/recovery: ICU/CCU/dialysis stress; sensory deprivation → disorientation; pre-surgical reaction shapes recovery (Johnston, 1986).
Mnemonic CARES Communication · Assessment & intervention · Recovery & procedure prep · Environment (ward, view) · Scope (branches, research, policy) — health care that CARES psychologically.
