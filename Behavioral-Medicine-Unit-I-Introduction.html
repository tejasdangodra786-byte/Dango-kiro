1 Definition, boundary & scope of Behavioral Medicine

Behavioral medicine is the meeting-point of behavioral science and medicine. It asks a single, practical question: how does the way people act, think, feel and live shape who becomes ill, how ill they become, and how well they recover — and then it turns that knowledge into real techniques for prevention, treatment and rehabilitation. A defining idea of the field is the professional boundary: the understood limit of the clinician–patient relationship that keeps the relationship safe and trustworthy.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Start with a clear definition — give the 1977 Yale / Schwartz & Weiss definition first, then the wider ISBM charter definition, and say what each hard word means.
  2. Tell the short history — how thinking moved from "body only" (the biomedical view) to "body + mind + society" (the biopsychosocial model), with names and dates.
  3. Explain the scope — who works in it, which sciences it borrows from, and which neighbouring fields sit next to it (health psychology, psychosomatic medicine, medical psychology).
  4. Define health and illness — the WHO definition of health, the Ottawa Charter, and why illness is partly a personal judgement, not just a lab result.
  5. Cover boundaries in full — what a boundary is, why it matters, the types, crossing versus violation, high-risk situations, the three boundary challenges, warning signs and guidelines.
  6. End with significance and a one-line summary — why the field matters for prevention and the mind–body link.

The detailed notes below are grouped to follow exactly this order, so you can read top-to-bottom and write the answer in the same sequence.

Characterisations by standard texts

definition "The interdisciplinary field concerned with the development and integration of behavioral and biomedical science knowledge and techniques relevant to health and illness and the application of this knowledge and these techniques to prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation." [Yale Conference, 1977; Schwartz, G. E., & Weiss, S. M. (1978)]

charter "The interdisciplinary field concerned with the development and integration of socio-cultural, psychosocial, behavioral and biomedical knowledge relevant to health and illness, and the application of this knowledge to disease prevention, health promotion, etiology, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation." [International Society of Behavioral Medicine (ISBM) charter, 2008]

model The biopsychosocial model reframed the focus away from the aberrations that lead to illness and toward the conditions that promote health. [Engel, G. L. (1977)]

Detailed notes

1. Definition — what behavioral medicine is

Read the definition slowly, because every phrase in it is doing work. Behavioral medicine is an interdisciplinary (many separate professions and sciences working as one team, not one profession alone) field that develops and integrates behavioral science (the study of how people act, think and feel) and biomedical science (biology and medicine — the body, its organs and diseases) knowledge about health and illness, and then applies that knowledge to four tasks: prevention (stopping illness before it starts), diagnosis (naming the problem), treatment (curing or managing it) and rehabilitation (helping the person recover function afterwards). [Schwartz & Weiss, 1978]

  • The later charter was written by the ISBM — the International Society of Behavioral Medicine (the worldwide professional body that represents national behavioral-medicine societies; its website is www.isbm.info). Its charter widens the definition to add socio-cultural and psychosocial (society, culture, the mind, and relationships between people) knowledge, and it adds health promotion and etiology (the study of what causes a disease) to the earlier aims. In plain terms, the 2008 version says the field is not only about the body and behaviour but also about the social world the patient lives in. [ISBM charter, 2008]
  • The definition deliberately puts application at the centre: knowledge is not collected for its own sake, it is turned into usable techniques. This is what separates behavioral medicine from pure theory — it is a doing field. [Schwartz & Weiss, 1978; Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]
  • Behavioral medicine combines medicine and psychology and draws on epidemiology (the study of how disease spreads in populations), anthropology, sociology, physiology, pharmacology, neuroanatomy (the structure of the nervous system), endocrinology (the study of hormones) and immunology (the study of the body's defence system) — a genuinely multi-science field.
  • Its practice is broader than health psychology alone: it also uses applied psychophysiological therapies (treatments that work on body-and-mind processes together, e.g. biofeedback, hypnosis), occupational therapy, rehabilitation medicine, physiatry (physical medicine and rehabilitation) and preventive medicine, and it stresses the remediation and healing of illness.
  • Practitioners act as behavioural change agents — people whose job is to help a patient change a habit — and include qualified nurses, social workers, psychologists and physicians (including medical students and residents).

2. History and emergence — how the field grew

The short story: for centuries medicine treated the body as a machine and ignored the mind; across the twentieth century, evidence slowly forced the mind and the social world back into the picture, and in the 1970s this crystallised into a named field.

  • Behavioral medicine has deep roots but only recently became a named field; its boundaries later blurred, a problem teachers call "drift" (the edges of the field spread until it was unclear what did and did not count).
  • Before the 1960s the dominant view was reductionist (explaining the whole by breaking it into small physical parts) and strictly biomedical, looking at organs and organ systems rather than the whole person. [Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]
  • Ancient Greek physicians held an interactionist (mind and body constantly influence each other) view; in the 19th century physicians such as Benjamin Rush reintroduced the idea that psychological and behavioural factors matter for health and disease.
  • In the 20th century, psychosomatic medicine (the Flanders Dunbar era), psychophysiology (measuring body responses that go with mental states), and conditioning research showed that autonomic responses (automatic body reactions such as heart rate and temperature) can be conditioned (learned by association). Cohen and Ader even showed that immune function could be conditioned. [Cohen & Ader, 1988]
  • The field was first formulated as a reaction against psychoanalytic psychosomatic medicine (seen as hard to test) and was strengthened by the more experimentally grounded behaviourist movement (which measured observable behaviour). [Pomerleau & Brady, 1979]
  • The first behavioral medicine research unit was set up by Ovide F. Pomerleau and John Paul Brady at the University of Pennsylvania (1973); another early laboratory was founded by W. Stewart Agras at Stanford.
  • The 1977 Yale Conference on Behavioral Medicine is the field's formal birthplace; Schwartz and Weiss modelled the term and stressed its interdisciplinary scope.
  • "Drift" happened because psychiatry sometimes used the term as a synonym for psychopathology, and complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) (therapies outside mainstream medicine, covering biological, psychological, social and spiritual approaches) also borrowed behavioural approaches, widening the boundaries of the field.

3. Biomedical versus biopsychosocial model

This is the conceptual heart of Unit I. The two models are rival answers to the question "what causes illness?" — and behavioral medicine only makes sense once you accept the second answer.

  • The biomedical model governed medical thinking for about 300 years. It explains all illness by aberrant somatic processes (faults in the body's physical workings), assumes mind–body dualism (mind and body are two separate things), stresses illness over health, and treats psychological processes as largely independent of disease. Its strength is precision; its weakness is that it ignores the person who has the disease. [Engel, G. L. (1977)]
  • George Engel's biopsychosocial model (1977) says that biological, psychological and social factors are all genuine determinants of health. Macro-level (large-scale: social support, mood, poverty) and micro-level (small-scale: cells and chemicals) processes interact, so mind and body cannot be cleanly separated. [Engel, G. L. (1977)]
  • It rests on systems theory (the idea that reality is organised in nested levels, from atoms up to societies, each level a "system" inside a bigger one): every level of organisation is linked in a hierarchy, so a change at one level ripples through all the others. This is exactly why a social stress (losing a job) can show up as a chemical change (raised cortisol), and why a chemical change can alter mood and behaviour. [Engel, G. L. (1977)]
  • The key shift is of focus: from the aberrations that cause illness toward the conditions that promote health. Instead of asking only "what broke?", the model also asks "what keeps people well?". [Engel, G. L. (1977)]

4. Health and illness — the key concepts

  • The World Health Organization (WHO) (the United Nations agency responsible for international public health) defines health as "a complete state of physical, mental and social well-being; it is not simply the absence of illness or disease." This matches the field's whole-person stance — health is a positive state, not merely "no disease". [WHO, 1948]
  • The WHO Ottawa Charter (1986) adds that health is "a resource for everyday life, not the objective of living... a positive concept emphasizing social and personal resources, as well as physical capacities." In plain words: health is something you use to live well, not a goal you chase for its own sake. [WHO Ottawa Charter, 1986]
  • Illness is "a condition marked by pronounced deviation from the normal healthy state"; it is subjective — it depends on the individual's own evaluation of their internal state and ability to function. Two people with the same test result can feel very differently ill. [Dorland, 1965]
  • Illness versus disease — these are not the same. Disease (an objective, measurable bodily abnormality) can exist without illness (the subjective experience of being unwell) — e.g. symptom-free high blood pressure — and a person can feel ill without any disease being found. [Emson & McWhinney, 1987]
  • Sickness behaviour (the set of changes an ill body produces to help it recover) is an evolved, adaptive response: lethargy, low mood, loss of appetite, sleepiness, hyperalgesia (increased sensitivity to pain) and poor concentration. These are not weakness — they conserve energy for fighting infection. [Hart, 1988; Johnson, 2002; Kelley et al., 2003]

5. Related fields — how to tell them apart

  • Health Psychology is housed in the American Psychological Association's Division 38 (the APA's specialist section for health psychology). It studies the behavioral, cognitive, psychosocial and physiological factors that shape responses to health and illness. Its three aims are promoting good health, preventing poor health, and maintaining quality of life and well-being within illness, and it works in three areas: research, public policy and clinical work.
  • Psychosomatic medicine is the older, psychoanalytically rooted field that behavioral medicine partly grew in reaction to; it focused on how unconscious conflict could produce bodily disease.
  • Medical psychology — in some countries a clinical health psychologist who takes extra training becomes a medical psychologist and can even gain prescription privileges.

6. Professional boundaries — the full treatment

A boundary is the invisible line that keeps the clinician–patient relationship professional. The whole relationship rests on trust, and trust needs clear limits; when the limits blur, behaviour becomes ambiguous and can slide into unethical conduct. This is the sub-topic examiners most often find thin, so each part below is explained in its own right.

Characterisations by standard texts

definition "Boundaries are mutually understood, unspoken physical and emotional limits of the professional relationship between the patient and the physician or student, or supervisor and student."

image "Boundaries are the lines we draw between and around things." [Gabbard & Nadelson, 1995, JAMA]

Why boundaries matter. They create a "safe space" in which the patient can be vulnerable and the clinician can act in the patient's interest without self-interest creeping in. Boundaries must be flexible, not rigid: too rigid and the relationship becomes cold and inhibiting; too porous or unclear and it becomes unsafe and conflicted.

The three types of boundary. The word "boundary" is used in three senses, and knowing all three shows you understand the concept, not just the clinical rule:

  • Physical boundary (a line in space) — like the border that defines where one country ends and another begins.
  • Biological boundary (a line between living things) — the membranes and structures that differentiate one cell, organ or organism from another.
  • Social boundary (a line of expected conduct) — the norms and rules that say what is appropriate in a particular relationship, conversation or piece of behaviour. The clinician–patient boundary is a social boundary.

When confusion arises. Boundaries become unclear when participants disagree about them, when multiple relationships overlap with the same person (someone is both your patient and your friend, each needing a different boundary), or when people simply do not know how to build and keep healthy boundaries.

Crossing versus violation — a crucial distinction:

  • A boundary crossing (a small, sometimes harmless step outside the usual frame) — e.g. a brief personal disclosure. It may be benign, but it can slide into harm if unchecked.
  • A boundary violation (the relationship becomes something other than patient–clinician) — e.g. using the professional role for personal gain. Violations are harmful by definition.

SituationWhat it means and why it is risky

The three boundary challenges (each a mini-dilemma a clinician faces):

  1. The limits of caring — the safe space is partly physical and partly felt; over-involvement leads to burnout, while rigid walls cut the clinician off from the genuine rewards of caring. The skill is to sit between the two.
  2. Sexuality and professionalism — sexual feelings can be normal, but acting on them exploits the patient's vulnerability and is a serious violation.
  3. Medical advice for family members — treating a relative creates two relationships at once (doctor and family member) and so breeds boundary confusion. [Committee on Bioethics / AAP, 2009]

Warning signs of boundary confusion — clouded judgment; a request that simply "doesn't feel right"; changes in the patient's behaviour (more calls, repeated no-shows, personal questions, provocativeness); a change in the clinician's own usual behaviour; and a fear of hurting or angering the patient.

Guidelines to keep boundaries healthy — recognise the warning signs; stay aware of your own feelings and needs; use a professional support network or Balint groups (small peer groups where clinicians discuss difficult cases and their feelings about them); keep practising the skills; and seek professional counselling if needed. Know the relevant ethical and legal statutes and look after your own well-being. [Gabbard & Nadelson, 1995]

7. Significance — why the topic matters

Behavioral medicine matters because most of today's leading causes of death are tied to behaviour (smoking, diet, activity, stress) and so are modifiable. By integrating mind, body and society, and by guarding the professional boundary that makes care trustworthy, the field turns the mind–body link from a slogan into a practical programme of prevention and treatment. [Stroebe & Stroebe, 1995]

Topic 1 at a glance
  • Definition: interdisciplinary field integrating behavioral + biomedical knowledge for prevention, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation (Schwartz & Weiss, 1978); ISBM charter (2008) adds socio-cultural and psychosocial knowledge plus health promotion and etiology.
  • History: reductionist biomedical view → 1970s biopsychosocial shift; first unit Pomerleau & Brady 1973; 1977 Yale Conference.
  • Two models: biomedical (body only, dualism) vs biopsychosocial (Engel, 1977 — biology + psychology + society, systems hierarchy).
  • Health/illness: WHO (1948) whole-person health; Ottawa Charter (1986) health as a resource; illness is subjective and differs from disease; sickness behaviour is adaptive.
  • Boundaries: three types (physical, biological, social); crossing vs violation; six high-risk situations; three challenges; warning signs; guidelines.

2 Psychological & behavioral influences on health and illness

This topic answers a simple question with large consequences: can the way we behave make us ill or keep us well? The answer is yes. What we eat, whether we smoke or drink, how much we move and sleep, and how we handle stress all feed into who gets sick and who recovers — partly through direct effects on the body and partly through indirect effects on our habits.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Define health behaviour and give the two assumptions that make it worth studying.
  2. List the main behaviours — smoking, alcohol, exercise, diet, sleep — and explain the mechanism by which each helps or harms.
  3. Explain the two routes from stress to illness — the indirect (behaviour) route and the direct (physiology) route.
  4. Add the personality and symptom-perception twist — why some people report more illness without more disease.
  5. Finish with the roles of health psychology (research, policy, clinical) and a one-line summary.

Detailed notes

1. What "health behaviour" means

Health behaviour (any activity a person does in order to prevent or detect disease, or to improve general well-being) covers three groups: medical service usage (physician visits, vaccination, screening), compliance with medical regimens (following a diet, taking antihypertensive drugs), and self-directed behaviours (diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol). [Conner & Norman, 1996]

Interest in it rests on two assumptions: (1) a large share of deaths from the leading causes is attributable to how individuals behave, and (2) that behaviour is modifiable (it can be changed). If either were false, studying health behaviour would be pointless; both are true, so it is central. [Stroebe & Stroebe, 1995]

Why one person adopts a healthy behaviour and another does not depends on many determinants: demographic factors (age, sex, class), social factors, emotional factors, perceived symptoms, access to medical care, personality factors and cognitive factors. [Adler & Matthews, 1994; Rosenstock, 1974; Taylor, 1991]

2. The specific behaviours — and how each works

  • Smoking. Risk is dose-related (the more and the longer you smoke, the higher the risk). It raises the chance of lung and other cancers (uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells), cardiovascular disease, and chronic respiratory diseases such as emphysema (damaged air sacs in the lungs, causing breathlessness) and chronic bronchitis (long-term inflammation of the airways).
  • Alcohol. Harms through accidents (especially driving), liver damage (scarring of the liver, i.e. cirrhosis), and effects on the brain.
  • Exercise. Benefits both physical and psychological health, reduces problems in the elderly, and aids recovery after a heart attack (myocardial infarction — death of heart muscle when its blood supply is blocked). The catch is that adherence is hard, and information alone rarely changes behaviour — beliefs and social networks must also be addressed.
  • Diet & weight. People who eat regular meals, keep a healthy weight, do not smoke, drink little, sleep adequately and exercise are healthier and live longer — the behaviours cluster.
  • Sleep. Adequate sleep is part of the same healthy cluster; too little is linked with worse health.

3. How stress turns into illness — the two routes

This is the pivot of the topic. Stress can harm health in two distinct ways, and a good answer names both:

  • Indirect (behavioural) route. Stress pushes people toward risk behaviour — lighting a cigarette, drinking more, eating badly, skipping exercise — and it is these behaviours that damage the body.
  • Direct (physiological) route. Stress changes the body itself — raising blood pressure, suppressing the immune system, and exacerbating or triggering disease in a vulnerable person. During stress the brain's releasing factors drive the pituitary to release ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone), which makes the adrenal cortex release corticosteroids (stress hormones such as cortisol); adrenaline and noradrenaline are released, glucose is mobilised, and endorphins (the body's natural pain-killers) rise. Steroids affect the immune system, so both acute stress (e.g. exams) and chronic stress (e.g. long-term caregiving) can produce harmful immune change, slow wound healing and raise infection risk.

Note also that under overwhelming stress even a previously stable person can develop transient psychological problems. [Berenbaum & Connelly, 1993]

4. Personality and symptom perception — the subtle twist

People high in negative affectivity (a stable tendency to feel negative emotions such as anxiety, sadness and irritability) are not necessarily more disease-prone, but they notice bodily changes and symptoms more and seek help more often. So higher reported illness does not always mean more actual disease — a point that matters when interpreting who turns up at clinics. [Wiebe & Smith]

5. The roles of health psychology

Health psychologists (APA Division 38) work in three roles: research (finding out what influences health behaviour), public-policy work (advising on programmes and laws), and clinical work (helping individuals change).

Topic 2 at a glance
  • Health behaviour = activity to prevent/detect disease or improve well-being; worth studying because much mortality is behaviour-based and behaviour is modifiable.
  • Key behaviours: smoking (dose-related cancers/CVD/respiratory), alcohol (accidents, liver, brain), exercise (helps but adherence is hard), diet, sleep.
  • Two routes: indirect (stress → risky behaviour) and direct (stress → physiology: ↑BP, immune suppression).
  • Personality twist: high negative-affect people report more symptoms without necessarily more disease.
  • Health-psychology roles: research, public policy, clinical.

3 Neuroendocrine, neurotransmitter & neuroimmune responses to stress

This topic is the body's side of stress. When the mind sees a threat, the brain sets off a chain of hormone and nerve signals that get the body ready to fight or run, and it also talks to the immune system. Done briefly, this is life-saving; done for too long, it wears the body down. Here we trace each pathway step by step, in plain language.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Start with what stress is — Selye's idea that stress is the body's general response to any demand, and that the response is not identical every time.
  2. Describe the two main stress pathways — the fast nerve pathway (SAM) and the slower hormone pathway (HPA), each step by step.
  3. Add the other hormone axes — gonadal, thyroid, growth hormone, prolactin/oxytocin.
  4. Map the brain systems — RAS, locus coeruleus, hippocampus, amygdala, cortex.
  5. Explain homeostasis — how the body holds itself steady.
  6. Finish with PNI — how stress hormones talk to the immune system, and why that links stress to infection and disease.

Detailed notes

1. What stress is — Selye's starting point

Hans Selye (1936) described stress as "a syndrome produced by diverse noxious agents" — in plain words, many different threats produce the same general body response. He called stress the body's nonspecific response (the same basic reaction whatever the cause) to any demand placed on it. [Selye, H. (1936), Nature]

But the hormone response is not identical every time. How strongly the body reacts depends on the stressor's nature and intensity, the species, the time of day, temperature, the person's past history of stress, their age, health and emotion. So stress is a general pattern that is fine-tuned by circumstances. [Kopin, 1995; Pacak et al., 1995]

The overall route is: a threat is recognised by brain centres → the hypothalamus (a small control centre at the base of the brain) releases releasing or inhibiting hormones → these act on the pituitary gland (the master gland just below the brain) → the pituitary drives peripheral stress-hormone release → the body shows cardiovascular, metabolic and immune changes. The two peripheral limbs that carry this out are the HPA axis and the SAM system. [Stratakis & Chrousos, 1995]

2. The SAM axis — the fast "fight-or-flight" pathway

SAM stands for the sympathetic–adrenomedullary system (the sympathetic nerves plus the inner part of the adrenal gland). It was first described by Walter Cannon (1914) as the fight-or-flight response. Trace it step by step:

  1. The brain judges a threat and fires the sympathetic nerves.
  2. These nerves (the splanchnic nerves (nerves running to the abdominal organs)) stimulate the adrenal medulla (the inner core of the adrenal gland, sitting on top of each kidney).
  3. The adrenal medulla pours catecholamines (adrenaline/epinephrine and noradrenaline/norepinephrine) straight into the bloodstream.
  4. These hormones raise heart rate and the force of each beat, widen the arteries to the heart and skeletal muscles (so they get more blood), reduce blood flow to the skin and gut lining, and speed up blood clotting by acting on platelets.

The whole point: in seconds the body is primed to run or fight — more oxygen and fuel to the muscles, less to systems that can wait, and faster clotting in case of injury. [Cannon, W. B. (1914)]

3. The HPA axis — the slower hormone pathway

HPA stands for the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (a hormone chain linking the hypothalamus, the pituitary and the outer layer of the adrenal gland). It is slower than SAM but lasts longer. Step by step:

  1. The hypothalamus releases CRH / CRF (corticotropin-releasing hormone/factor) together with vasopressin (a hormone that also helps the body hold on to water).
  2. These travel to the anterior pituitary, which releases ACTH (adrenocorticotropic hormone) into the blood.
  3. ACTH reaches the adrenal cortex (the outer layer of the adrenal gland), which releases cortisol (the main stress hormone, a type of corticosteroid).
  4. Cortisol then produces its effects: it drives gluconeogenesis (making new glucose so there is fuel for the emergency), shifts the body into a catabolic (energy-releasing) state, and modulates the immune system.
  5. Finally, cortisol feeds back to the brain and pituitary to switch the system off once the threat passes — a built-in "off" switch. [Stratakis & Chrousos, 1995]

4. The other hormone axes touched by stress

  • HP–gonadal axis. Stress suppresses the reproductive axis (for example, lowering testosterone (the main male sex hormone)) — the body "pauses" reproduction during an emergency. [Carstensen et al., 1973]
  • Prolactin and oxytocin are rapidly activated by stress; oxytocin is also tied to social bonding.
  • HP–thyroid axis and growth hormone (GH) are affected too. GH is driven by GHRH (growth-hormone-releasing hormone) and held back by somatostatin (GH-inhibiting hormone); the liver's IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1) helps regulate growth and metabolism.
  • Vasopressin and oxytocin also act as neurotransmitters in the brain, influencing social behaviour, the body clock and ACTH release.

5. The brain systems behind the stress response

Several brain structures shape how we detect, feel and remember a threat:

  • Reticular Activating System (RAS) (a network in the brainstem that controls general alertness) — drives arousal and anxiety and tunes how the limbic and cortical areas process information.
  • Locus coeruleus (LC) (a small blue-tinted nucleus in the brainstem, the brain's main noradrenaline source) — handles threat appraisal, arousal, vigilance, attention, startle and sleep; acute stress raises its activity.
  • Hippocampus (a seahorse-shaped structure for memory) — turns short-term into long-term memory and handles spatial and declarative memory. It is a major target of cortisol: repeated stress can inhibit new neuron growth and even shrink the hippocampus, a change linked to PTSD (post-traumatic stress disorder — lasting distress after trauma).
  • Amygdala (an almond-shaped centre for emotion) — stores learned fear, sets the emotional value of what we sense, and signals the motor, autonomic and hormone systems.
  • Cortex — its appraisal (judgement of meaning) decides the quality and intensity of the emotion. Damage here can remove normal fear: in Kluver–Bucy syndrome (a condition after temporal-lobe damage) animals lose fear of threatening cues.

6. Homeostasis — the body's steady state

Homeostasis (the body keeping its internal conditions steady) means holding key variables inside an ideal range — for example, core temperature around 36–37°C (97–98°F). If you get too hot, blood vessels widen (vasodilation (opening up vessels to lose heat)); too cold, they narrow (vasoconstriction (tightening vessels to keep heat)). The hormone (endocrine) and gland (exocrine) systems work together to keep this balance.

7. Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI) — mind, nerves and immunity

Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI) (the study of how the mind, the nervous system and the immune system influence one another) is the key reason stress can make us physically ill. The link runs both ways: the brain regulates immune activity, and the immune system signals back to the brain. [Moynihan & Ader, 1996]

  • Stress hormones — CRH/corticosteroids and catecholamines — act on white blood cells. On balance, glucocorticoids and catecholamines suppress immunity, so chronic stress is immunosuppressive (it weakens defences over time).
  • Effects include changed activity of NK cells (natural killer cells that destroy infected or tumour cells), more activity of dormant viruses, less multiplication of lymphocytes (immune cells that fight specific invaders), and altered cytokines (chemical messengers of the immune system). [Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 1994]
  • The immune system has two arms: innate immunity (the fast, general first line of defence), which spots invaders using pattern-recognition receptors on cells such as macrophages and neutrophils, and adaptive immunity (the slower, targeted defence that remembers specific germs).
  • Conditioned immunosuppression: Ader and Cohen showed the immune response itself can be learned — pair a taste with an immune-suppressing drug, and later the taste alone dampens immunity. This proved the brain controls immunity. [Ader, R., & Cohen, N. (1975); Cohen & Ader (1988)]
  • Cytokines released during infection switch on the HPA axis and produce fever and sickness behaviour — the body's own feedback loop. PNI links stress to infections, cancer and HIV/AIDS progression. [Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 2002]
Topic 3 at a glance
  • Selye: stress is the body's general (nonspecific) response to any demand; the exact hormone response is fine-tuned by circumstances.
  • SAM (fast): sympathetic nerves → adrenal medulla → adrenaline/noradrenaline → ↑heart rate, fuel to muscles, faster clotting (Cannon, 1914).
  • HPA (slow): hypothalamus CRH + vasopressin → pituitary ACTH → adrenal cortex cortisol → glucose up, immune change, then negative feedback.
  • Other axes: gonadal (suppressed), thyroid, GH/IGF-1, prolactin/oxytocin.
  • Brain: RAS & locus coeruleus (arousal), amygdala (fear), hippocampus (memory, shrinks under cortisol), cortex (appraisal).
  • PNI: brain and immune system talk both ways; chronic stress suppresses immunity (↓NK cells, ↓lymphocytes); links to infection, cancer, HIV.

4 Negative affectivity, behavioral patterns & coping styles

Some people seem to stay well under pressure while others fall ill. Part of the answer lies in personality patterns (such as Type A or Type D) and in how a person copes with stress. This topic explains the emotions and personality styles linked to disease, and the main ways people cope — each defined simply and with an example.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Begin with negative affectivity — what it is and how negative emotions are linked to disease.
  2. Explain the personality types — Type A, Type B, Type C and Type D, each fully, with the research and its criticisms.
  3. Unpack hostility — the "toxic" part of Type A.
  4. Explain appraisal — Lazarus's idea that stress depends on how we judge a situation.
  5. Lay out the coping styles — problem-focused, emotion-focused and avoidant — and when each suits.

Detailed notes

1. Negative affectivity — negative emotions and disease

Negative affectivity (a lasting tendency to feel unpleasant emotions such as anxiety, sadness and anger) is linked to poorer health. [Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 2002]

  • Depression is tied to immune changes, higher death rates in medical inpatients, raised osteoporosis (thinning, fragile bones) risk in women, and loss of muscle strength in men. [Robles et al., 2005; Herrmann-Lingen et al., 2001; Michelson et al., 1996; Rantanen et al., 2000]
  • Anxiety is associated with the development of coronary heart disease (CHD) (narrowing of the heart's own blood vessels).
  • Chronic anger and hostility are risk factors for CHD and death. [Miller et al., 1996]

2. The behavioural patterns — Type A, B, C and D

Type A. First described by cardiologists Friedman and Rosenman (1959), Type A people are ambitious, tightly organised, status-conscious, impatient, time-pressured, competitive "workaholics" with free-floating hostility (general irritability not aimed at anything in particular). Their landmark 8.5-year study of men aged 35–59 found Type A roughly doubled the risk of CHD. Friedman (1996) later boiled Type A down to three core symptoms: free-floating hostility, time urgency/impatience, and competitive drive. [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959, JAMA; Friedman, 1996]

Criticism of Type A. A later re-analysis found Type A was tied to getting CHD but was not a risk factor for dying from it, and other researchers questioned the pattern. So the modern view is that it is mainly the hostility part, not the whole pattern, that harms the heart. [Ragland & Brand, 1988, NEJM; Eysenck, 1990]

Type B is the opposite: relaxed, steady, reflective, not thrown by losing, and under less self-generated stress. [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959]

Type C ("cancer-prone" pattern) describes people who are cooperative, unassertive, patient, and who suppress negative emotions, especially anger — a pattern some researchers have linked to poorer outcomes, though the evidence is weaker.

Type D ("distressed" personality) is the combination of negative affectivity + social inhibition (feeling bad emotions and holding them in around others). Type D predicts cardiac events independently of standard medical risk factors and goes with a higher resting heart rate and blood pressure, reduced heart-rate variability (less healthy beat-to-beat variation), and more arrhythmia and ischaemia. [Behavioral Medicine: An Evidence-Based Biobehavioral Approach]

3. Hostility — the toxic core

Hostility (a mix of cynical thoughts, angry feelings and aggressive behaviour) has three parts: cynical thinking, angry feeling, and aggressive acting. It also goes with altered body regulation — reduced calming (vagal) control of the heart and a dominant sympathetic "accelerator" — which helps explain the heart-disease link.

4. Appraisal — the Lazarus idea

Richard Lazarus (1966) argued that stress is not in the event itself but in how we judge it. Coping has two steps: appraisal (perceiving a stressor and sizing up whether we can handle it) and coping (what we then do). We appraise whenever we meet, or expect, a stressor, or see a chance for mastery or gain. Whether we feel stressed depends on whether we believe we have the resources to cope. [Lazarus, R. S. (1966)]

5. Coping styles — the three main ways

  • Problem-focused coping — tackling the problem itself (gathering information, planning, deciding, acting to change the situation). Best when the stressor is controllable. Example: if you are failing a subject, you make a study timetable and get a tutor. [Lazarus & Folkman, 1984]
  • Emotion-focused coping — managing the feelings (seeking social support, accepting, venting, reframing) rather than the situation. Best when the stressor is uncontrollable. Example: after a bereavement, you lean on friends and allow yourself to grieve. [Carver et al., 1989; Park et al., 2004]
  • Avoidant coping — minimising, denying or ignoring the stressor (turning away from it, wishful thinking). It is passive and, used long-term, tends to make things worse. [Holahan et al., 2005]

A related split is approach vs avoidance: approach coping engages the stressor, while avoidance coping withdraws from it (wishful thinking, distancing, denial that puts a wall between reality and experience).

Topic 4 at a glance
  • Negative affectivity (lasting negative emotion) links to immune change, CHD and higher mortality; depression and hostility are key.
  • Type A (time-urgent, competitive, hostile) ≈ doubles CHD risk (Friedman & Rosenman, 1959); criticised as mainly the hostility matters (Ragland & Brand, 1988).
  • Type B relaxed; Type C suppresses anger; Type D = negative affectivity + social inhibition, predicts cardiac events.
  • Hostility = cynical thoughts + angry feelings + aggression; reduced vagal control of the heart.
  • Appraisal (Lazarus, 1966): stress depends on judging the event vs our resources.
  • Coping: problem-focused (controllable), emotion-focused (uncontrollable), avoidant (passive, usually worse).

5 Psychophysiological models of disease

This topic explains how, exactly, stress turns into disease in the body. It is not enough to say "stress is bad"; the models here spell out the different mechanisms — the stage-by-stage wear of the General Adaptation Syndrome, the "weak spot meets pressure" idea of diathesis–stress, the judgement-based transactional model, four separate ways that bodily reactivity harms health, and the slow build-up called allostatic load.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Start with the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) — Selye's three stages and what the body does in each.
  2. Add the diathesis–stress model — illness needs both a weak spot and a trigger.
  3. Explain the transactional model — Lazarus & Folkman: stress depends on appraisal, not the event alone.
  4. Lay out the four reactivity mechanisms — the four different ways body reactions cause or worsen disease.
  5. Finish with allostasis and allostatic load — how repeated small adjustments add up to long-term damage.

Detailed notes

1. General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) — Selye's three stages

Hans Selye noticed that whatever the stressor, the body passes through the same three stages as it tries to adapt. He called this the General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS) (the body's standard three-stage reaction to prolonged stress). [Selye, H. (1936), Nature]

  1. Alarm reaction. The body first recognises the threat. There is a brief shock phase (resistance drops for a moment as the body is caught off guard) followed by counter-shock (the SAM system fires, adrenaline surges, and the body is primed for fight-or-flight). This is the emergency "all hands on deck" stage.
  2. Resistance (adaptation). If the stressor continues, the body settles into coping: arousal stays high but steadier, and the body draws on its reserves to keep functioning. It looks like it is managing — but it is spending resources to do so.
  3. Exhaustion. If stress drags on, those reserves run out. Resistance falls below normal, leaving the person vulnerable to illness, and in extreme cases the strain can lead to serious disease or death.

The takeaway: the body can handle short stress well, but long, unrelenting stress is what causes the damage, because the adaptation itself is costly. [Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]

2. Diathesis–stress model — weak spot meets trigger

The diathesis–stress model (illness appears when a pre-existing vulnerability is pushed by stress) says disease needs two ingredients: a diathesis (an inherited or acquired weak spot — a genetic tendency, an organ already under strain, a personality style) and a stressor that triggers it. Neither alone is usually enough. Example: two people face the same exam stress; the one with a family history of high blood pressure develops hypertension, the other does not — same stress, different vulnerability.

3. The transactional model — Lazarus & Folkman

The transactional model (stress is a two-way deal between the person and the situation, decided by how the person judges it) says stress exists when a person meets circumstances that tax or exceed their ability to cope. It is not the event by itself — it is the transaction between the event and the person's judgement. There are two appraisals: [Lazarus, R. S., & Folkman, S. (1984)]

  • Primary appraisal — "Is this a problem, and how?" The event can be seen as a threat (danger ahead), harm/loss (damage already done), or challenge (a chance to gain or master something).
  • Secondary appraisal — "Can I handle it? What are my resources and options?"

The balance decides the outcome: if resources seem adequate, the event feels like a manageable challenge; if the threat outweighs resources, the person feels stressed. This is why the same event stresses one person and energises another.

4. The four psychophysiological reactivity mechanisms

Here is the part exam answers usually skip. Physiological reactivity (how strongly the body reacts — blood pressure, gut movement, muscle tension — to psychological demands) can harm health in four different ways. They are separate mechanisms, so treat each on its own.

(1) Reactivity as a CAUSAL factor. Here reactivity directly causes the disease. Some people react to stress with unusually large responses in one system (for example, big blood-pressure jumps). If life keeps triggering that response, the repeated strain can gravitate from short, acute reactions into permanent damage — e.g. years of blood-pressure surges settling into fixed hypertension (persistent high blood pressure). Evidence: people with hypertension show bigger blood-pressure responses to a mental-stress test than people with normal blood pressure.

(2) Reactivity as an INHIBITOR of host resistance and defence. Here reactivity does not cause disease directly; instead it weakens the body's defences, letting invading organisms take hold. The four panels below show how exposure to a germ and the state of the defences combine to decide whether illness follows.

  • Panel A — defences are adequate and beat the germ → no illness.
  • Panel B — stress has impaired the defences, so the same germ now causes illness. This is the key scenario where reactivity alters host resistance.
  • Panel C — the germ is so virulent (powerful) that illness occurs even with intact defences; stress is not the deciding factor here.
  • Panel D — defences are disrupted but there is no germ present, so no illness results.

A newer insight is that infections are not limited to the usual self-limiting illnesses; they may also contribute to conditions like peptic ulcer (a sore in the stomach lining), gastric cancer, dementia, vasculitis (inflamed blood vessels) and insulin-dependent diabetes. [Lorber, 1996; O'Connor, Buckley & O'Morain, 1996]

Supplement: how to tell the four mechanisms apart in an exam
  • Causal — healthy person, reactivity itself creates the disease over time (e.g. hypertension).
  • Inhibitor of host resistance — reactivity weakens defences so a germ can cause illness (the A/B/C/D panels).
  • Disruptive — person is already ill; reactivity shifts the day-to-day course (asthma, pain, diabetes).
  • Trigger — a single intense reaction sets off an acute event (heart attack) in someone already vulnerable.

Quick test: ask "is the person healthy or already sick?" and "does it build up slowly or strike suddenly?" — those two questions separate all four.

(3) Reactivity as a DISRUPTIVE factor. This one applies to people who are already ill (even if not yet formally diagnosed). Here stress reactions shift the day-to-day course of an existing disease, so even small daily hassles can matter. It is especially active in chronic or on-off conditions such as pain syndromes (long-term pain conditions), bronchial asthma (sudden tightening of the airways) and diabetes. The reaction may act directly (e.g. stress tightening the airways in asthma) or by disrupting resistance (as in autoimmune disease).

(4) Reactivity as a TRIGGER of acute clinical events. Here a single, intense stress reaction can set off a sudden serious event — but usually only when severe underlying disease has already made the person vulnerable. The classic example is the heart: an intense reaction can disturb heart rhythm and coronary blood flow and so trigger a myocardial infarction (heart attack) or sudden cardiac death in a susceptible person. What makes this mechanism distinctive is that one occurrence can have serious consequences.

5. Allostasis and allostatic load — the slow build-up

Allostasis ("stability through change" — the body keeps itself stable by constantly adjusting its stress hormones) is healthy in the short term. The problem is cumulative:

  • Allostatic state (mediators such as cortisol, adrenaline and cytokines stay elevated) — the body is running its stress chemistry too high for too long.
  • Allostatic load (the cumulative "wear and tear" of repeated or constant stress responses) — the bill for all that adjusting.
  • Allostatic overload (the point where wear-and-tear becomes real damage) — showing up as atherosclerosis (furred-up arteries), bone-density loss, hypertension, cell loss and nerve degeneration.

Mechanistically, stress mediators act on brain receptors and remodel connections in the hippocampus and amygdala. A hyperactive HPA axis raises cortisol, driving insulin resistance, belly fat and immune suppression; a hypoactive HPA axis can allow chronic inflammation and autoimmunity. When one system under-responds, another over-compensates, pushing up pro-inflammatory cytokines. Over months, repeated ANS and HPA activation raises blood pressure and accelerates atherosclerosis — the disease end of the pathway. [McEwen, B. S. (2017)]

Topic 5 at a glance
  • GAS (Selye): alarm (shock → counter-shock, SAM) → resistance (adapt, spend reserves) → exhaustion (reserves gone, illness).
  • Diathesis–stress: disease = vulnerability (weak spot) + stress trigger.
  • Transactional model (Lazarus & Folkman): stress depends on primary appraisal (threat/harm/challenge) vs secondary appraisal (resources).
  • Four reactivity mechanisms: (1) causal (reactivity → hypertension), (2) inhibits host defence (A/B/C/D panels), (3) disrupts existing disease (asthma, pain), (4) triggers acute events (heart attack, sudden death).
  • Allostatic load: repeated stress → allostatic state → load (wear & tear) → overload (atherosclerosis, bone loss, hypertension).
GATOR

6 Theoretical models of health behavior

Why does one person quit smoking and another not? These models of health behaviour try to answer that by naming the beliefs and thoughts that make a healthy action more or less likely. This section is the one examiners said was "empty" before, so every model is fully unpacked: the core idea in plain words, each building block defined in its own right, a worked health example, the evidence, and the criticisms.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. Set the scene — these are "social cognition models"; most research uses only a handful of them.
  2. Explain each model in turn — HBM, then TRA/TPB, then SCT & self-efficacy, then HLOC, then PMT, then TTM, then HAPA.
  3. For each model give: the core idea, every construct defined, a worked example (smoking, screening or exercise), and where sourced, evidence and criticism.
  4. Compare the models in a table so you can see what each adds.
  5. End with a one-line summary of which belief matters most (self-efficacy keeps reappearing).

Framing: these are social cognition models (SCMs) (models that explain behaviour through a person's thoughts and beliefs). A well-known review found that only about 45% of studies actually used a theory at all, and the ones most used were the HBM, the theory of reasoned action/planned behaviour, social cognitive theory and the transtheoretical model. For prediction specifically, the most widely used are HBM, TPB, HLOC, PMT and self-efficacy. [Glanz et al., 1997; Conner & Norman, 1996]

Detailed notes

1. Health Belief Model (HBM)

Who & when. Developed by US public-health researchers to design health-education programmes. [Hochbaum, 1958; Rosenstock, 1966; Becker, 1974; Janz & Becker, 1984]

Core idea in plain words. People weigh up how threatening an illness feels against whether the healthy action seems worth it. If the threat feels real and the action feels worthwhile, they act.

Every construct, defined:

  • Perceived susceptibility (how likely I think I am to get the problem) — e.g. "smokers like me often get lung disease."
  • Perceived severity (how serious I think the consequences would be) — e.g. "lung cancer could kill me." Susceptibility + severity together = perceived threat.
  • Perceived benefits (how much I believe the action would help) — e.g. "quitting would cut my risk."
  • Perceived barriers (the costs or obstacles I see) — e.g. "quitting is hard, I'll gain weight." Benefits minus barriers = the behavioural evaluation / outcome expectation.
  • Cues to action (triggers that prompt me to act) — internal (a symptom like a cough) or external (a media campaign, a doctor's advice).
  • Health motivation (my general readiness to care about health) — some people are simply more health-minded. [Becker, 1974]
  • Self-efficacy (my confidence I can actually do it) — added later to the model.
  • Modifying variables — demographic, social and personality factors shape the beliefs above.

Worked example (smoking). A smoker quits when she believes she could get lung disease (susceptible), that it would be serious (severe), that quitting would clearly help (benefit), that the downsides are manageable (low barriers), and a nagging cough (internal cue) plus her doctor's warning (external cue) tip her over.

Criticism. The model is incomplete — beliefs alone do not always predict action, and it leaves out habit and emotion. [Behavioural health research reviews in Glanz et al., 1997]

2. Theory of Reasoned Action (TRA) & Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB)

Who & when. TRA by Ajzen & Fishbein; TPB is its extension. [Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980; Ajzen, 1991]

Core idea in plain words. What you intend to do predicts what you do. Intention comes from your attitude, what important people think, and how much control you feel. TRA assumes behaviour is under voluntary control and people act rationally; TPB adds control for behaviours that are not fully voluntary.

Every construct, defined:

  • Intention (your conscious plan to make the effort) — the closest predictor of behaviour.
  • Attitude (your overall good-or-bad judgement of doing the behaviour). It is built from behavioural beliefs using expectancy-value (how likely an outcome is × how good or bad that outcome is, summed over outcomes). Example: "exercise will make me fitter (likely, very good) but tire me (likely, slightly bad)."
  • Subjective norm (your sense of whether important people want you to do it). Built from normative beliefs × motivation to comply, summed over referents: what each important person thinks × how much you want to please them.
  • Perceived behavioural control (PBC) (how easy or hard you think the behaviour is for you). Built from control beliefs × perceived power: the factors that help or block you × how strong each is. These factors are internal (information, skills, abilities, emotions) and external (opportunities, dependence on others, barriers).
  • PBC's direct path — when PBC reflects real control, it can influence behaviour directly, not only through intention.

Worked example (exercise). You intend to jog three times a week because you believe it brings fitness you value (attitude), your partner and friends encourage it (subjective norm), and you have the time, gear and skill to do it (high PBC). High PBC also helps you actually get out the door on a cold morning (direct path).

TRA vs TPB. TRA works for behaviours fully under your control; TPB adds PBC so it also fits behaviours needing resources, opportunities or skill (like quitting an addiction).

3. Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) & Self-Efficacy (SE)

Who & when. Albert Bandura. [Bandura, 1977, 1986, 1991]

Core idea in plain words. We learn behaviour by watching others and from our own experience, and the person, their behaviour and their environment all shape each other. The single most powerful belief is self-efficacy — confidence that you can do the thing.

Every construct, defined:

  • Reciprocal determinism (person, behaviour and environment all influence one another, both ways).
  • Behavioural capability (knowing what to do and how to do it).
  • Observational learning / modelling (picking up a behaviour by watching someone else do it).
  • Expectations (what you think will happen if you act) and expectancies (how much you value that outcome).
  • Self-regulation (managing your own behaviour by goal-setting, self-monitoring and self-reward).
  • Reinforcement (rewards or consequences that make a behaviour more or less likely to repeat).

Self-efficacy in depth. SE has its roots in Bandura's social cognitive theory, where behaviour is a function of incentives and expectancies. There are three kinds of expectancy:

  • Situation-outcome expectancy (belief about how events are linked — "my current lifestyle threatens my health").
  • Outcome expectancy (belief that the new behaviour will reduce that threat — "quitting will help").
  • Self-efficacy expectancy (belief that I can actually perform the behaviour — "I can quit").

Self-efficacy is measured on three parameters: magnitude (how hard a task you feel able to do), strength (how confident you are) and generality (how far that confidence spreads across situations). People with strong self-efficacy form stronger intentions, put in more effort, and persist longer against obstacles — which is why it is the strongest predictor. [Bandura, 1977; Wallston, 1992; Bandura, 1991]

Worked example (exercise). Whether someone sticks to regular exercise depends heavily on how confident they are that they can walk, jog or swim regularly — high self-efficacy turns intention into sustained action.

4. Health Locus of Control (HLOC)

Who & when. Grows out of Rotter's social learning theory. [Rotter, 1954]

Core idea in plain words. Where do you think your health comes from — your own actions, or outside forces? That belief shapes whether you bother with healthy behaviour.

  • Internal HLOC (I believe my own actions control my health) — these people are more likely to take up health-promoting behaviour.
  • External HLOC (I believe chance or fate controls my health) — less likely to act, since "what's the point?"
  • Powerful-others HLOC (I believe doctors/experts control my health) — mixed: it can mean openness to medical advice, or over-reliance on others to "cure" you later.

Worked example. An internal-HLOC patient changes their diet because they believe it is up to them; a fatalistic external-HLOC patient shrugs that heart disease "runs in the family, nothing I can do."

5. Protection Motivation Theory (PMT)

Who & when. Rogers, originally built to explain how fear appeals in health messages work. [Rogers, 1983]

Core idea in plain words. A health message sets off two appraisals — "how bad is the threat?" and "can I do something about it?" — and the balance decides whether you protect yourself.

  • Threat appraisal = perceived severity (how bad the danger is) + perceived vulnerability (how likely it is to hit me), minus the intrinsic and extrinsic rewards (the pleasures or social perks of the unhealthy behaviour) that pull toward the bad habit.
  • Coping appraisal = response efficacy (belief the recommended action works) + self-efficacy (belief I can do it), minus the response costs (effort, money, inconvenience).
  • Protection motivation (the intention to protect yourself) is a positive function of severity, vulnerability, response efficacy and self-efficacy, and a negative function of the rewards of the bad habit and the costs of the good one. For it to kick in, severity and vulnerability must outweigh the rewards of the bad habit, and response efficacy and self-efficacy must outweigh the costs.

Worked example (sun protection). After a skin-cancer campaign, a person uses sunscreen when the fear of cancer (severe + likely) beats the appeal of a tan, and belief that sunscreen works plus confidence in using it beats the hassle of applying it.

6. Transtheoretical Model (TTM) / Stages of Change

Who & when. Prochaska and DiClemente. [Prochaska & DiClemente]

Core idea in plain words. People do not change all at once; they move through stages, and they often slip back before moving on, so change is a spiral, not a straight line.

The five stages, each defined with its marker:

  1. Precontemplation (not intending to change within the next 6 months) — e.g. a lifelong smoker not thinking about quitting.
  2. Contemplation (thinking about changing within the next 6 months) — open to information, but not yet committed.
  3. Preparation (committed to change soon, usually within 30 days) — often has already made small steps or past attempts.
  4. Action (has changed within the past 6 months) — change is new, relapse risk is high, needs vigilance.
  5. Maintenance (has sustained the change for 6+ months) — the change is becoming a habit, relapse risk lower.

The other TTM pieces:

  • Spiral of change (people recycle to earlier stages but keep what they learned).
  • Processes of change (the ten activities people use to move stages — e.g. consciousness-raising, counter-conditioning, stimulus control, reinforcement management, helping relationships).
  • Decisional balance (weighing the pros against the cons of changing).
  • Self-efficacy vs temptation (confidence to resist vs the pull to relapse).

Worked example (smoking). A smoker moves from "not interested" (precontemplation) → "maybe I should" (contemplation) → "I'll quit this month" (preparation) → "I quit last week" (action) → "smoke-free for a year" (maintenance), perhaps relapsing once and recycling.

7. Health Action Process Approach (HAPA)

Core idea in plain words. HAPA bridges the famous "intention–behaviour gap" (people intend to act but don't) by splitting the path into two phases: first you decide, then you plan and do.

  • Motivation phase — risk perception (seeing yourself at risk) + outcome expectancies (belief the action helps) + self-efficacy (belief you can do it) combine to form a goal/intention.
  • Volitional phase — action planning (when, where, how) and coping planning (how to handle obstacles) turn intention into initiative, then maintenance, then recovery after any lapse; barriers and resources shape this phase.

Worked example. You decide to take up running (motivation phase), then plan to run at 7 am in the park and to go anyway if it rains by using the gym (volitional phase) — the planning is what closes the gap between wanting and doing.

Supplement: the expectancy-value idea, in one line

Several models (HBM, TPB, SCT, PMT) share one engine: expectancy × value. You act when you believe an action is likely to produce an outcome (expectancy) and you care about that outcome (value). In TPB this is written as "likelihood of outcome × evaluation of outcome, summed over outcomes." If either part is near zero — you doubt it will work, or you do not value the result — motivation collapses. Spotting this shared engine makes the models far easier to remember.

  • HAPA: motivation phase → intention → volitional phase (action & coping planning) closes the intention–behaviour gap.

7 Scope & application of psychological principles in health, illness & health care

This topic is about what health psychologists actually do. It covers the professional tasks they are allowed to carry out, the four main branches of the field, the everyday applications (talking to patients, helping them follow advice, managing pain), and the special case of being in hospital, which brings its own stresses and chances to help.

Answer flow — how to write this topic (easy language)
  1. List the professional scope — the tasks a clinical/health psychologist is trained and permitted to do.
  2. Explain the four branches — clinical, public-health, community and critical health psychology.
  3. Give the applications — doctor–patient communication, adherence, and pain management.
  4. Cover hospitalisation — its psychological effects, the environment, communication, specific treatments and stressful procedures, with the key studies.

Detailed notes

1. The professional scope — what the work includes

Alongside the general scope of the profession, the following tasks fall within the application of psychological principles:

  • Assessing, diagnosing and intervening in people facing life challenges, developmental difficulties and psychological distress or psychopathology (mental-health problems).
  • Identifying and diagnosing psychiatric disorders and psychological conditions.
  • Applying evidence-based psychological interventions to people with psychological and psychiatric conditions.
  • Referring clients on to other professionals for further assessment or treatment.
  • Advising on policy based on psychological theory and research.
  • Designing, managing and evaluating programmes for psychological and psychiatric problems.
  • Training and supervising other clinical psychologists.
  • Conducting ethical research and providing expert evidence or opinions.

2. The four branches of health psychology

  • Clinical Health Psychology (ClHP) (applying health-psychology science to individual clinical problems) — works one-to-one across the health-care spectrum, using education, behaviour-change techniques and psychotherapy. It feeds both the prevention-focused field of behavioural health and the treatment-focused field of behavioural medicine. With extra training a clinician here can become a medical psychologist with prescription privileges. [Behavioral Medicine: An Evidence-Based Biobehavioral Approach]
  • Public Health Psychology (PHP) (works at the level of whole populations) — allied to epidemiology, nutrition, genetics and biostatistics; targets groups at risk.
  • Community Health Psychology (CoHP) (works at community level) — designs interventions with and for local communities.
  • Critical Health Psychology (CrHP) (examines power, social justice and health inequalities) — asks why health is distributed unfairly and challenges it.

3. Applications — communication, adherence and pain

  • Improving doctor–patient communication. Consultations can be patient-centred (built around the patient's views and concerns) or doctor-centred (led mainly by the doctor's agenda); patients often misunderstand medical and anatomical terms, so clear communication matters.
  • Improving adherence to medical advice — tailoring advice so it fits a patient's actual daily life makes them far more likely to follow it.
  • Managing pain — methods include patient-administered analgesia, acupuncture, biofeedback and CBT. Note the pain anomalies: episodic analgesia (feeling no pain despite injury, e.g. in emergencies), causalgia (severe burning pain after nerve injury), neuralgia (sharp pain along a nerve) and phantom limb pain (pain felt in a limb that has been amputated).

4. Hospitalisation — its psychology

Being admitted to hospital is itself a stressor. Each sub-area below is a place where psychology helps:

  • Psychological effects of hospitalisation. Patients differ in age, personality, circumstances, severity and length of stay, so reactions vary widely — and the reaction may reflect the illness as much as the admission itself.
  • Physical and social environment. The surroundings affect recovery: patients whose window looked out on trees recovered better after surgery than those facing a brick wall. Enforced lifestyle change (losing your normal routine and independence) is a key stressor captured by the Hospital Stress Index. [Koenig et al., 1995; Volicer & Bohannon, 1975]
  • Communication in hospital. Much fear comes from not knowing what is wrong or what will happen. Patients are often dissatisfied with communication; in one study, giving an informational visit raised satisfaction to about 80% — roughly double the other groups. [Ley et al., 1972; Ley, Bradshaw, Kincey & Atherton, 1976]
  • Specific treatments. Settings like ICUs (intensive care units), CCUs (coronary care units) and haemodialysis (a machine filtering the blood when kidneys fail) bring their own stress. Long sensory deprivation or overload can cause wakefulness, disorientation and hallucination. In coronary care, patients hold clear beliefs about the cause, timeline, consequences and controllability of their heart attack. [Goldberger, 1982; Petrie et al., 1996]
  • Stressful procedures. Investigations such as barium X-rays (an X-ray using a chalky liquid to show the gut) and endoscopy (passing a camera on a tube into the body) cause discomfort and also carry the fear of uncovering a serious condition. How a patient reacts to a procedure can influence the outcome, especially recovery from surgery. [Allan & Armstrong, 1984; Johnson & Leventhal, 1973; Weinman & Johnston, 1988; Johnston, 1986]

5. Roles and research methods

Health psychologists work as consultant, principal, research or in-training (assistant) psychologists, and use a wide range of research methods — RCTs (randomised controlled trials — the gold-standard experiment), quasi-experiments, longitudinal and time-series studies, cross-sectional and case-control designs, and qualitative and action research.

Topic 7 at a glance
  • Scope: assess/diagnose/intervene, apply evidence-based interventions, refer, advise policy, design programmes, train, research, give expert evidence.
  • Four branches: Clinical (ClHP), Public health (PHP), Community (CoHP), Critical (CrHP).
  • Applications: doctor–patient communication (patient- vs doctor-centred), adherence, pain management (plus pain anomalies).
  • Hospitalisation: admission is a stressor; environment (tree vs wall), communication (informational visit → ~80% satisfaction), ICU/CCU/dialysis, stressful procedures.
  • Methods: RCTs, longitudinal, case-control, qualitative, action research.
