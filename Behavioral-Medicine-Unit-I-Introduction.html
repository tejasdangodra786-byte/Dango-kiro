1 Definition, boundary & scope of Behavioral Medicine

Behavioral medicine is the interdisciplinary meeting-point of behavioral science and medicine; it asks how the way people act, think and live shapes who becomes ill, how ill they become, and how well they recover and can be treated.

Answer flow — how to write this topic
  1. Definition — the 1977 Yale/Schwartz & Weiss definition, then the broader ISBM definition.
  2. Background — the shift from the reductionist biomedical view to the biopsychosocial model.
  3. Key points — interdisciplinary scope, boundary of the professional relationship, related fields.
  4. Clinical link / evidence — first research unit, WHO health definition, illness as subjective.
  5. Significance — prevention, health promotion and the credibility it lent to mind–body links.
  6. One-line summary.

The points below are grouped to follow exactly this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

definition "The interdisciplinary field concerned with the development and integration of behavioral and biomedical science knowledge and techniques relevant to health and illness and the application of this knowledge and these techniques to prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation." [Yale Conference, 1977; Schwartz, G. E., & Weiss, S. M. (1978)]

charter "The interdisciplinary field concerned with the development and integration of socio-cultural, psychosocial, behavioral and biomedical knowledge relevant to health and illness, and the application of this knowledge to disease prevention, health promotion, etiology, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation." [International Society of Behavioral Medicine (ISBM) charter]

model The biopsychosocial model reframed the focus from the aberrations that lead to illness toward the conditions that promote health. [Engel, G. L. (1977); BM Notes (Senior)]

Exam points

Definition

  • Behavioral medicine is formally defined as the interdisciplinary field that develops and integrates behavioral and biomedical knowledge relevant to health and illness and applies it to prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation. [Schwartz & Weiss, 1978; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The broader ISBM charter widens this to include socio-cultural and psychosocial (society, culture and mind-and-relationship) knowledge, adding health promotion and etiology to the earlier aims of the field. [ISBM charter; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The definition is important because it makes application central: knowledge is not collected for its own sake but is deliberately turned into techniques for preventing and treating real illness. [Schwartz & Weiss, 1978; Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]

Background and emergence

  • Before the 1960s the dominant view of health and disease was reductionist (breaking the body down into parts) and strictly biomedical, focused narrowly on organs and organ systems rather than on the whole person. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]
  • During the 1970s the empirical study of how behavior and illness interact produced a movement that integrated behavioral techniques into medicine and lent fresh credibility to the mind–body relationship. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]
  • Engel's 1977 biopsychosocial model (biology, mind and society together) marked the key conceptual shift, moving attention from what causes disease toward the conditions that actively promote and protect health. [Engel, G. L. (1977); BM Notes (Senior)]
  • A more empirically grounded behaviourist strand of the field developed in parallel, giving behavioral medicine firmer experimental roots alongside its broad biopsychosocial framing. [Pomerleau & Brady, 1979; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Key points — scope, boundary and related fields

  • The scope of behavioral medicine spans improving health and longevity and addressing cognition and lifestyle, so it reaches from everyday habits through to formal medical treatment. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The first dedicated behavioral medicine research unit was established by Pomerleau and Brady at the University of Pennsylvania, giving the field an institutional base for systematic study. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • A boundary (the understood limit of a professional relationship) is the mutually understood, usually unspoken physical and emotional limit between patient and clinician that keeps the relationship safe. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Gabbard & Nadelson, 1995]
  • The essence of the clinical relationship is trust, and clear boundaries are what allow that trust to exist, which is why boundary is treated as a defining concept of the field. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Gabbard & Nadelson, 1995]
  • Health psychology, defined by APA Division 38, is a closely related field studying the behavioral, cognitive, psychosocial and physiological factors that shape responses to health and illness. [APA Division 38; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The stated aims of health psychology are the promotion of good health, the prevention of poor health, and maintaining quality of life and well-being even within illness. [APA Division 38; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Clinical link, evidence and significance

  • The World Health Organization defines health as "a complete state of physical, mental and social well-being," not merely the absence of disease, which fits the field's whole-person stance. [WHO, 1948; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Illness is characterised as a pronounced deviation from the normal healthy state and is subjective (judged from the person's own experience), depending on the individual's evaluation of their internal state and ability to function. [Dorland, 1965; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Because behavior strongly influences who becomes sick and how much, the field treats behavior as a genuine determinant of illness rather than a mere add-on to biology. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The field's significance lies in bringing prevention and health promotion to the centre of medicine, so that disease is forestalled and recovery is supported, not only diagnosed. [Engel, G. L. (1977); Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • By integrating two traditions, behavioral medicine gave scientific credibility to the mind–body link, replacing vague intuition with testable, applicable knowledge. [Comprehensive Handbook of Behavioural Medicine]

One-line summary

  • In one line, behavioral medicine is the interdisciplinary field that integrates behavioral and biomedical science and applies it across prevention, diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation of illness. [Schwartz & Weiss, 1978]
At a glance
  • What: interdisciplinary integration of behavioral + biomedical science applied to health and illness.
  • When: defined at the 1977 Yale Conference (Schwartz & Weiss, 1978); broadened by the ISBM charter.
  • Frame: biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977) replacing the reductionist biomedical view.
  • Boundary: the understood limit of the patient–clinician relationship, resting on trust.
  • Aim: prevention, promotion, diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation.
Mnemonic BRIDGE Behavioral + biomedical · Rehabilitation · Interdisciplinary · Diagnosis · General prevention & promotion · Engel's biopsychosocial frame — behavioral medicine bridges mind and body.

2 Psychological & behavioral influences on health and illness

What people habitually do — how they eat, move, sleep, smoke and drink, and how they handle stress — pushes their risk of illness up or down, acting both directly on the body and indirectly through risky behaviour.

Answer flow — how to write this topic
  1. Definition / framing — behavior and psychological state as modifiers of health.
  2. Background — healthy habits are linked to better health and longer life.
  3. Key points — the direct and indirect pathways from stress to illness.
  4. Clinical link / evidence — smoking, alcohol, exercise and stress effects.
  5. Significance — why information alone rarely changes behaviour.
  6. One-line summary.

The points below are grouped to follow exactly this order.

Exam points

Framing and background

  • Health is not fixed by biology alone; everyday behavior and psychological state work as powerful modifiers that can either protect the body or steadily wear it down. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • People who eat regular meals, keep a healthy weight, do not smoke, drink little alcohol, sleep adequately and exercise are in measurably better health and live longer. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • This cluster of habits matters because it is largely modifiable (able to be changed), so the same behaviors that raise risk are also the realistic targets of prevention. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Psychological stability is also health-relevant, since under overwhelming stress even a previously stable person may develop transient psychological problems. [Berenbaum & Connelly, 1993; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Key points — the two pathways

  • Stress is linked to illness through an indirect pathway, in which it raises risk behaviour such as smoking and drinking that then damages the body over time. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Stress is also linked through a direct pathway, in which physiological mechanisms (bodily processes) such as raised blood pressure and immune suppression injure health without any change in behaviour. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Distinguishing these two routes matters in the exam because it explains why some stressed people fall ill through lifestyle and others through bodily reactions alone. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Clinical link and evidence

  • Smoking carries a dose-related higher risk of lung and other cancers, meaning the more a person smokes the sharper the rise in their cancer risk. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Smoking also raises cardiovascular disease and chronic respiratory disease, including emphysema (damaged, over-stretched air sacs in the lungs) and chronic bronchitis. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Alcohol contributes to accidents, liver damage and harmful effects on the brain, so its health cost runs across injury, organ disease and cognition. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Regular exercise benefits both physical and psychological health, and specifically reduces physical problems in the elderly population. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Exercise even aids recovery after a heart attack, showing that behaviour influences not just the onset of illness but also the course of recovery from it. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Significance — why change is hard

  • Adherence (sticking to the advised behaviour) is genuinely hard, which is why simply knowing what is healthy does not reliably translate into doing it. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Information alone rarely changes behaviour, a finding central to the whole field because it justifies the need for structured psychological interventions. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • This gap between knowing and doing is the practical reason behavioral medicine studies motivation and habit rather than merely handing out health facts. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Because behaviours are modifiable, targeting them offers one of the most cost-effective routes to prevention at a population level. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

One-line summary

  • In one line, psychological state and daily behaviour shape health both directly through physiology and indirectly through risk habits, yet information alone seldom changes them. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
Common exam pitfall

Do not reduce this topic to "smoking is bad." The marks are in the two-pathway model (direct vs indirect) and in the point that information alone rarely changes behaviour.

At a glance
  • Protective habits: regular meals, healthy weight, no smoking, little alcohol, adequate sleep, exercise.
  • Two pathways: indirect (risk behaviour) and direct (physiology — BP, immunity).
  • Evidence: smoking → cancer/CVD/respiratory; alcohol → accidents/liver/brain; exercise → prevention + recovery.
  • Key caveat: adherence is hard; information alone rarely changes behaviour.
Mnemonic SEALS Smoking · Exercise · Alcohol · Lifestyle habits (sleep, weight, meals) · Stress — the behavioral SEALS that guard or breach health.

3 Neuroendocrine, neurotransmitter & neuroimmune responses to stress

Stress is read by the brain and then broadcast to the body along three systems — a fast nervous route, a slower hormonal route, and an immune route — which together explain how a psychological threat becomes a physical change.

Answer flow — how to write this topic
  1. Definition — Selye's stress as the nonspecific response of the body to any demand.
  2. Background — Cannon's fight-or-flight and the General Adaptation Syndrome.
  3. Key points — the HPA axis, brain centres, homeostasis and allostasis.
  4. Clinical link / evidence — psychoneuroimmunology and stress–immune findings.
  5. Significance — allostatic load as the pathway to disease.
  6. One-line summary.

The points below are grouped to follow exactly this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

definition Stress is the nonspecific response of the body to any demand made upon it, first set out as "a syndrome produced by diverse noxious agents." [Selye, H. (1936), Nature; General Adaptation Syndrome]

model The fight-or-flight / emergency reaction: the adrenal medulla, innervated by the splanchnic nerves, pours catecholamines straight into the blood to ready the body for action. [Cannon, W. B. (1914)]

system The HPA axis and the sympathetic-adrenomedullary system together form the two peripheral limbs of the stress system. [Stratakis & Chrousos, 1995; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Exam points

Definition and background

  • Selye defined stress as the nonspecific response (the same general reaction whatever the trigger) of the body to any demand, which is why very different stressors can produce similar bodily effects. [Selye, H. (1936); Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • He described the General Adaptation Syndrome in three stages — alarm, resistance and exhaustion — tracing how the body first mobilises, then copes, and finally wears down. [Selye, 1956/1976; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • Cannon's earlier fight-or-flight account showed the adrenal medulla, driven by the splanchnic nerves, secreting catecholamines (adrenaline-type hormones) directly into the bloodstream. [Cannon, W. B. (1914); Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • These catecholamines raise heart rate and force of contraction and dilate the coronary and skeletal-muscle arteries, preparing the body for sudden physical effort. [Cannon, W. B. (1914); Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

Key points — the neuroendocrine axis

  • In the HPA axis (hypothalamus–pituitary–adrenal chain), a stress signal recognised by neural centres triggers hypothalamic releasing hormones that act on the pituitary. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The pituitary then secretes ACTH, which drives the adrenal cortex to release corticosteroids (stress hormones such as cortisol), producing cardiovascular, metabolic and immune changes. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The HPA axis and the sympathetic-adrenomedullary system act as the two peripheral limbs of the stress system, one hormonal and slower, one neural and faster. [Stratakis & Chrousos, 1995]

Key points — brain centres and balance

  • Several neurotransmitter-linked centres shape the stress response: the Reticular Activating System drives arousal, and the Locus Coeruleus (a brainstem alarm centre) governs threat appraisal, vigilance and startle. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The hippocampus supports memory but can atrophy under repeated cortisol exposure, a change linked to post-traumatic stress disorder. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The amygdala drives fear learning and assigns emotional value, projecting to motor, autonomic and neuroendocrine systems, while cortical appraisal can dampen or amplify the reaction. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Homeostasis (keeping internal variables steady) holds values like body temperature near 36–37°C using vasoconstriction and vasodilation to conserve or shed heat. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Allostasis (stability through change) describes the body actively adjusting its set-points to meet demand, which is adaptive in the short term. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

Clinical link and evidence — neuroimmune

  • Psychoneuroimmunology (study of mind, nerves and immunity together) examines how psychological processes and the nervous and immune systems interact, with the CNS regulating immune activity. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Stress hormones such as corticosteroids and catecholamines modulate leukocytes, so chronic stress tends to suppress immunity rather than strengthen it. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Classic conditioning work demonstrated conditioned immunosuppression, showing the immune response can be learned and influenced psychologically. [Ader & Cohen, 1975; Moynihan & Ader, 1996]
  • Stress–immune findings include suppressed NK cells (natural-killer immune cells), increased latent viral activity and decreased lymphocyte proliferation under chronic stress. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Chronic stress also slows wound healing and raises susceptibility to infection, giving concrete clinical evidence of the mind–immune link. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Significance and summary

  • Allostatic load (cumulative wear from repeated stress) builds when mediators stay elevated, and allostatic overload brings damage such as atherosclerosis, hypertension and bone-density loss. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • This matters clinically because it names the exact bridge by which repeated psychological stress becomes long-term physical disease. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • In one line, stress is relayed through the fast sympathetic route, the slower HPA axis and immune modulation, which adapt the body short-term but cause disease when overloaded. [Selye, 1936; Stratakis & Chrousos, 1995]
Supplement — Selye's three GAS stages in brief

At a glance
  • Definition: stress = nonspecific response to any demand (Selye); fight-or-flight (Cannon).
  • Neuroendocrine: HPA axis → ACTH → cortisol; SAM → catecholamines.
  • Neurotransmitter centres: RAS, locus coeruleus, hippocampus, amygdala, cortex.
  • Neuroimmune: PNI — chronic stress suppresses NK cells, slows wound healing.
  • Pathway to disease: homeostasis → allostasis → allostatic load/overload.
Mnemonic HANDS HPA axis (cortisol) · Allostatic load · Neuroimmune (PNI) · Defence catecholamines (fight-or-flight) · Selye's GAS — stress works through all these HANDS.

4 Negative affectivity, behavioral patterns & coping styles

Who falls ill is shaped not only by what people do but by their emotional tone, their enduring behaviour patterns such as Type A, and the coping styles they use when a stressor appears.

Answer flow — how to write this topic
  1. Definition — negative affectivity and the Type A/B behavioral patterns.
  2. Background — Friedman & Rosenman's original work.
  3. Key points — appraisal and the three coping styles.
  4. Clinical link / evidence — negative emotion, hostility and heart disease.
  5. Significance — symptom-noticing vs true disease-proneness.
  6. One-line summary.

The points below are grouped to follow exactly this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

pattern Type A behaviour is marked by ambition, time-urgency, competitiveness, impatience and free-floating hostility; Type B is steadier and under lower stress. [Friedman, M., & Rosenman, R. (1959), JAMA; Friedman (1996)]

process Coping has two components: appraisal — perceiving the stressor and analysing one's ability to deal with it — and the coping effort itself. [Lazarus, R. S. (1966); Lazarus & Folkman (1984)]

Exam points

Definition and background

  • Negative affectivity (a stable tendency to feel unpleasant emotions) is associated with poorer health, making emotional tone a genuine variable in behavioral medicine. [Kiecolt-Glaser et al., 2002; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The Type A behaviour pattern, identified by Friedman and Rosenman, describes ambitious, time-urgent, competitive and impatient people carrying free-floating hostility. [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • By contrast the Type B pattern describes individuals who are steadier and live under lower stress, giving a useful comparison point in the exam. [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Friedman later distilled Type A into three core symptoms: free-floating hostility, time urgency and impatience, and a competitive drive. [Friedman, 1996; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Key points — appraisal and coping

  • Lazarus framed coping around appraisal (sizing up the threat and one's ability to handle it), so the same event stresses people differently depending on how they read it. [Lazarus, R. S. (1966); Lazarus & Folkman, 1984]
  • Problem-focused coping targets the stressor itself, aiming to change or remove the source of difficulty through direct action. [Carver et al., 1989; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Emotion-focused coping instead manages the feelings the stressor produces, which is adaptive when the situation itself cannot be changed. [Carver et al., 1989; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Avoidant coping (escaping or disengaging from the problem) is the third recognised style and is often linked to poorer long-term adjustment. [Holahan et al., 2005; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Clinical link and evidence

  • Depression is linked to immune changes, raised mortality in medical inpatients, and heightened osteoporosis risk in women, showing how affect reaches the body. [Robles et al., 2005; Herrmann-Lingen et al., 2001; Michelson et al., 1996]
  • Chronic anger and hostility are identified as risk factors for coronary heart disease and death, which is why Type A hostility draws clinical attention. [Miller et al., 1996; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Early research tied Type A behaviour to the incidence of coronary heart disease (narrowed heart arteries), establishing personality as a cardiac risk topic. [Friedman & Rosenman, 1959; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Subsequent analysis, however, questioned whether Type A is a true mortality risk factor, so it should be presented as a contested rather than settled finding. [Ragland & Brand, 1988, NEJM]

Significance — noticing vs proneness

  • People high in negative affect are not necessarily more disease-prone; crucially they notice bodily changes more and seek help more often. [Wiebe & Smith; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • This distinction matters in the exam because it separates genuine physiological risk from a reporting and help-seeking bias in symptom perception. [Wiebe & Smith; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Coping style therefore carries clinical weight: flexible, problem-focused coping tends to protect, while rigid avoidance tends to prolong distress. [Lazarus & Folkman, 1984; Holahan et al., 2005]

One-line summary

  • In one line, emotional tone, enduring patterns like Type A, and coping style jointly shape health risk, though some of the apparent link reflects greater symptom-noticing. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Wiebe & Smith]
Nuance worth a mark

State honestly that Type A's status as a mortality risk factor was later questioned (Ragland & Brand, 1988). Examiners reward this balance over a flat "Type A causes heart attacks."

At a glance
  • Negative affectivity: stable tendency toward unpleasant emotion, linked to poorer health.
  • Type A: hostility + time urgency + competitiveness (Friedman & Rosenman); Type B = steadier.
  • Coping: appraisal (Lazarus) → problem-focused / emotion-focused / avoidant.
  • Evidence: hostility → CHD; Type A–mortality link later questioned.
  • Key nuance: high negative affect → more symptom-noticing, not always more disease.
Mnemonic PEAT Problem-focused · Emotion-focused · Avoidant coping · Type A (hostility) — affect and patterns sit on this PEAT.

5 Psychophysiological models of disease

Psychophysiological models explain the actual routes by which psychological stress is converted into bodily disease, from Selye's adaptation stages through reactivity and transaction to allostatic load.

Answer flow — how to write this topic
  1. Definition / framing — models that map stress onto disease mechanisms.
  2. Background — Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome as the founding model.
  3. Key points — reactivity, diathesis–stress and the transactional model.
  4. Clinical link / evidence — the allostatic load pathway.
  5. Significance — direct vs indirect pathways to illness.
  6. One-line summary.

The points below are grouped to follow exactly this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

model The General Adaptation Syndrome frames disease as the end-point of prolonged stress passing through alarm, resistance and exhaustion. [Selye, H.; BM Notes (Senior)]

model The transactional model holds that stress exists when a person confronts circumstances that tax or exceed their ability to manage them. [Lazarus & Folkman; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Exam points

Framing and background

  • Psychophysiological models exist to specify the mechanism by which a psychological state becomes a physical disease, rather than merely noting that stress harms health. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Selye's General Adaptation Syndrome is the founding model, moving through alarm, resistance and exhaustion, with prolonged stress leading to exhaustion and illness. [Selye, H.; BM Notes (Senior)]
  • The model usefully distinguishes acute (short, sharp) from chronic (long-lasting) stress, since it is sustained demand that drives the body toward the exhaustion stage. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I; BM Notes (Senior)]

Key points — reactivity and transaction

  • The reactivity model (the body over-responding then re-setting) proposes that repeated situational elevations of blood pressure can, over time, reset the baseline toward hypertension. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • In reactivity, the regulatory process effectively adjusts the set-point, so what began as a temporary response becomes a stable pathological state. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The diathesis–stress (vulnerability meets stress) style of reasoning holds that stress exacerbates or triggers disease specifically in a vulnerable individual. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The transactional model locates stress not in the event alone but in the mismatch between demand and the person's perceived ability to manage it. [Lazarus & Folkman; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • This model works through threat, loss and challenge appraisals plus a coping appraisal, so the disease pathway runs through the person's interpretation. [Lazarus & Folkman; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

Clinical link and evidence — allostatic load

  • The allostatic load model treats disease as the cumulative cost of the autonomic nervous system and HPA axis being activated repeatedly over time. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Through this pathway, repeated activation raises blood pressure and accelerates atherosclerosis (fatty narrowing of arteries), a concrete cardiovascular consequence. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Allostatic load also produces hypertension, bone-density loss and immune dysregulation (the control system going out of balance), spreading damage across several systems. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • It is further linked to increased pro-inflammatory cytokines, connecting chronic stress to the low-grade inflammation seen in many chronic diseases. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

Significance — direct and indirect pathways

  • These models formalise the direct pathway, in which physiology such as blood pressure and immunity carries stress straight into disease. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • They also accommodate the indirect pathway, in which stress changes behaviour, and that behaviour in turn produces illness. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Their significance is giving clinicians a testable mechanism to target, so interventions can aim at reactivity, appraisal or load rather than at stress in the abstract. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

One-line summary

  • In one line, psychophysiological models trace disease from Selye's adaptation stages through reactivity and appraisal to allostatic load, each a mechanism linking stress to pathology. [Selye, H.; Lazarus & Folkman; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
At a glance
  • GAS: alarm → resistance → exhaustion (Selye).
  • Reactivity: repeated BP spikes reset the baseline toward hypertension.
  • Diathesis–stress: stress triggers disease in a vulnerable person.
  • Transactional: stress = demand exceeding perceived coping ability.
  • Allostatic load: repeated ANS + HPA activation → atherosclerosis, hypertension, inflammation.
Mnemonic GRADA GAS (Selye) · Reactivity · Allostatic load · Diathesis–stress · Appraisal (transactional) — the five disease GRADA routes.

6 Theoretical models of health behavior

Health-behavior theories try to predict and change what people do about their health, from the beliefs weighed in the Health Belief Model to the staged change of the Transtheoretical Model.

Answer flow — how to write this topic
  1. Framing — the most-used models and why models are needed.
  2. Background — the Health Belief Model.
  3. Key points — reasoned action/planned behavior and social cognitive theory.
  4. Clinical link / evidence — the Transtheoretical Model's stages.
  5. Significance — Protection Motivation and HAPA extensions.
  6. One-line summary.

The points below are grouped to follow exactly this order.

Characterisations by standard texts

model The Health Belief Model holds that behaviour depends on perceived susceptibility, severity, benefits and barriers, later extended with cues to action and self-efficacy. [Rosenstock; Becker, M. H. (1974); Janz, N. K. & Becker, M. H. (1984)]

model The Theory of Reasoned Action holds that intention predicts behaviour and arises from attitudes and subjective norms, extended in Planned Behavior by perceived behavioral control. [Ajzen, I., & Fishbein, M. (1980)]

model Social Cognitive Theory centres on self-efficacy, outcome expectations, observational learning and self-regulation. [Bandura, A. (1986)]

Exam points

Framing and background

  • Reviews of the field found the most-used health-behavior models to be the Health Belief Model, Reasoned Action/Planned Behavior, Social Cognitive Theory and the Transtheoretical Model. [Glanz et al., 1992–1994; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • In the Health Belief Model, perceived susceptibility and severity combine into a sense of perceived threat (how dangerous the illness feels) that motivates action. [Becker, 1974; Janz & Becker, 1984]
  • Perceived benefits and barriers together form the outcome expectation, weighing how worthwhile and how costly the protective behaviour seems. [Becker, 1974; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The model was later extended with cues to action (triggers that prompt behaviour) and self-efficacy, improving its power to predict real behaviour. [Janz & Becker, 1984; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

Key points — intention and learning models

  • The Theory of Reasoned Action holds that behaviour is predicted by intention, which itself arises from attitudes toward the behaviour and subjective norms. [Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The Theory of Planned Behavior extends this by adding perceived behavioral control (how able one feels to do it) as a further predictor of intention and action. [Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • These theories rest on two assumptions: that behaviour is under volitional control and that people act rationally when deciding. [Ajzen & Fishbein, 1980; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • Bandura's Social Cognitive Theory centres on self-efficacy (belief in one's ability to succeed), outcome expectations, observational learning and self-regulation. [Bandura, A. (1986); Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • It adds behavioral capability and reinforcement, stressing that people learn health behaviour by watching others as well as by direct experience. [Bandura, A. (1986); Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Clinical link and evidence — stages of change

  • The Transtheoretical Model (the stages-of-change model) describes five stages: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance. [Prochaska/DiClemente; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • It is driven by processes of change, a decisional balance (weighing pros against cons), self-efficacy and temptation across the stages. [Prochaska/DiClemente; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • Crucially the model views change as a spiral rather than linear process, allowing relapse and return, which fits real-world quitting and dieting. [Prochaska/DiClemente; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Significance — extensions

  • Protection Motivation Theory splits motivation into threat appraisal (severity plus vulnerability) and coping appraisal (response efficacy plus self-efficacy), predicting adaptive or maladaptive responses. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • The Health Action Process Approach (HAPA) separates a motivation phase from a volitional phase, bridging the common gap between intending and actually doing. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • In HAPA, risk perception, outcome expectancies and self-efficacy build a goal, which then needs planning, initiative and maintenance against barriers and resources. [Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]
  • These social-cognition models share a split between motivational and volitional phases, which is the key insight examiners look for across the set. [Fiske & Taylor, 1991; Shreya Ma'am's Behavioral Medicine class notes, Unit I]

One-line summary

  • In one line, health-behavior theories predict action from beliefs, intentions, self-efficacy and stage of readiness, with newer models adding a volitional phase to close the intention–behaviour gap. [Glanz et al., 1992–1994; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

7 Scope & application of psychological principles in health, illness & health care

Psychological principles are applied right across the health-care journey — in the clinic's assessment and intervention, in how hospitalisation is experienced, in communication, and in preparing patients for stressful procedures.

Answer flow — how to write this topic
  1. Framing — the clinical-psychology scope in health care.
  2. Background — hospitalisation as a stressor.
  3. Key points — environment, communication and specific settings.
  4. Clinical link / evidence — named studies of procedures and recovery.
  5. Significance — why psychological input improves outcomes.
  6. One-line summary.

The points below are grouped to follow exactly this order.

Exam points

Framing — clinical scope

  • The clinical-psychology scope in health care covers assessing, diagnosing and intervening in psychological distress (emotional suffering) and psychopathology using evidence-based interventions. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • It further extends to referral, policy advice, programme design, training and supervision, giving the role a system-wide as well as individual reach. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The scope also includes conducting research and providing expert evidence, so the clinical psychologist links practice to the knowledge base of the field. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Background — hospitalisation as a stressor

  • Admission to hospital is itself a stressor, and the physical and social environment of the ward measurably affects how well patients recover. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • A striking illustration is the window-view study, where patients with a tree view recovered better than those facing a brick wall. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • The stress of admission can be measured with tools such as the Hospital Stress Index and the Hospital Stress Rating Scale, giving the concept a concrete form. [Koenig et al., 1995; Volicer & Bohannon, 1975]

Key points — communication and settings

  • Patients frequently report dissatisfaction with communication, identifying how clinicians share information as a key weak point in care. [Ley et al., 1972; Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • A simple informational visit (explaining things in advance) raised satisfaction to roughly eighty percent, against about half without it, showing a strong psychological lever. [Ley, Bradshaw, Kincey & Atherton, 1976]
  • Specialised settings such as the ICU, CCU and haemodialysis (blood-filtering treatment for kidney failure) carry their own distinct stressors for patients. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
  • In such settings, sensory deprivation or overload (too little or too much stimulation) can lead to disorientation and even hallucination. [Goldberger, 1982; Behavioural Medicines Notes]

Clinical link and evidence — procedures and recovery

  • Patients' self-regulatory beliefs about a heart attack while in the coronary care unit influence how they adjust, highlighting the role of illness perception. [Petrie et al., 1996]
  • Stressful investigations such as the barium X-ray and endoscopy (a camera-tube examination inside the body) have been studied as sources of distress that psychology can ease. [Allan & Armstrong, 1984; Johnson & Leventhal, 1973]
  • Crucially, a patient's psychological reaction before surgery influences their subsequent surgical recovery, tying mental state directly to physical outcome. [Johnston, 1986]

Significance and summary

  • These findings matter because they show psychological input is not an optional extra but a measurable determinant of recovery, satisfaction and distress in care. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Johnston, 1986]
  • They justify embedding preparation, communication and environment design into routine medical care rather than treating only the disease. [Behavioural Medicines Notes; Ley et al., 1976]
  • In one line, psychological principles apply across assessment, the hospital environment, communication and procedure preparation, improving recovery and patient satisfaction throughout care. [Behavioural Medicines Notes]
At a glance
  • Clinical scope: assessment, diagnosis, intervention, referral, programme design, research, expert evidence.
  • Environment: hospitalisation is a stressor; tree-view patients recovered better.
  • Communication: informational visit raised satisfaction to ~80% vs ~half.
  • Settings: ICU/CCU/dialysis stressors; sensory deprivation/overload → disorientation.
  • Recovery: pre-surgical reaction influences surgical recovery (Johnston, 1986).
