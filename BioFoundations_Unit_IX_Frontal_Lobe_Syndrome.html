1Frontal lobe syndrome and prefrontal anatomy

Frontal lobe syndrome is the cluster of behavioural changes that follows damage to the frontal cortex, especially the prefrontal cortex, which divides into dorsolateral, orbitofrontal (ventromedial) and medial (anterior cingulate) regions, each tied through frontal–subcortical circuits to a distinct clinical picture: dysexecutive, disinhibited and apathetic.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: what frontal lobe syndrome is and why the deficits are grouped as a syndrome.
  • Anatomy: the frontal lobe's size and gross divisions, then the three prefrontal subdivisions.
  • Key features: the three classic syndrome pictures and the frontal–subcortical circuits.
  • Evidence and examples: the heterogeneity of the syndrome and the common etiologies.
  • Significance: why the frontal lobe is the brain's control and personality centre.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Frontal lobe syndrome is described as a cluster of behavioural symptoms that reliably appear after damage to the frontal cortex, impairing higher functions such as motivation, planning, social behaviour and speech. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Standard text Because the deficits cannot be neatly subclassified, the term is used for an amorphous, varied group of deficits that arise from diverse etiologies, different locations and variable extents of frontal abnormality. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]
  • Classical account The general function of the frontal lobe is the temporal organisation of behaviour: it holds control systems that implement behavioural strategies in response to internal and external cues, now often called executive functions. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Exam points

Definition and the idea of a syndrome

  • Frontal lobe syndrome is the clinical picture that follows damage to the frontal cortex, producing impairments of motivation, planning, social behaviour and language that cluster together often enough to be named as a syndrome. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • It is deliberately called an amorphous syndrome because the deficits cannot be sharply subclassified and arise from diverse causes, locations and extents of damage, so no two frontal patients look exactly alike. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]
  • A key teaching point is that not all frontal patients show all symptoms, and severity varies with the specific region and hemisphere injured, which is why the syndrome is defined loosely rather than as a fixed list. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The frontal lobe is the most recently evolved lobe and makes up about one-third of the cerebral cortex, so it is regarded as the cortical locus of higher learning and the structure that defines the self. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Gross divisions of the frontal lobe

  • The frontal lobe is commonly divided into a precentral (motor) area, a premotor area and a large prefrontal area, moving from behind forward, so posterior regions execute movement and anterior regions control and plan it. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The primary motor cortex sits on the precentral gyrus just in front of the central sulcus and drives voluntary movement, while the premotor and supplementary motor areas select and programme those movements. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The frontal eye fields control voluntary eye movements for visual search, and Broca's area in the inferior frontal gyrus controls the motor production of speech. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The prefrontal cortex is defined by its input from the dorsomedial nucleus (a thalamic relay nucleus whose projection marks out the prefrontal cortex) of the thalamus, and it controls the cognitive processes that select the right action at the right time and place. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

The three prefrontal subdivisions

  • The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (the upper, outer prefrontal region handling planning and working memory) (around Brodmann areas 9 and 46) is the largest prefrontal region and carries the executive functions that use multimodal input to generate appropriate responses. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The orbitofrontal cortex (the prefrontal region above the eye sockets handling emotion and social conduct) (roughly areas 10–14), also called ventromedial, modulates affective and social behaviour, reward, working memory for features, and the discrimination of smell and flavour. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The medial frontal cortex and anterior cingulate (areas 24 and 32), together with the supplementary motor area, govern drive, motivation and the exploration of the environment, drawing on deep limbic connections. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The orbital cortex gains input from all sensory modalities and projects to the amygdala and hypothalamus, giving it a route to influence the autonomic changes that accompany emotion. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

The three classic syndrome pictures

  • A dorsolateral lesion typically yields the dysexecutive syndrome (poor planning, control and flexibility after dorsolateral frontal damage), with poor planning, perseveration and apathy, a picture also labelled pseudodepressed. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • An orbitofrontal lesion typically yields the disinhibited syndrome (impulsive, tactless, socially unrestrained behaviour after orbitofrontal damage) or pseudopsychopathic picture, with impulsivity, tactlessness, emotional lability and poor judgement. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]
  • A medial / anterior cingulate lesion typically yields the apathetic or akinetic picture, with loss of drive, abulia and, in severe cases, akinetic mutism, reflecting a collapse of motivation. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • These are tendencies, not rigid rules, because real lesions cross boundaries and the same patient may show a blend, so the three pictures are best used as organising prototypes. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]

Circuits, etiology and significance

  • Each prefrontal region sits in a frontal–subcortical circuit running cortex → striatum → globus pallidus / substantia nigra → thalamus → cortex, so a small subcortical lesion can mimic a large cortical one. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The circuits act as a filter that modulates the output of the frontal cortex to the motor systems, which is why basal-ganglia disorders such as Huntington's and Parkinson's disease can produce frontal-type deficits. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • Common etiologies include closed-head trauma, neurodegenerative dementias such as frontotemporal degeneration, tumours compressing the frontal lobe, stroke, epilepsy with frontal foci, HIV and multiple sclerosis. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The syndrome matters clinically because the frontal lobe is the brain's 'orchestra leader', directing and coordinating the sensory, motor, cognitive and limbic systems, so its damage disrupts the orchestration of behaviour as a whole. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Its significance is heightened by poor insight: patients often fail to appreciate their deficits, which makes the syndrome disabling and creates a substantial, lifelong need for care and supervision. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
Frontal lobe syndrome at a glance
  • A cluster of behavioural changes after frontal damage; amorphous and heterogeneous, so not all patients show all symptoms.
  • Frontal lobe = about one-third of the cortex; divided into precentral (motor), premotor and prefrontal areas.
  • Three prefrontal subdivisions: dorsolateral (executive), orbitofrontal/ventromedial (emotion, social), medial/cingulate (drive).
  • Three syndrome pictures: dysexecutive, disinhibited (pseudopsychopathic), apathetic (pseudodepressed).
  • Frontal–subcortical circuits mean subcortical lesions mimic cortical ones; many etiologies; poor insight disables.
Mnemonic
DOM
Dorsolateral (executive / dysexecutive), Orbitofrontal (emotion & social / disinhibited), Medial-cingulate (drive / apathetic) — the three prefrontal subdivisions and the three syndrome pictures; all loop through the frontal–subcortical circuits, and the frontal lobe is the brain's orchestra leader.

2Disturbances of regulatory (executive) functions

The frontal lobes regulate goal-directed behaviour through the executive functions — planning, initiation, working memory, set-shifting, inhibition and abstraction — and dorsolateral damage produces a dysexecutive syndrome marked by perseveration, poor strategy and a striking dissociation between knowing a rule and being able to use it.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: what the regulatory or executive functions are and where they sit.
  • Background: the frontal lobe as the central executive / supervisory system.
  • Key deficits: planning, initiation, working memory, set-shifting, inhibition, perseveration, abstraction.
  • Evidence and examples: Wisconsin Card Sorting, the knowing–doing dissociation, real-life tasks.
  • Assessment and significance: the tests and why executive loss is so disabling.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text The executive functions are the higher-order processes essential for planning, setting goals, and regulating and verifying behaviour, including organisation, initiation, reasoning, judgement, abstraction and cognitive flexibility. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Classical account Frontal (specifically prefrontal) dysfunction has been characterised as a dysexecutive syndrome involving disruption of the central executive of working memory, the component that controls resources and monitors information processing. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Classical account Luria described a dissociation between knowing and doing after frontal damage, such that verbalisation no longer controls active behaviour — the patient can state the rule but cannot use it to steer action. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]

Exam points

Definition and the central executive

  • The regulatory or executive functions are the control processes that organise, direct and verify goal-directed behaviour, and they are the special province of the prefrontal cortex. [Joaquin M. Fuster, The Prefrontal Cortex]
  • They can be thought of as a central executive that allocates attentional resources and monitors ongoing processing, so frontal damage is aptly named a dysexecutive syndrome. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Because these functions supervise other systems rather than carrying out one task, their loss leaves basic perception and movement intact while wrecking the orchestration of behaviour over time. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Planning, initiation and goal-directed behaviour

  • Frontal patients are especially impaired at devising novel plans or strategies, and sequencing a chain of actions toward a goal is perhaps the behaviour most clearly disrupted in them. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Many show a loss of initiation and spontaneity, speaking little, producing few responses, and having difficulty getting out of bed, dressing or starting the day's activities. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The temporal organisation of a real errand — planning ahead, selecting from options, and persisting — is exactly what frontal patients cannot manage, as in Kolb and Whishaw's shopping-trip example. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • They tend to conceive activities impulsively but rarely complete them, beginning chores at once without first planning how to carry them out. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Working memory, set-shifting and inhibition

  • Working memory (holding and manipulating information in mind for a few seconds) is disrupted, so frontal patients fail delayed-response and delayed-alternation tasks that require them to hold information across a gap. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • They show poor set-shifting (switching flexibly from one rule or category to another), an inflexibility in switching cognitive sets that leaves them locked into an outdated response. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Response inhibition fails, so the patient cannot suppress an automatic or prepotent response, which is seen in poor performance on go/no-go and Stroop tasks. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Impaired inhibition can extend to utilisation behaviour (automatically using an object just because it is in reach), in which the sight of an object becomes an order to use it. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Perseveration and abstraction

  • Perseveration (getting stuck and repeating the same response when a new one is needed) is a hallmark error, seen when a patient keeps drawing the same figure, repeats a rule, or cannot abandon a sorting category. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • Abstract thought is impaired, and loss of the abstract attitude was regarded as an early sign of frontal damage, so proverbs are interpreted concretely and concepts are hard to form. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Patients also struggle with rule deduction and rule following, failing to identify the rule, to use feedback, or to switch set, which is captured in their poor Wisconsin Card Sorting performance. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Impaired associative learning means they cannot reliably learn to pair a stimulus with a response or learn from experience, as in Luria's patients who could not learn to respond to coloured lights. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Evidence, assessment and significance

  • The clearest evidence is the knowing–doing dissociation: patients can verbalise a task and know their errors, yet cannot use that knowledge to modify their behaviour. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]
  • In Shallice and Burgess's errands study, frontal patients were inefficient and broke rules (such as entering unnecessary shops) even though, when quizzed, they understood the task and tried to comply. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Executive function is assessed with the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test (set-shifting and perseveration), Tower tests and maze tests (planning), verbal fluency (initiation) and the Stroop test (inhibition). [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Significance is underlined by a paradox of everyday life: many patients score near-normally on standard tests yet cannot plan and organise daily activities, so the deficit is often missed by conventional testing. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
Executive disturbance at a glance
  • Executive functions = planning, initiation, working memory, set-shifting, inhibition, abstraction; the prefrontal 'central executive'.
  • Dorsolateral damage → dysexecutive syndrome: poor novel plans, lost initiation, impulsive uncompleted actions.
  • Hallmark errors: perseveration, inflexible set, failed inhibition, concrete thinking, poor rule use.
  • Core sign: a dissociation between knowing and doing — can state the rule, cannot use it to steer behaviour.
  • Tests: Wisconsin Card Sorting, Tower of London, mazes, verbal fluency, Stroop; often missed on routine testing.
Mnemonic
PISA-IP
Planning, Initiation, Set-shifting, Abstraction, Inhibition, Perseveration — the executive functions the dorsolateral frontal lobe regulates and the errors that appear when it is damaged; remember the Wisconsin Card Sort and Luria's knowing–doing dissociation as the signature evidence.

3Disturbances of attentional processes

The frontal systems provide the top-down, supervisory control of attention, so frontal damage leaves the patient with impaired attentional capacity, poor concentration and high distractibility, affecting sustained, selective, divided and switching attention and the ability to resolve response conflict.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: attention and the frontal lobe's role in its top-down control.
  • Background: the supervisory attentional system and its link to executive function.
  • Key deficits: sustained, selective, divided and switching attention; distractibility.
  • Evidence and examples: distractibility, Stroop interference, released parietal activity.
  • Assessment and significance: the attention tests and why attention underpins the rest.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Frontal lobe patients appear to have impaired attentional capacity: they show poor concentration and are highly distractible, so attention is counted among the cognitive symptoms of the syndrome. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Classical account The frontal lobes exert a supervisory, top-down control over attention, selecting what to attend to and holding focus, rather than performing the basic registration of stimuli themselves. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Classical account Utilisation behaviour is read as a release of parietally-driven responses from frontal inhibition, so the patient becomes captured by whatever external stimulus is present. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Exam points

Definition and the frontal role

  • Attention is the selection and maintenance of processing on relevant information, and the frontal lobes supply its top-down, effortful control rather than its automatic capture. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Attention and concentration are listed among the cognitive and executive functions of the prefrontal cortex, so they break down together with planning and working memory. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Because frontal attention is supervisory, its loss does not blind or deafen the patient but leaves them unable to steer and sustain the spotlight of attention where it is needed. [Joaquin M. Fuster, The Prefrontal Cortex]

The kinds of attention affected

  • Sustained attention (keeping focus on a task over a stretch of time), or vigilance, is impaired, so the patient cannot hold concentration on a continuous task and lapses as it goes on. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Selective attention (focusing on a target while ignoring competing distractors) is disturbed, and frontal patients are described as showing difficulty with selective attention and being highly distractible. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Divided attention (sharing attention between two tasks at once) suffers, so performance collapses when the patient must track two streams or do two things together. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Attentional switching between tasks or sets is impaired, linking the attention deficit directly to the set-shifting failure of the dysexecutive syndrome. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]

Distractibility and conflict

  • The most visible everyday sign is high distractibility: the patient's attention is pulled to irrelevant stimuli, so they cannot persist in the task at hand. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • They show difficulty in resolving response conflict, which is why they perform worse than patients with posterior lesions on the Stroop test, where a prepotent reading response must be suppressed. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Attentional capture can shade into utilisation and imitation behaviour, in which the sight of an object or gesture drives an automatic response because frontal control over parietal activity is released. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • There may also be a delayed habituation to external stimuli after cingulate damage, so the patient keeps responding to a stimulus that should have faded from attention. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]

Assessment and significance

  • Sustained attention is assessed with the Continuous Performance Test and Digit Span, which probe vigilance and the simple holding of information. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Selective, divided and switching attention are probed by the Test of Everyday Attention and the Trail Making Test, the latter also tapping visual scanning and executive control. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The Stroop Test measures freedom from distractibility, selective attention and the ability to resolve response conflict, and it is a classic marker of frontal, as opposed to posterior, damage. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Attention matters because it is the gateway to the other functions: poor sustained and selective attention undermines memory encoding, learning and the execution of any multi-step plan. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • The deficit also explains part of the real-life disability, since a highly distractible patient cannot see a daily task through from start to finish even when they understand it. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
Attentional disturbance at a glance
  • The frontal lobe supplies top-down, supervisory control of attention, not basic sensory registration.
  • Impaired across sustained (vigilance), selective, divided and switching attention.
  • Signature signs: poor concentration, high distractibility, failure to resolve conflict (Stroop).
  • Links to utilisation / imitation behaviour — attention captured by external stimuli when frontal control is released.
  • Tests: CPT, Digit Span, Trail Making, Test of Everyday Attention, Stroop; attention gates memory and executive tasks.
Mnemonic
SSDS
Sustained, Selective, Divided, Switching — the four kinds of attention the frontal supervisory system controls; when it fails the patient becomes DISTRACTIBLE and cannot resolve conflict (think Stroop), which is why attention is the gateway deficit of the syndrome.

4Disturbances of emotion, personality and social conduct

Frontal damage, especially of the orbitofrontal and ventromedial cortex, changes emotion, personality and social conduct, producing either a disinhibited pseudopsychopathic picture or an apathetic pseudodepressed one; the case of Phineas Gage and the somatic-marker idea capture how lost emotional signals wreck judgement though intellect is spared.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: the frontal lobe's role in emotion, personality and social behaviour.
  • Anatomy: the orbitofrontal / ventromedial and dorsolateral contributions.
  • Key features: the two frontal personalities — disinhibited versus apathetic.
  • Evidence and examples: Phineas Gage, impaired empathy and theory of mind, the somatic-marker idea.
  • Assessment and significance: the tests and the social disability produced.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Blumer and Benson distinguished two frontal-lobe personalities: an orbitofrontal pseudopsychopathic picture of impulsivity and disinhibition, and a dorsolateral pseudodepressed picture of apathy and inertia. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]
  • Classical account The case of Phineas Gage (Harlow, 1848 and 1868) was the first report of personality change following frontal damage: after a tamping iron passed through his frontal lobes he recovered physically but became profane, capricious and 'no longer Gage'. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Classical account Orbitofrontal patients are notorious for social gaffes: they struggle to choose context-appropriate behaviour and to use feedback from the social environment to adjust it. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Exam points

Definition and anatomy

  • The frontal lobe governs personality, emotional regulation, impulse control and social behaviour, so it is often described as the structure that defines the self. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The orbitofrontal cortex (the prefrontal region above the eye sockets handling emotion and social conduct) and the adjacent ventromedial cortex modulate affect, reward and social conduct through their links to the amygdala and hypothalamus. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Because the orbital cortex projects to the autonomic system, it couples the bodily changes of emotion to events, giving it a central role in emotional responding. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

The disinhibited (pseudopsychopathic) picture

  • An orbitofrontal lesion tends to produce impulsivity, disinhibition and a lack of tact and restraint, a presentation labelled pseudopsychopathic. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]
  • Features include emotional lability, irritability and aggression, increased motor activity, poor judgement, risk-taking and rule-breaking, and coarse or inappropriate language. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Patients show decreased concern with propriety, facetiousness, egocentricity and inappropriate social comments, jokes and sexual behaviour such as reduced inhibition. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • This disinhibition is a release phenomenon, a loss of the frontal brake over behaviour, rather than a gain of any new drive. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]

The apathetic (pseudodepressed) picture

  • A dorsolateral or medial lesion tends to produce apathy, indifference and inertia, a presentation labelled pseudodepressed because it mimics depression without the sustained low mood. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Features include reduced motivation, loss of initiative, aspontaneity and little overt emotion, with slowed thinking and reduced verbal output. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • At its extreme the medial picture becomes akinetic mutism (a state of being awake but neither moving nor speaking spontaneously), reflecting a profound collapse of drive. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The two pictures are mirror images, so clinicians use the lesion site to predict whether disinhibition or apathy will dominate, while noting that mixed pictures are common. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]

Empathy, social judgement and the somatic-marker idea

  • Frontal patients show impaired theory of mind (the ability to infer other people's thoughts, feelings and intentions), being poorer than other patients at inferring others' viewpoints and at detecting deception. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • They lose autonoetic awareness (the conscious sense of oneself as a continuous person over time), so orbital-frontal injury impairs self-knowledge and the self-regulation of behaviour. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Emotional regulation fails, with lability of mood and affect — exhilaration, depression, anxiety and irritability — linked to ventromedial damage and difficulty grasping the emotional consequences of one's behaviour. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The somatic-marker idea (the view that bodily 'gut' feelings normally flag the value of choices) holds that emotional signals normally guide decision-making, so ventromedial damage leaves judgement and social decisions poor despite preserved intellect. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]

Evidence, assessment and significance

  • Phineas Gage remains the classic evidence, and drastic personality change of this kind generally follows large and usually bilateral frontal injuries. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • A modern parallel is the salesman M. L., whose closed-head orbital injury left him unable to relate to his wife, illustrating how the deficit corrodes everyday relationships. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Personality and social conduct are assessed with the Frontal Lobe Personality Scale (premorbid versus current personality), the Hare Psychopathy Checklist and the Faux Pas Test of theory of mind. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The significance is that one of the possible consequences of severe frontal derangement is social inadequacy, so the patient may keep their intellect yet lose the capacity to live and work with others. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
Emotion and personality at a glance
  • Orbitofrontal / ventromedial cortex modulates affect, impulse control and social conduct via amygdala and hypothalamus.
  • Two frontal personalities: orbitofrontal disinhibited (pseudopsychopathic) vs dorsolateral/medial apathetic (pseudodepressed).
  • Impaired empathy, theory of mind, autonoetic awareness and emotional regulation; social gaffes and poor judgement.
  • Phineas Gage is the classic case; the somatic-marker idea explains lost emotional guidance of decisions.
  • Tests: Frontal Lobe Personality Scale, Hare Psychopathy Checklist, Faux Pas Test; outcome often social inadequacy.
Mnemonic
GAGE
Gaffes & disinhibition (orbitofrontal), Apathy & inertia (dorsolateral/medial), Gut-feeling loss (somatic marker / ventromedial), Empathy & theory-of-mind failure — the frontal changes in emotion, personality and social conduct, anchored by the case of Phineas Gage.

5Disturbances of memory

Frontal memory disturbance is not a primary amnesia but a failure of the organisation, retrieval and monitoring of memory: working memory, prospective memory, temporal-order and source memory and metamemory are impaired, and confabulation can appear, yet recognition is relatively spared because storage itself is intact.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: frontal memory as a retrieval / organisation problem, not a storage amnesia.
  • Background: the prefrontal role in working memory and the central executive.
  • Key deficits: working, prospective, temporal-order / source memory, metamemory, confabulation.
  • Evidence and examples: spared recognition, the Tulving encoding/retrieval asymmetry.
  • Assessment and significance: the memory tests and the contrast with hippocampal amnesia.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Classical account Frontal dysfunction has been characterised as a dysexecutive syndrome involving disruption of working memory, specifically a malfunction of the central executive that controls resources and monitors processing. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Standard text Frontal patients show poor temporal memory, especially poor working memory and poor delayed response, the deficits most associated with dorsolateral lesions. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Classical account Tulving and colleagues proposed a hemispheric asymmetry in which the left prefrontal cortex is more engaged in encoding information and the right prefrontal cortex more in its retrieval. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Exam points

Definition: not a primary amnesia

  • Frontal memory disturbance is best defined as a failure to organise, retrieve and monitor memory, rather than the loss of the ability to store new information that marks hippocampal amnesia. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The telling sign is that recall is poor while recognition is relatively spared, so the information has been stored but the patient cannot strategically retrieve it unaided. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Frontal patients therefore exhibit memory deficits on free recall, interference and working-memory tasks, the tasks that make the heaviest demands on self-organised retrieval. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Working memory and prospective memory

  • Working memory (holding and manipulating information in mind for a few seconds) is central to the frontal deficit, and its disruption is the core of Baddeley's dysexecutive account of frontal patients. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Working-memory failure is shown on spatial and feature delayed-response and delayed-alternation tasks, where the patient must hold a position or a figure across a delay. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • Prospective memory (remembering to carry out an intended action in the future) is impaired, so the patient forgets to perform a planned activity at the right moment. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Prospective-memory failure is especially disabling in daily life, because it undermines keeping appointments, taking medication and following through on intentions. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]

Temporal-order, source memory and metamemory

  • Temporal-order memory (remembering the sequence in which events happened) is impaired, so a patient may recall items yet misjudge their recency or order, a deficit tied to dorsolateral damage. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Source memory (remembering where or how a piece of information was learned) is disturbed, so the patient knows a fact but cannot say when or from whom they learned it. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Metamemory (awareness and monitoring of one's own memory) is poor, so the patient misjudges what they do and do not know, as in an unreliable feeling of knowing. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • There is also an impaired ability to retrieve episodic (autobiographical) memories despite intact semantic retrieval, so the patient claims to know the information but cannot consciously recollect the event. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Confabulation and evidence

  • Confabulation (producing false memories without any intent to deceive) can appear, as the patient unwittingly fills memory gaps with plausible but untrue material because retrieval is not monitored or verified. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]
  • Confabulation is most associated with orbitomedial and ventromedial damage, linking it to the same monitoring failure that underlies the social and personality changes. [Donald T. Stuss & D. Frank Benson, The Frontal Lobes]
  • The relative sparing of recognition and of stored knowledge is the key dissociation that separates frontal memory disturbance from the storage amnesia of medial-temporal damage. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • The proposed left-encode / right-retrieve asymmetry remains debated, and it is given as a hypothesis rather than a settled fact, so it is noted with its controversy. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]

Assessment and significance

  • Memory is assessed with the Wechsler Memory Scale and digit span, and with list-learning tests such as the California Verbal Learning Test and the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Everyday and prospective memory is probed by the Rivermead Behavioural Memory Test, which samples remembering an appointment, a name or a message. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The frontal pattern of poor recall but preserved recognition, with poor use of strategy, is what distinguishes it on these tests from an amnesic storage deficit. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • The significance is that the patient's knowledge is intact but inaccessible when needed, so they fail real tasks that depend on self-cued retrieval even though they can recognise the right answer when shown it. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
Memory disturbance at a glance
  • Frontal memory = a retrieval, organisation and monitoring failure, not a storage amnesia.
  • Impaired working, prospective, temporal-order and source memory, and metamemory; episodic retrieval poor, semantic spared.
  • Confabulation (unintended false memories) can appear, most after orbitomedial / ventromedial damage.
  • Key dissociation: poor recall but relatively spared recognition — unlike hippocampal amnesia.
  • Tests: WMS, digit span, California / Rey verbal learning, Rivermead; knowledge intact but inaccessible when needed.
Mnemonic
WPM-SC
Working memory, Prospective memory, Metamemory, Source/temporal-order memory, Confabulation — the frontal memory failures; remember the signature dissociation (poor RECALL, spared RECOGNITION) that proves it is a retrieval problem, not a storage amnesia.

6Disturbances of intellectual activity

Frontal damage impairs the active side of intelligence — abstract reasoning, concept formation, problem-solving and cognitive flexibility — while often leaving measured IQ near normal, producing the paradox of a patient whose stored knowledge is intact yet who cannot apply it to a novel, open-ended problem in real life.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: intellectual activity and the frontal lobe's role in its active, fluid side.
  • Background: the problem-solving loop and the prefrontal cortex.
  • Key deficits: abstraction, concept formation, problem-solving, cognitive flexibility, decision-making.
  • Evidence and examples: Wisconsin Card Sorting, Tower and maze tests, the IQ paradox.
  • Assessment and significance: the reasoning tests and why near-normal IQ misleads.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Classical account Loss of abstract thought was considered an early sign of frontal-lobe damage, so the patient falls back on a concrete attitude and cannot adopt the abstract one. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Standard text Frontal patients are especially impaired at developing novel cognitive plans or strategies for solving problems, and sequencing is perhaps the behaviour most clearly affected. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Standard text Despite scoring normally on conventional tests of general cognitive ability, patients may require constant care because they cannot apply their retained cognitive skills in everyday life. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Exam points

Definition and the active side of intellect

  • Intellectual activity here means the active, fluid use of reasoning to solve new problems, as distinct from the crystallised store of facts and vocabulary that frontal damage largely spares. [Joaquin M. Fuster, The Prefrontal Cortex]
  • The prefrontal cortex supports intelligence, creativity and higher-order reasoning, so its damage strikes the generative side of thought rather than knowledge already laid down. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • This is why the deficit is best framed as a loss of fluid rather than crystallised intelligence, a distinction that explains the near-normal IQ scores. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]

Abstraction and concept formation

  • Abstract reasoning is impaired, so proverbs are interpreted concretely and the patient cannot rise above the literal surface of a problem. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Concept formation (grouping things by an underlying rule or category) is poor, so the patient struggles to sort by an abstract principle and to shift that principle when it changes. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
  • Rule deduction and rule following break down, with the patient failing to identify the rule, to use feedback, or to switch set, which is the core of Wisconsin Card Sorting failure. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Loss of the abstract attitude was treated historically as an early, sensitive marker of frontal involvement, underlining how central abstraction is to frontal intellect. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Problem-solving and flexibility

  • Frontal patients are especially poor at generating novel strategies, so open-ended problems with no ready-made solution expose the deficit most clearly. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Cognitive flexibility (switching approach when the situation or feedback demands it) is reduced, leaving the patient rigid and unable to abandon a failing strategy. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Decision-making is impaired, with studies suggesting that frontal patients have great difficulty making correct decisions, particularly where outcomes are uncertain. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Sequencing and planning of the steps of a solution are disrupted, so even when the patient knows the goal they cannot build the ordered path toward it. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Evidence, assessment and the IQ paradox

  • The Wisconsin Card Sorting Test is the classic demonstration, with frontal patients making more perseverative errors and failing to complete category sorts. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Tower tests (Hanoi, London, Toronto) and maze tests (Porteus) show impaired planning and rule-adherence, as the patient cannot build a sequence of moves to a goal and ignores the rules. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Abstraction itself is assessed with the Category Test and Proverb Test, which probe concept formation and abstract thinking. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The central finding is the IQ paradox: patients can score normally on general-ability tests yet need round-the-clock care because they cannot apply those skills to real life. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The significance is that measured IQ can badly understate the disability, so a frontal patient must be assessed with executive and real-life tasks, not a standard intelligence test alone. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]
Intellectual disturbance at a glance
  • Frontal intellect = the active, fluid use of reasoning on novel problems; crystallised knowledge is largely spared.
  • Impaired abstraction (concrete proverbs), concept formation, rule deduction, decision-making and sequencing.
  • Reduced cognitive flexibility → rigid, cannot drop a failing strategy (Wisconsin Card Sorting).
  • Tests: Wisconsin Card Sorting, Tower of London / Hanoi, Porteus mazes, Category Test, Proverb Test.
  • The IQ paradox: near-normal IQ yet unable to apply skills in daily life — so IQ understates the disability.
Mnemonic
ACPF
Abstraction, Concept formation, Problem-solving, Flexibility — the four pillars of frontal intellect that break down; remember the IQ PARADOX (normal score, real-life failure) and the Wisconsin Card Sort as the signature test.

7Disturbances of language functions

Frontal damage disturbs the output and regulatory sides of language rather than comprehension, giving non-fluent Broca-type speech, reduced verbal fluency, transcortical motor and dynamic aphasia, agrammatism and impaired discourse, and, at its deepest, the loss of the regulatory power of language over behaviour.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: the frontal language profile as a disturbance of output, not comprehension.
  • Anatomy: Broca's area, the supplementary motor area and the left frontal cortex.
  • Key deficits: Broca's aphasia, transcortical motor and dynamic aphasia, reduced fluency, discourse.
  • Evidence and examples: agrammatism, sequence comprehension, the regulatory power of language.
  • Assessment and significance: the language tests and the link to executive control.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Broca's aphasia, linked to lesions in Broca's area, is non-fluent — sparse, effortful, dysarthric, dysprosodic and agrammatic — with poor repetition but relatively good comprehension. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Standard text Transcortical motor aphasia, linked to left medial frontal (supplementary motor) lesions, shows an inability to initiate spontaneous speech, yet repetition and comprehension remain comparatively intact. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Classical account Luria described a loss of the regulatory power of language, so frontal patients may verbalise a task correctly yet cannot use that verbalisation to direct their behaviour. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]

Exam points

Definition and anatomy

  • The frontal language profile is a disturbance of speech output and the control of language, not of the comprehension that depends on the posterior temporal cortex. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Broca's area in the inferior frontal gyrus (around area 44) controls the motor production of voluntary speech and written expression. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The supplementary motor area and left medial frontal cortex supply the drive to initiate speech, which is why their damage silences spontaneous output while sparing repetition. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

The frontal aphasias

  • Broca's aphasia (non-fluent, effortful, agrammatic speech with spared comprehension) gives halting, telegraphic output with difficulty in word-finding, reading and writing but relatively preserved understanding. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Transcortical motor aphasia (loss of spontaneous speech with repetition and comprehension intact) leaves the patient unable to start speaking of their own accord even though they can still repeat what they hear. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Dynamic aphasia (fluent words but an inability to build connected, propositional discourse), described by Luria, spares single words yet blocks the construction of a connected narrative or proposition. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]
  • Aphemia (an apraxia of speech with intact comprehension and writing) is an isolated breakdown of articulatory coordination, a complete articulatory failure with comprehension and writing preserved. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Fluency, grammar and discourse

  • Reduced verbal fluency is characteristic, so on letter (FAS) and category fluency tasks the patient names far fewer words than controls or patients with non-frontal lesions. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Agrammatism (difficulty using verbs and correct grammar) appears, with impoverished use of verbs and grammatical structure giving a telegraphic quality to speech. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Because frontal patients cannot maintain a verbal sequence, their comprehension of long word-strings also suffers: they understand single words but grasp only part of a sequence, often the first or last items. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • At the discourse level, pragmatics and connected narrative are impoverished, so the patient's talk is thin, poorly organised and hard to sustain. [Muriel D. Lezak et al., Neuropsychological Assessment]

The regulatory power of language, assessment and significance

  • The deepest frontal language sign is Luria's loss of the regulatory power of language: the patient can state a task aloud, yet the words no longer steer action, mirroring the knowing–doing dissociation. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]
  • This loss ties language directly to executive control, because speech normally guides and plans behaviour, and frontal damage severs that guiding link. [Joaquin M. Fuster, The Prefrontal Cortex]
  • Language is assessed with the Boston Naming Test (word retrieval), the Token Test (verbal comprehension of sequences) and the Controlled Oral Word Association test of verbal fluency. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Comprehensive aphasia batteries such as the Western Aphasia Battery and the Boston Diagnostic Aphasia Battery are used to classify the type and severity of the aphasia. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The significance is that frontal language disturbance is primarily a failure of production and self-regulation, so the patient may understand yet cannot generate, organise or use speech to control what they do. [Alexander R. Luria, Higher Cortical Functions in Man]
Language disturbance at a glance
  • Frontal language = a disturbance of output and control, with comprehension largely spared.
  • Broca's aphasia (non-fluent, agrammatic), transcortical motor aphasia (no spontaneous speech, repetition intact), Luria's dynamic aphasia.
  • Reduced verbal fluency (FAS / category), agrammatism, and impaired comprehension of long word-sequences.
  • Deepest sign: loss of the regulatory power of language — words no longer steer behaviour.
  • Tests: Boston Naming, Token Test, Controlled Oral Word Association, Western / Boston aphasia batteries.
Mnemonic
BTD-FR
Broca's (non-fluent), Transcortical motor (no spontaneous speech), Dynamic (no connected discourse), reduced Fluency, and loss of the Regulatory power of language — the frontal language disturbances, all sparing comprehension while wrecking output and control.

8Disturbances of motor functions

The frontal lobe organises movement along a rostral-to-caudal hierarchy — prefrontal planning, premotor and supplementary-motor programming and selection, primary motor execution — so its damage gives contralateral weakness, loss of fine movement, apraxia, poor movement programming, akinesia, gait disturbance and the re-emergence of primitive release reflexes.

Answer flow — how to write this topic

Write the answer in this order, and the points below follow the same skeleton:

  • Definition: the frontal lobe's role in the organisation and execution of movement.
  • Anatomy: the primary motor, premotor / supplementary-motor and prefrontal levels.
  • Key deficits: weakness, fine-movement loss, apraxia, poor programming, akinesia, perseveration.
  • Evidence and examples: corollary discharge, the grasp reflex, gait and release signs.
  • Assessment and significance: the motor tests and the link to the wider syndrome.
  • One-line summary (use the mnemonic).
Characterisations by standard texts
  • Standard text Damage to the primary motor cortex (M1, area 4) causes contralateral motor deficits — initially a flaccid and later a spastic hemiplegia or hemiparesis. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • Standard text The motor cortex executes individual movements while the premotor cortex selects the movements to be executed, so the two contribute to movement in different ways. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Standard text Frontal patients may show release signs — reflexes such as the snout, suck, glabellar and grasp reflexes that are present in infancy but normally disappear with development. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]

Exam points

Definition and anatomy

  • The frontal lobe organises movement along a rostral-to-caudal hierarchy, from prefrontal intention through premotor programming to primary-motor execution. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The primary motor cortex on the precentral gyrus projects via the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts to drive voluntary limb, hand, digit and facial movement. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The premotor and supplementary motor areas (area 6) integrate sensory and motor information to select and programme actions, projecting onward to the primary motor cortex. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The frontal eye fields (area 8) control voluntary eye movements for visual search, so their damage impairs active scanning while sparing the passive following of a moving object. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]

Weakness, fine movement and apraxia

  • Damage to the primary motor cortex causes contralateral weakness, a flaccid hemiparesis at first that later becomes spastic, with the distribution depending on how much cortex is involved. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • There is a chronic loss of fine, independent movements of the hands, fingers and face, together with a reduction in fine motor speed and strength. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Apraxia (loss of skilled, learned movement that is not due to weakness) can follow premotor damage, so the patient cannot carry out skilled actions they once performed even though they are not paralysed. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • Buccofacial apraxia is seen as difficulty copying simple facial gestures, and frontal patients are specifically poor at copying sequences of facial movements. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Movement programming, corollary discharge and perseveration

  • Movement programming is impaired, so the patient has difficulty planning, preparing and selecting movements and serially ordering a complex chain of actions. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Luria described a disintegration of learned sequential actions into a 'series of isolated fragments', so smooth skilled sequences fall apart into disconnected pieces. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Corollary discharge (the brain's internal copy of a movement command, telling the rest of the brain a movement is coming) is impaired, so a frontal lesion both disturbs movement and interferes with the signal that a movement is taking place. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Motor perseveration appears, with compulsive repetition of an initiated action in movement, speech, drawing or writing, especially when the lesion extends to the basal ganglia. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]

Akinesia, release signs and gait

  • Akinesia (poverty or absence of spontaneous, self-initiated movement) and reduced spontaneous facial expression occur, reflecting the prefrontal and medial contribution to the initiation of movement. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
  • Frontal release signs re-emerge, including the grasp, snout, suck, palmomental and glabellar reflexes, as the loss of frontal inhibition frees these infantile reflexes. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • The grasp reflex is a telling example, an involuntary grasp of an object placed in the hand that persists despite the patient's conscious attempts to let go. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Dystonic signs such as gegenhalten — a paratonic resistance to passive movement — appear, distinct from the cogwheel rigidity of Parkinson's disease. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • Gait is disturbed, classically a magnetic or apraxic gait in which the patient has difficulty lifting the feet from the ground when walking. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]

Assessment and significance

  • Motor strength and speed are assessed with hand dynamometry (grip strength) and finger tapping, and motor sequencing with the Facial Sequence Test and Luria's alternating-movement tasks. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • Visuomotor integration and apraxia are probed with the Bender Visual Motor Gestalt Test and simple gesture or object-use tasks, and voluntary gaze with a finger-gaze test. [Frontal Lobe Syndromes: Clinical Features and Assessment (seminar materials)]
  • The significance is that these motor signs are objective, examinable markers of frontal dysfunction, anchoring an otherwise amorphous syndrome in observable neurological findings. [Regions of the Frontal Lobes (unit note)]
  • They also show the continuity of the frontal lobe's work, from executing a single movement to programming sequences and initiating action, so motor and executive deficits are two ends of one organising function. [Bryan Kolb & Ian Q. Whishaw, Fundamentals of Human Neuropsychology]
Motor disturbance at a glance
  • Motor hierarchy: prefrontal (plan) → premotor / SMA (select & programme) → primary motor (execute).
  • Primary-motor damage → contralateral weakness and loss of fine movement; premotor damage → apraxia and poor programming.
  • Impaired corollary discharge and serial ordering; motor perseveration; akinesia and reduced facial expression.
  • Frontal release signs (grasp, snout, suck, glabellar), gegenhalten, and a magnetic / apraxic gait.
  • Tests: grip dynamometry, finger tapping, facial-sequence and alternating-movement tasks, reflex testing.
Mnemonic
WAP-GR
Weakness & fine-movement loss, Apraxia, Programming failure (+ perseveration), Gait disturbance & akinesia, Release signs (grasp, snout, suck) — the frontal motor disturbances along the plan→programme→execute hierarchy.
